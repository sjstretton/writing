# Peer review of 3.1, working paper v0.2 and policy brief v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 3.1, *Guarantee the Contract, Not the Debt: A Payment Intermediary for Clean Power*, written from a full reading before the v0.3 working paper and v1.0 brief. Every number in the calibration was recomputed from `2.Source/model.py`, and every derivation in Appendix A was checked by hand. The references are checked separately in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a sharp question (how can a developing country remove the payment risk that sets the cost of capital for clean power, at the lowest contingent cost to the state?) and gives a clear answer: a payment intermediary facility that buys under one standard power purchase agreement, pays generators whatever the distribution company does, and is backed by a guarantee waterfall contracted in advance. The offtaker model is tractable and its algebra is right. The India and Nigeria sections are carefully sourced and honest about failure. It is close to the paper standard.

It has one real error, and it sits in the headline. The "five dollars saved per dollar of the guarantor's expected cost" (abstract, Table 3, brief, deck) counts the facility's cost after it has been repaid by the waterfall, treating the subnational owner's and national government's payments as recovery rather than as cost, while Proposition 3 counts the same payments as calls on those layers. On a consistent basis the contract guarantee saves about \$2.0 per dollar of expected cost to the public layers, not \$5.3. The ranking against the debt guarantee survives; the size of the advantage, and the claim that it is mostly a real resource gain, do not. Beyond that: the debt-guarantee comparison charges the debt guarantee for risks whose removal it never credits; two sources do not say what they are cited for; the strongest objection in the literature (the World Bank's own critique of single-buyer models) is not engaged; the closest policy precedents (the World Bank's IDA payment guarantees under Scaling Solar, the African Trade Insurance Agency's liquidity facility, the IEA's 2024 recommendation to use SECI-type intermediaries) are missing; and there is no non-technical executive summary. The brief, at about 3,300 words with a sectioned executive summary and no bold lead sentences, is a second working paper rather than a brief.

## Major points

**M1. The contract guarantee's expected cost is understated by a factor of about 2.7, and the error drives the headline.** Proposition 2 and Table 3 set the facility's recovery at $R_G = 95$ percent "because it can call on the waterfall", giving an expected cost of \$0.18 million a year on the 100 MW plant and a saving of \$5.3 per dollar. But the waterfall's payments are not recovery from the offtaker: they are the calls on the subnational owner, the national government, the multilateral agency and the donor account that Proposition 3 and Table 4 count as costs. In Table 4 the facility recovers only $\tilde R = 58$ percent (in present value) from the offtakers and the layers pay 5.5 percent of contract payments a year. Applied to the 100 MW plant (contract payments \$8.69 million), the layers' expected cost is \$0.48 million a year, not \$0.18 million, and the saving per dollar is $0.94/0.48 = 2.0$. The two parts of the paper must use one recovery assumption. *Fix:* define the guarantee's cost as the expected arrears not recovered from the offtaker itself, borne by all layers together, $c_C = \varphi(\lambda)(1 - \tilde R_F)$, where $R_F$ is what the facility recovers from the offtaker. The central case sets $R_F = R = 60$ percent (the facility collects no better than generators would, consistent with Table 4); a sensitivity sets $R_F = 80$ percent for a repeat creditor with letters of credit and a late-payment surcharge, which gives \$3.6 per dollar.

**M2. With the cost counted correctly, the "real resource gain" from better recovery is an assumption, not a result.** Proposition 2(iii) splits the efficiency ratio into a price-of-risk factor, $\varphi(m\lambda)/\varphi(\lambda) = 1.77$, and a recovery factor, $(1-\tilde R)/(1-\tilde R_F)$. The v0.2 calibration reported the second as 2.7 and called it "a real resource gain: the facility recovers more than a dispersed set of generators would". That 2.7 came entirely from counting waterfall payments as recovery. With $R_F = R$ the recovery factor is 1, and with no price of risk ($m = 1$) the contract guarantee saves the offtaker \$0.51 million at an expected public cost of \$0.48 million: a near-pure transfer of expected loss from investors to the state. The sentence "Even with no price of risk, the contract guarantee would remove nearly three times its expected cost" is wrong and must go. The paper's honest claim is narrower and still strong: (i) the contract guarantee removes the whole premium where the debt guarantee removes under a third; (ii) per dollar of expected cost it beats the debt guarantee whenever its recovery is at least as good; (iii) whether it creates value for society, rather than moving risk to the state, depends on three things the facility must earn: better collection ($R_F > R$), fewer default episodes ($\lambda$ falls because the offtaker faces one repeat creditor and reform conditions), and a state that bears the risk more cheaply than investors, which fails when offtaker default and fiscal stress are correlated. State this as a remark after Proposition 2 and in the executive summary.

**M3. The debt-guarantee comparison is lopsided.** The debt guarantor's expected cost includes \$0.18 million a year for construction and operating risks (1 percent a year of a 30 percent loss on \$60 million), but the model never credits the tariff reduction that removing those risks from lenders would bring, because $r_0$ is held fixed. The "0.8 per dollar" therefore compares a full cost with a partial benefit. *Fix:* compare the two guarantees on the offtaker risk alone (contract \$2.0 per dollar, debt \$1.4 per dollar), report the debt guarantee's cover of other risks as a separate line, and say that whether that cover is worth buying is a question about construction finance, which Paper 3.2 addresses. The ranking survives because $\tilde R_F = 0.58 > \tilde R_D = 0.39$ and because coverage (0.54 against 0.17 cents) and maximum exposure (\$8.7 million against \$60 million) favor the contract guarantee by a wide margin.

**M4. The strongest objection is missing: single buyers have a bad record.** A World Bank Viewpoint (Lovei 2000) argued that the single-buyer model in developing countries invites corruption, weakens payment discipline and imposes contingent liabilities on the government; a later World Bank review of centralized purchasing (Arizu, Gencer and Maurer 2006) found that a pure single buyer is sometimes the only workable option in distressed systems, but that a legal monopoly is neither necessary nor desirable. Nigeria's NBET is the paper's own example of the failure. The paper must state the objection in this form and answer it: the facility is not a monopoly buyer of all power; it buys only contracted renewable output under one standard agreement; it leaves the distribution company's obligation alive; procurement (by open auction) is separated from payment; and entry is conditional on reform. That is the difference between SECI and NBET.

**M5. The contribution must be stated against the closest policy precedents, which the paper omits.** Three are close. (a) The IEA's 2024 report for the G20 recommends "using third-party creditworthy institutions such as Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI) to manage off-taker risk" and identifies payment risk as one of the top three sector risks. (b) Under the World Bank Group's Scaling Solar program in Zambia, an IDA payment guarantee of \$3.5 million backed the utility's letter of credit for the 47.5 MW West Lunga plant, which bid 6.015 US cents per kWh; that is a contract guarantee in this paper's sense, capped at a few months of payments. (c) The African Trade Insurance Agency's Regional Liquidity Support Facility (2017, €63.2 million from KfW and ATI) gives independent power producers up to six months of liquidity cover against state utility payment delays. The paper's contribution relative to these is the model: the conditions under which the contract form dominates, the decomposition of its gain, the expected calls on each layer and the buffer for correlated arrears. Say so in the introduction and the literature section.

**M6. A non-technical executive summary is missing.** Add one as chapter 1 (about 600 words, bold lead sentences, no equations, the paper's real numbers), and link it to the brief.

## Checks of each result

- *Equation (1), LCOE.* Recomputed: 3.61, 3.92, 4.26, 4.96, 5.72, 6.51 and 7.75 cents at 4–15 percent. Correct. The "return on capital" share (capital charge above straight-line depreciation) is a defensible definition but is not the same as the IEA's "financing costs"; say "comparable to" rather than "consistent with".
- *Proposition 1.* The renewal-reward argument and the averaging that gives $\tilde R = R(1-e^{-r_0D})/(r_0D)$ are correct. The central case reproduces: $\varphi(m\lambda) = 0.231$, $\tilde R = 0.577$, $h = 0.098$, tariff 4.96 to 5.50 cents (+10.8 percent), WACC-equivalent 9.4 percent. The stationary-regime assumption ignores that a new contract starts in normal state; with $\lambda D = 0.15$ this matters little. State it.
- *Table 2 (premium grid).* All twelve cells reproduce. "Episodes of six months halve the premium" (5.7 against 10.8 percent) and "two years nearly double it" (20.1 percent): correct.
- *Proposition 2.* Algebra correct given its definitions; the calibration is not (M1–M3). Part (iii)'s second condition, "removes more value than it costs whenever $m \ge 1$ and $\tilde R_G \ge \tilde R$", holds with equality at $m = 1$, $R_F = R$: say so, since it is the case in M2.
- *Proposition 3.* (i) correct; the layer calls (4.80, 3.84, 0.86, 0.10 million dollars) reproduce, as does "the donor's cost would rise two and a half times if the national layer paid half the time". The assumption that layers pay independently of each other and of the offtaker's default is strong: a subnational government whose utility defaults is itself likely to be under stress. Positive correlation shifts expected calls down the waterfall; say so. (ii) The buffer bound uses the one-factor quantile of the share of offtakers *entering* an episode in a year as the share *in arrears* at once; with one-year episodes this is a reasonable approximation, and the paper should call it one. The text says "against an average of 14 percent": that is $p = 1 - e^{-0.15} = 13.9$ percent, the annual entry probability; the long-run share in arrears is $\varphi(\lambda) = 13.0$ percent. Make clear which. The timing in (ii) (a layer pays liquidity after $H$ years) differs from (i) (layers pay the unrecovered shortfall after the episode): explain that (ii) is about bridging and (i) about final cost.
- *Buffer figures.* 65 percent stressed share at $\rho = 0.3$, \$57 million buffer (33 percent of payments, \$28,000 per MW), 39 and 85 percent at $\rho = 0.1$ and 0.5: all reproduce.
- *India in the model.* "The margin pays for itself if the intermediary lowers the WACC by 0.35 points" uses the LCOE elasticity at 10 percent while everything else is at 8 percent. At 8 percent it is 0.32 points. Use 0.32 ("about a third of a point", as the brief says). The Rs 3,300 crore against Rs 740 crore comparison reproduces (Rs 3,335 and Rs 736 crore).
- *Declining tariff.* 7.74 falling to 3.87 cents has the same present value at 8 percent as a flat 5 cents over 20 years; remaining value at year 7 is 30.6 against 39.5 (23 percent lower); above new-build cost 10.8 against 19.8 (45 percent lower); the older 6-to-3 path is worth 22 percent less. All correct. The brief's "halves the part above the cost of new solar" overstates 45 percent slightly: say "nearly halves".
- *Script.* `model.py` prints the plant's output as "175 MWh/yr"; it is 175,200 MWh (175 GWh). Cosmetic; fix.

## Sources (detail in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`)

1. The IEA's 2022 news release could not be read from the IEA site; its two-to-three-times figure for 2021 is reported only in secondary sources. The IEA's 2024 report, which was read, says the cost of capital for utility-scale solar in emerging and developing economies is "well over twice" that in advanced economies and that financing can be "around half or more" of the levelized cost. Use the 2024 report and drop the news release.
2. The Power Line article of 9 July 2019 does not give the order's date or its 1 August 2019 effective date; the magazine's 1 July 2019 report does. Replace.
3. The Power Line article of June 2022 does not mention that the late-payment surcharge stops on timely installments. A law-firm summary of the rules does (notified 3 June 2022; no surcharge on the rescheduled dues while installments are paid; surcharge on the whole outstanding on default). Add it.
4. Trimble et al. (2016) say two countries had "a financially viable electricity sector" under existing performance and that only 19 of 39 covered operating costs; use their words and add the second number.
5. Driessen (2005) finds significant premia on common default-intensity factors and a positive but imprecisely estimated premium on the default event itself; it does not give a ratio of risk-neutral to actual intensity. Re-word, and keep $m = 2$ as stylized.
6. Schmidt (2014) argues that de-risking can raise the cost-effectiveness of policy; that it is "cheaper for the public than compensating investors" could not be confirmed from accessible text. Re-word.
7. The Vasicek (2002) entry's title differs from the publisher's: Risk.net lists the December 2002 article as "Loan Portfolio Value" (often cited as "The Distribution of Loan Portfolio Value"). Use the publisher's title, as the series' other papers do.

## Minor points

1. The first italic line cites @CFPB31 in plain text; make it a link to the brief, `[Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 3.1](brief:)`.
2. The introduction's roadmap sentence is boilerplate; cut it.
3. "In form it is a payment intermediary" (NBET): fine, but say what NBET lacked in the terms of Proposition 3 (no funded layer, no intercept, collection near a quarter of invoices).
4. Section ids: every section and subsection has one; keep them stable, since the brief links into them.
5. JEL and keywords are fine. Abstract (about 190 words) is within the standard but must change with M1.
6. The brief's YAML says version 0.1 while the README says 1.0; the new brief is v1.0.
7. The facility's maximum exposure "a year or two of unpaid bills" (brief) should be "a year of unpaid bills per episode" (the model's episode length).
8. Use "distribution company" consistently after defining "offtaker"; the brief mixes "buyer", "distributor", "utility" and "distribution company". The series term is *offtaker*; in plain prose "distribution company" is acceptable as the same thing, but not four words for it.

## What the brief and the deck need

*Brief.* Rebuild to the 1.1 pattern: about 2,000 words; `# Key messages` (four or five bold one- or two-sentence paragraphs); claim headings; every paragraph's first sentence in bold; no model, no formulas; Table 1 (cost by WACC) and Table 2 (contract against debt guarantee) with the corrected numbers; links into the working paper's sections (`wp:sec-...`); "What to do"; "Related papers in this series" with `paper:` links; references. Drop the sectioned executive summary (the working paper now has its own). Keep India and Nigeria short and link to the working paper's precedents section.

*Deck.* The current deck has three content slides in an old format. Rebuild at about ten slides in the 1.1 format: title; the cost of capital in numbers; the buyer is the risk (premium grid); the intermediary and its waterfall; contract against debt guarantee (corrected); where the cost falls (waterfall table); liquidity, not loss, breaks it; India: works but the buyers were not fixed; design rules; what to do. Every title a full-sentence message, sources on each slide, numbers identical to the papers.

## Disposition

All major points (M1–M6), the checks and the minor points are addressed in working paper v0.3 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026): the guarantee comparison is recomputed on a consistent basis (contract guarantee \$2.0 of tariff saving per dollar of expected cost to the public layers, \$3.6 if the facility collects 80 percent; debt guarantee \$1.4 on the same offtaker risk, \$0.75 including other risks it covers), the "real resource gain" claim is replaced by a stated condition, the single-buyer objection and the Scaling Solar, ATI and IEA precedents are added, the sources are corrected, and the deck is rebuilt at ten slides. The headline changes from "five dollars per dollar" to "about two dollars per dollar, and the whole premium rather than under a third of it".
