# Peer review of 3.2, working paper v0.1 and policy brief v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of *From Projects to Asset Classes: Two-Stage Finance and Synthetic Sovereign Bonds for Clean Power*, written from a full reading before the v0.2 working paper and v1.0 brief. Every number in both forms was recomputed with `2.Source/model.py` (after installing SciPy, which the script needs). Sources were checked separately; see `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a clear question (once payment risk is removed, how do single clean power projects become standard securities that institutional and domestic savers will buy cheaply?) and gives a clear answer (split at commissioning, pool operating plants under one contract, tranche the pool; use tiered bond guarantees where there is no contract). The three propositions are correct and their proofs check. The weaknesses are in the bridge from the credit model to the price of power, where one calculation is inconsistent with the paper's own calibration and overstates the headline saving; in the absence of the strongest counter-evidence, the much thicker subordination that real infrastructure securitizations carry; and in a dozen sentences that their sources do not support. The brief is accurate but long (about 3,200 words), has no bold lead sentences and no key messages, and repeats the working paper's tables rather than arguing the policy. There is no executive summary in the working paper and no deck worth the name (three slides).

## Major points

**M1. The headline saving applies the pool's cost of debt to the whole plant, which contradicts the calibration.** Loss given default (20 percent) is calibrated to Moody's recoveries on project finance *bank loans* (79.5 percent on average, 81.2 percent for operation-phase defaults). That calibration is consistent only if the pool holds each plant's senior debt, with the sponsor's equity below it. But Table 4 then refinances the *whole* capitalized cost of the plant at the pool's blended 5.65 percent, as if there were no project equity. The two cannot both hold. If the pool held plants outright, its loss on a default would be a loss of asset value, not a loan loss: with 80 percent gearing and 79.5 percent loan recovery, asset value at default is at most about 64 percent of cost, so the loss given default is about 36–40 percent, and the "recovery 60 percent" column of Table 2 is the right base case (subordination 9.6 percent at ρ = 0.10 and 15.4 percent at ρ = 0.20). If, as the background material and the *Time and Chance* sizing (80 percent gearing) intend, the pool refinances senior debt, then the plant's operating cost of capital is 80 percent at the pool's cost and 20 percent at the project equity return. With stylized project equity at 12 percent, the operating rate is 6.92 percent, not 5.65, and the LCOE falls from 6.22 to 5.04 cents, **19 percent, not 27 percent**; the project-bond case falls by 7 percent, not 12. *Fix:* state that the pool refinances senior project debt (80 percent of capitalized cost), keep λ = 20 percent, compute the operating rate with the project equity layer, and change "about a quarter" to "about a fifth" throughout (abstract, introduction, Table 4, conclusion, brief, deck). Report the sensitivity to the equity return (16–21 percent for 14–10 percent equity). The 3.0 overview also says "about a quarter" and must change.

**M2. The strongest counter-evidence is missing: real securitizations of project loans carry far more subordination than the model says.** In Clifford Capital's first Bayfront transaction (2018), cited by the paper as its precedent, the most senior class was \$320.6 million of \$458 million, about 70 percent; 30 percent of the notes sat beneath it. The model's large-pool figure for a near-AAA senior is 4.8–7.7 percent. The gap has identifiable causes: a pool of 37 loans is not a large pool; a quarter of the Bayfront projects were still under construction; the pool mixed sectors and countries of very different credit quality; and agencies add stresses for concentration and model error. The paper must say this, quantify what it can, and show the result survives. *Fix:* (a) add the finite-pool result (a pool of 40 equal loans needs 6.0 percent at ρ = 0.10 and 8.5 percent at ρ = 0.20, against 4.8 and 7.7 in the large-pool limit); (b) add an "agency-style" structure with 30 percent below the senior bond (10 percent first loss, 20 percent mezzanine), which still cuts the LCOE by 14 percent; (c) present 5–8 percent as the model's lower bound for a large, homogeneous, operating-only pool, not as what an agency would grant on day one.

**M3. The closest prior work is not named and the contribution is not stated against it.** Arezki, Bolton, Peters, Samama and Stiglitz (2017, *Economic Policy*) argue for turning infrastructure into an asset class through standardization and co-investment platforms; Ehlers (2014, BIS) identifies the lack of investable, standard projects and the under-use of capital markets as the binding constraints; Sorge (2004, BIS) shows that project finance credit risk is high at inception and falls over a project's life, which is the empirical basis of two-stage finance; Lowder and Mendelsohn (2013, NREL) estimate the scope for securitizing US solar. None is cited. The contribution is then: an explicit sizing of the loss-absorbing layer for clean power pools as a function of correlation, recovery and pool size; the source of correlation (the host sovereign) and how cross-country pooling under one contract removes it; and the translation into the price of power. *Fix:* add the four, verified, to the literature section and restate the contribution against them.

**M4. The calibration of the default rate needs its anchor stated.** The base ten-year default probability of operating plants (5 percent) is called "below the 6.7 percent average" but the paper does not use the information that pins it down. Moody's cumulative rates (Exhibit 11) are 4.01 percent by year 3 and 6.74 percent by year 10, so a loan that survives its first three years defaulted at about 2.8 percent over the next seven. A ten-year rate of 5 percent for operating plants is therefore conservative for the average market; the stress case (10.2 percent, lower-middle-income economies) covers poorer hosts. Also anchor the rating labels: Moody's 10-year cumulative default rate for Baa corporates was 3.78 percent (1920–2015), so a 1 percent senior target sits well inside investment grade.

**M5. The paper needs a non-technical executive summary, and the brief must become a brief.** Add a chapter-1 executive summary (500–700 words, bold leads, real numbers). Rewrite the brief to about 2,000 words with key messages, bold lead sentences, claim headings, one table and one figure, links into the working paper's sections and no repetition of its tables of subordination by correlation.

## Minor points

1. **Life of the plant.** The model has 25 years of operation after a 2-year build; the text says operation is "about 23 of a plant's 25 years" (introduction), "twenty-three years" (Proposition 1 discussion) and "about twenty-three years" (brief). Use 25 years of operation after 2 of construction everywhere.
2. **Proposition 1(b) range.** The ratio is 10.6, 9.6 and 8.6 at operating rates of 6, 8 and 10 percent; "between about 9 and 11" should be "between about 8.6 and 10.6". Define $r_o$ as the operating-phase cost of capital, which (after M1) blends the refinanced debt and the project equity.
3. **Proposition 2's condition** requires $p < 1/2$ for the monotonicity in ρ (used in the proof); state it.
4. **Table 5, N = 1.** The Monte Carlo value (9.2 percent) differs from the exact one-factor value at ρ = 0.25 (9.1 percent) by simulation noise. Use 9.1. The policy section says a regional pool "needs about half the subordination of a single-country pool": 5.3/9.1 is 58 percent, so "about 40 percent less" (half is the limit, 4.8).
5. **"A single percentage point off the cost of capital is worth more than a decade of further falls in panel prices"** is unsourced. Replace with the model's own equivalence: at 10 percent, one point off the cost of capital cuts the capital charge by 7.6 percent, the same as cutting the plant's cost by 7.6 percent.
6. **Rates basis.** The series reads cost of capital as real unless stated; the paper says "all rates are in dollars". State that rates are stylized US dollar rates, read as real like the rest of the series, and that every comparison is on one basis.
7. **Vietnam and South Africa** were cited to the author's book manuscript, and both are overstated. Vietnam added 16.5 GW of solar in 2019–2020 and 4.1 GW of wind, 95 percent of the solar in the south, and curtailed about 364 GWh of solar in 2020; "curtailed much of it" is not supported. South Africa's sixth bid window sought 3,200 MW of wind and 1,000 MW of solar and awarded 860 MW of solar to five projects and no wind, because Eskom had no grid capacity in the Eastern and Western Cape; the old text said the grid "in the sunniest provinces was full", which is wrong (the constraint was in the windy Cape provinces). Cite public sources.
8. **Tiered guarantee.** "A state government and its national government fail together more often than not" holds only at the highest correlation in Table 6 (at ρ = 0.8, 74 percent of national failures coincide with state failure). Say "often fail together".
9. **IFC's MCPP** began with \$3 billion from the People's Bank of China (IFC's 2016 release), not "China's State Administration of Foreign Exchange" as written (SAFE sits under the central bank; use the source's wording).
10. **GuarantCo.** The source says guarantees are typically in local currency and that PIDG is funded by six governments; "most of its portfolio is in local currency" and "ratings rest on its government owners" are not in it. Re-word.
11. **Steffen (2020)** does not report "3 to more than 10 percent" or attribute the gap to "policy design rather than technology"; it finds, across 46 countries in 2009–2017, that developing countries have significantly higher costs of capital, with a consistent technology ranking. **Egli et al. (2018)** is about Germany. Re-word both.
12. **Moody's sample** is 6,389 projects, and the share of defaults with full recovery is "over 60 percent" (the summary also says "almost two-thirds"). Fine as written; add the project count.
13. **Abstract and introduction disagree** on subordination ("5 to 10 percent" against "5 to 8 percent"). Use one statement.
14. **The scale estimate** (\$2.0–2.5 trillion of solar investment over a decade) is the author's own estimate in a manuscript; say so.
15. **Roadmap paragraph** in the introduction: cut to one sentence or remove; give every section and subsection an identifier for the brief's links; number tables and figures through Quarto identifiers rather than typed "Table 1." captions.
16. **The "Lineage"** footnote should also credit the co-authors named in the series plan only where their material is used here (Jasper Zimmermann). It does.
17. **Series 4 multiplier.** The paper does not quote the series 4 figure (about 80 dollars of incentive per donor dollar on the MDB sovereign loss record, 2.7 in the stress case) and should not: the mezzanine co-guarantee here is a different ratio (expected loss of 0.13 percent of the guaranteed amount a year). Keep them apart, as 3.1 does.

## What the brief needs

About 2,000 words; key messages first; a heading that states a claim for each section; the first sentence of every paragraph in bold; one table (the four financing cases with the corrected LCOEs) and one figure (Figure 2, regenerated); "What to do"; related papers with links; and links into the working paper for the propositions, the subordination tables and the robustness checks. Drop the subordination-by-correlation table (it is evidence, and belongs in the working paper); keep its headline numbers in prose. Make the cross-references links (`wp:`, `paper:`).

## What the deck needs

Three slides is too few. About ten: title and message; the problem (financing share of the price of solar); why bespoke finance is expensive (construction versus operating risk); two-stage finance; pool and tranche (structure and subordination); correlation and cross-country pooling; what it saves (corrected Table 4 and figure); the counter-evidence and robustness (finite pools, agency-style structure); grids and local currency; what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.2 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). M1 changed the headline saving from about a quarter (27 percent) to about a fifth (19 percent); the 3.0 overview, which quotes "about a quarter" and "5 to 10 percent", needs the same change (coordinator). M2 is answered with the finite-pool and agency-style results and a stated lower-bound reading of the model; a calibration to loan-level GEMs data remains the next step. Point 17 needed no change.
