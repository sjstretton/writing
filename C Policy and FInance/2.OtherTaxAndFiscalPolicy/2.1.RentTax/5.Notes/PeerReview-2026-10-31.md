# Peer review of 2.1, *Taxing Rent, Not Effort*, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of the April 2026 draft (`RentTax.qmd`, identical in text to `RentTax.docx` and to the older `2.Source/RentTax.tex`), written from a full reading before any change to the masters. The paper has no policy brief and no deck yet. Every number in the draft was recomputed (script in the session scratch folder; the revised calculations are in `2.Source/rent_tax_calcs.py`).

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a good question and gives a clear, original answer. *Question:* can a country that allows free movement of capital and people still tax the income of the very rich at high rates? *Answer:* yes, if it taxes rent, the part of income that would be earned anyway, through bases that cannot leave: land above a household allowance, and the market power of large firms, reached by a high-rate tax on profit above a normal return plus destination-based layers that follow the customer. The idea that market power should be the default presumption for large firms, and the "presence credit" structure of the sales layer, are the distinctive contributions; the second is close to the "Dyson tax" in *Plucked!* (Stretton and Styles 2020) and to the substance-based carve-out of the OECD's Pillar Two, and should be placed against both.

As a paper, the draft is a sketch. It has no citations at all (only a "further reading" appendix), no author line, no executive summary, no statement of its contribution against prior work, and no objection answered. More seriously, three of its quantitative claims are wrong or unsupported as stated: the destination layer, read literally, is a 20–25 percent tax on gross revenue that can exceed a firm's whole profit; the sales layer can tax low-margin firms on profit they do not make; and the 40–60 percent effective rate and the 5.5–11.5 percent of GDP revenue figure are asserted rather than computed. Each can be fixed without changing the architecture. With the fixes below and the literature added, the paper can meet the standard.

## Major points

**M1. The destination layer (Layer Two) is mis-specified: as written it taxes gross revenue at 20–25 percent.** The main text says "20–25% on in-country revenue"; the appendix analyses "a destination-basis tax at rate $\tau_d$ on sales, with full deductibility of purchases", which is a destination-based cash-flow tax. The two readings differ by an order of magnitude. Read literally, a 22 percent tax on revenue takes 63 percent of profit from a platform with a 35 percent operating margin and more than 100 percent from a streaming service with a 20 percent margin; national digital services taxes are set at 2–3 percent of revenue for this reason. *Fix:* define Layer Two as a sectoral destination-based cash-flow tax: in-country sales less in-country costs, with payments to related parties abroad not deductible, at 20–25 percent, credited against Layer One; and cap the destination layers (see M2).

**M2. The sales layer (Layer Three) is a turnover tax and can exceed profit for low-margin firms.** $T = \tau_s S - d_K K - d_W W$ allows no deduction for purchased inputs. A fuel or grocery distributor with sales of €10 billion, payroll at 5 percent of sales and tangible assets at 10 percent pays 1.95 percent of sales (4 − 0.8 − 1.25), against a typical net margin of 1–3 percent. That taxes effort, not rent, and contradicts the paper's title. *Fix:* cap the combined destination layers at a share of in-country sales times the group's global pre-tax profit margin (taken from the consolidated accounts): a firm that makes little profit anywhere then pays little, while a firm that books high global margins but little local profit pays on the margin it earns from local customers. With a cap rate equal to the Layer One rate (50 percent), the destination layers can never take more than the profit tax would on sales-apportioned global profit.

**M3. $d_K$ and $d_W$ are credits against tax, not deductions from base, and their incentive is mis-described.** Because they are subtracted from the tax, each euro of in-country tangible assets cuts tax by 8 cents a year and each euro of payroll by 25 cents. The appendix says $d_K = 8\%$ gives "an extra 2–3% after-tax return" because it exceeds a normal return of 5–6 percent; that compares a credit rate with a rate of return. The incentive is an 8 percent annual subsidy on in-country assets and a 25 percent subsidy on in-country payroll, and only for firms whose Layer Three liability is positive; firms already at zero (the second worked example) get nothing at the margin. Land must be excluded from $K$, or firms will buy land to shelter tax. *Fix:* call them presence credits, state the incentive correctly, exclude land, and note that the credits make Layer Three a tax on local sales not matched by local activity, which is its purpose.

**M4. The 40–60 percent effective rate and the revenue estimates are asserted, not computed.** The draft never calculates a firm's tax under the design. When archetype firms are worked through (recomputed during this review; Table 1 of the revision), the 40–60 percent rate on rent holds for an asset-light foreign firm in a designated mobile-rent sector (50 percent once the cap in M2 applies), but a profit-shifting foreign seller outside those sectors pays about 13 percent of its local rent, because Layer Three at 4 percent of sales is small. The revenue appendix sums ranges whose inputs are unsourced ("corporate rent share of GDP is conventionally estimated at 4–8%"; "mobile-rent sector revenue approximately 8–12% of GDP") and applies the destination rate to gross sector revenue (M1). It also double counts: the land tax paid by firms is part of corporate rent. *Fix:* compute the archetypes, report the weak case honestly, and replace the revenue appendix with a transparent calculation from sourced inputs, labelled as an order of magnitude, with land tax deductible from the Layer One base.

**M5. The strongest objection is not stated: much "rent" is mobile, and origin taxation of mobile rent invites shifting.** The free-movement section says the ACE "does not distort the marginal investment decision, so capital has no reason to leave". That is right for the scale of an investment that will be made in the country anyway, but wrong for discrete location choices and for paper profits: location decisions for profitable projects depend on the average effective tax rate, not the marginal one (Devereux and Griffith 2003), and reported profit responds strongly to tax-rate differences (Beer, de Mooij and Liu 2020). A 50 percent rate on residual profit at origin will move intangible-heavy profit out of the declared base. The design already contains the answer (the destination layers reach mobile rent; the ACE layer at a high rate is safe only for location-specific rent), but the paper must say so, and the claim "no reason to leave" must go. This is the paper's central condition and should be stated as a proposition: a tax on rent at origin is safe from mobility only for rent that is specific to the location; rent that can be relocated must be reached at destination.

**M6. "Taxing those returns has no effect on the decisions that generated them" is the wrong justification.** Much of the market-power rent is a quasi-rent to past risky investment: R&D, brands, networks built at a loss. A permanent, anticipated tax on it lowers the expected return to that investment (the time-consistency objection). The correct argument is stronger: a tax on profit above a normal return, with losses carried forward at interest and investment (including R&D) allowed in full, is neutral ex ante for risky investment (Brown 1948; Boadway and Bruce 1984; Bond and Devereux 2003). The neutrality, not the past, protects innovation. The ACE also needs equity measured on the tax basis (tax written-down value of assets less debt) for neutrality; the draft says "book equity", which breaks it.

**M7. The market-power presumption needs its evidence and its counter-evidence.** The argument "large and sustained means rent or minimum efficient scale" omits the main alternative: large firms may be large because they are more productive (Demsetz 1973; "superstar firms", Autor et al. 2020). Ricardian rent from superior productivity is still rent, so the conclusion survives, but the paper should say so and cite the evidence on rising markups (De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger 2020) with the measurement critique (Basu 2019). It should also note the cliff at the €750 million threshold: a normal-return firm just above it pays nothing under Layer One, while one just below pays 25 percent of its normal return, and a rent-earning firm crossing it doubles its rate. Give the ACE to all firms and phase the higher rate in.

**M8. Several facts are wrong or need sources.**
- Appendix on border effects: "$\tau_d/(1+\tau_d)$ in tax-exclusive terms" mislabels the arithmetic. For a cash-flow tax at (tax-inclusive) rate $t$ with imports non-deductible, the border adjustment equals an import tariff and export subsidy of $t/(1-t)$ in tax-exclusive terms: 33 percent at $t = 25\%$, 100 percent at 50 percent (Auerbach et al. 2017). The draft's 20, 33 and 37.5 percent are the tax-inclusive equivalents of tax-exclusive rates, the opposite case. The conclusion (keep the rate moderate) is right and gets stronger.
- The claim that a high-rate destination tax gives "incentives to relocate production into the taxing country" is the wrong channel for an economy-wide tax, where the exchange rate offsets the border adjustment and location is neutral. For a *sectoral* tax there is no exchange-rate offset, so the right statement is that it taxes the sector's imported inputs and favors local costs at rate $t/(1-t)$.
- Incidence appendix: "firms with market power can pass through less because they were already pricing at the top of the demand curve" is not a general result. Under imperfect competition pass-through can exceed 100 percent, depending on the curvature of demand (Weyl and Fabinger 2013). The "self-correcting" incidence claim must go. VAT pass-through evidence is about full for standard-rate changes (Benedek et al. 2020), not 40–70 percent.
- "Belgium ran a version from 2006 to 2018; Italy from 2011 to 2023": check and date precisely (Belgium's notional interest deduction from tax year 2006, restricted to incremental equity from 2018; Italy's ACE from 2011, repealed from 2024).
- "The DST family of taxes faced challenge but survived when structured as indirect taxes": no court has ruled on a digital services tax in this way. Replace with what the Court of Justice did decide: progressive turnover taxes in Hungary (2020) and Poland (2021) are compatible with freedom of establishment and are not state aid.
- "The post-1945 US high-rate regime relied on Bretton Woods capital controls": the United States had few capital controls before 1963. Re-word to the general point about the Bretton Woods era.
- "Pillar Two reporting" does not contain sales by customer location; it has jurisdictional payroll and tangible assets (the substance-based income exclusion). Sales by destination come from VAT returns. Say so.
- Mirrlees Review: it endorsed an allowance for corporate equity and a land value tax on business and agricultural land; quote it accurately.
- "Top 0.1%": nothing in the paper links the design to the top 0.1 percent. State the link (ownership of land and corporate equity is highly concentrated) with a source, or drop the phrase.

**M9. The land tax needs its two classic qualifications.** (a) Capitalization: an announced land value tax lowers land prices at once by the present value of the tax, so the burden falls on owners at the announcement, not on later buyers (Mirrlees et al. 2011); this is the source of transition unfairness and the case for phasing. (b) Liquidity: land-rich, cash-poor owners need the option to defer until sale or death, as *Plucked!* proposes. The incidence claim "cannot be passed through" should cite the theory and note Feldstein's (1977) exception. The draft also needs the evidence: Pennsylvania's split-rate taxes (Oates and Schwab 1997; Banzhaf and Lavery 2010) and the OECD and IMF evidence that recurrent property taxes are the least growth-damaging and are under-used (around 1 percent of GDP in the OECD).

**M10. The paper omits the clearest real-world proof of its thesis: resource rent taxes.** Norway taxes petroleum profit at a marginal rate of 78 percent on a cash-flow-type base, and investment has continued; resource rent taxes (Garnaut and Clunies Ross 1975) are the working model of a high-rate tax on location-specific rent. This belongs in the literature and evidence sections.

## Minor points

1. "Reverse wealth tax" is used without definition and is confusing (it is an ordinary threshold with a land value tax above it; the "reverse" is that the allowance can be paid as a dividend to non-owners). Define once; otherwise call it a land value tax above a household allowance.
2. Business land: say whether the household allowance applies to companies (it should not) and that land is excluded from the Layer Three asset credit.
3. "The rate can be high without distorting the marginal investment decision ... whether the rate is 30% or 55%": true only with full loss offset or carry-forward at interest; state the condition.
4. The ACE allowance on the risk-free rate taxes the expected risk premium as well as rent; with loss offset the government's share of that risk premium has zero market value (it is paid for by bearing risk). Note this when reading revenue.
5. Step 3 of the algorithm says "credit against Layer One liability"; the combined formula needs to be written down once.
6. The scale carve-out ("reduced rate structure") is vague: specify it (the higher Layer One rate does not apply; Layers Two and Three apply as normal).
7. UK and US spellings are mixed ("behaviour", "neighbourhood", "unrealised", "labour"); use US spelling.
8. Bold run-in heads in the main text ("First, the threshold matters more than the definition") should become paragraph lead sentences.
9. Free-movement law: the deduction-for-presence structure may be challenged as a restriction on cross-border provision of services (Article 56 TFEU), since a remote seller gets no credit. The Hungarian cases do not settle that. Say so.
10. WTO: a border-adjusted direct tax is likely to be treated as a prohibited export subsidy (as was argued against the 2016 US border-adjustment proposal). State the risk; it is one more reason to keep Layer Two sectoral and to give it no export rebate.
11. Remove the empty `content-visible` block and the appendix "Notes on further reading" once citations are in the text.

## What the brief and the deck need

There is no brief. It should be the plain-language case: the problem (free movement caps rates on mobile bases; wealth taxes have been abandoned), the idea (tax rent through bases that cannot leave), the land tax with its allowance, the three corporate layers in words, one table of worked firms, the honest limits (mobile rent outside designated sectors; the legal risks), and what to do. About 2,000 words, bold lead sentences, no equations, links into the working paper for the arithmetic and the law.

The deck should take the same numbers: about ten slides, from the problem to the four worked firms and the actions.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.2 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). M1–M3: Layer Two redefined as a sectoral destination-based cash-flow tax (Proposition 4, Appendix B with the corrected $t/(1-t)$ arithmetic); the cap added (Proposition 5); $d_K$ and $d_W$ renamed presence credits, land excluded, incentive restated (Appendix C). M4: five archetype firms computed (Table 1, Figure 1) and the revenue appendix rebuilt from sourced inputs, with land tax deductible from Layer One (Table 2, Appendix D; `2.Source/rent_tax_calcs.py`); the first draft's 5.5–11.5 percent of GDP becomes 1.0–2.7 (land) plus 2.3–6.3 (corporate). M5: Proposition 3 states the location-specific condition and quantifies shifting. M6: Proposition 2 and Appendix A (neutrality, tax-basis equity, loss offset). M7: market-power evidence and counter-evidence added; ACE for all firms with a phased higher rate removes the cliff. M8–M10: corrected as recorded in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`; resource rent taxes and Norway added. Minor points 1–11 done (the "reverse wealth tax" term is explained once and dropped). Not resolved, and stated in the paper as open: the gap for foreign sellers outside the designated sectors (13 percent of rent), and country calibration with firm-level and cadastral data.
