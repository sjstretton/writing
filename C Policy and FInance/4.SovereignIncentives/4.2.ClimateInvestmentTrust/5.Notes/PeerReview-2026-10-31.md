# Peer review of 4.2, working paper v0.2 and policy brief v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of *The Climate Investment Trust: Hold Capital, Lend Its Strength*, written from a full reading before the v0.3 working paper and v1.0 brief. Every number in both forms was recomputed with `2.Source/model.py` (v0.2 output saved before any change; the v0.2 script is in `8.PreviousVersions/model-v0.2.py`). The chapter citations to the author's manuscripts were checked against `3.FinanceAndRisk/3.3.TimeAndChance/TimeAndChance.qmd` and `1.CarbonPricing/8.5.2.CarbonPricingRightPolicyWrongTime/CarbonPricingRightPolicyWrongTime.qmd`. Outside sources were checked separately; see `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a clear question (what institution can stand behind large guarantee-based incentives for climate policy at a donor cost that is politically possible, and grow without fresh donations?) and gives a clear answer (a trust that holds donor capital as a second-loss buffer behind policy guarantees, co-invests through a separate investment function, and allocates its surplus to national beneficiary accounts by a feebate between accounts). The three propositions are correctly derived and every number in the v0.2 text reproduces from the model. The problems are in what the numbers are taken to mean. Three results rest on assumptions the paper does not state or contradicts elsewhere: the incentive multiplier assumes the buffer earns what donors would otherwise earn (zero carry) and an infinite horizon, which makes one policy claim wrong; the growth results assume the Trust levers itself at 200 times capital on the development banks' record, which the paper itself advises against and which implies more guarantee exposure than the whole external debt of developing countries; and the buffer is sized for ultimate losses after recovery, not for the cash a call requires first. Each has a fix that keeps the paper's argument. The literature on why development banks lose so little (preferred creditor treatment) is the paper's crux and is barely cited. The brief is a second working paper (about 3,600 words, numbered sections, no key messages, no bold lead sentences, no links into the working paper) and the deck has four slides. The working paper has no executive summary.

## Major points

**M1. The multiplier of about 80 is right as defined, but its two conditions must be stated, and one claim built on it is wrong.** Proposition 1(b) gives $M = (s-f)/(\lambda - f)$ by discounting the incentive at the buffer's own net yield, with exposure kept at $K/b$ forever. (a) *Carry.* If donors' opportunity cost $d$ exceeds the buffer's net yield $r_s - c$, the held capital costs a carry $\kappa = d - (r_s - c)$ a year, and the same derivation gives $M = (s-f)/(\lambda - f + \kappa b)$. The buffer ratio then matters. At a 2 percent carry the ratios are 63, 8.6 and 2.3 (base, middle, stress), the figures already used in 4.1 and the series plan for "63 with a 2 percent carry". (b) *The wrong claim.* Section 5.2 says "a Trust that prices on the record but holds the stress-case buffer still delivers about 80". That is true only at zero carry and over an unbounded horizon: with a 9.25 percent buffer and losses at 0.038 percent a year, capital is consumed at 0.4 percent a year, so the 80 arrives over centuries. At a 2 percent carry the ratio is 13.5; at zero carry, savings to 2050 are 7.7 per dollar held, with 90 percent of the capital still there. The prudent policy the paper recommends (hold the stress buffer until a record exists) therefore delivers about 13, not 80, until the record is known. (c) *Horizon.* Even on its own buffer the base case delivers 68, not 80, by 2050. *Fix:* restate Proposition 1(b) with the carry term; say "per dollar of capital consumed by losses, at no net carry"; report 63/2.3 at a 2 percent carry and the horizon numbers; replace the wrong claim with the 13.5 figure. The series headline (about 80; 2.7 stress) survives, with its conditions stated.

**M2. The growth results assume an exposure no one would write.** Section 5.3 and the brief keep exposure at $K/b$ with the base-case buffer of 0.49 percent, so at the reported long-run capital of \$57 billion the Trust would guarantee \$11.5 trillion, more than the \$8.9 trillion combined external debt of all low- and middle-income countries in 2024. The same section and Section 8 advise holding the stress-case buffer (9.25 percent) until the Trust has its own record. The growth model should use the policy the paper recommends. With the stress buffer held: if losses follow the development banks' record, the buffer's yield exceeds its expected losses, the Trust grows by about 3 percent a year with no fee and no private income (\$10.7 billion after 25 years), and with \$95 billion of private co-investment it reaches \$34 billion after 10 years; if losses are at the stress level, it shrinks by 8.5 percent a year without fee income and levels off near \$28 billion with \$95 billion of co-investment. This is a cleaner result than v0.2's: the self-funding question is answered by the loss record, which the Trust will observe. *Fix:* recompute Section 5.3, Figure 3, the brief's Table 1 and Figure 1(B) on the stress buffer; drop the \$57 billion long-run figure (and the feebate limit of \$95 per tonne derived from it); replace with \$8 per tonne at launch and \$31–57 per tonne after 10 years. The series plan's headline "Trust long-run capital about \$57bn with no guarantee fee (\$28bn stress)" must change (coordinator).

**M3. The buffer is sized for ultimate losses, but a guarantee call needs cash first.** In the base case a called program loses 4.9 percent of its exposure in the end because 95.1 percent is recovered, but the Trust must pay the missed debt service when it falls due and wait for the recovery. A buffer of 0.49 percent of exposure cannot pay even one year's debt service on one of 20 programs (one-twentieth of exposure times about 13 percent for a 10-year amortizing loan at 5 percent, 0.65 percent). If each call costs one year's debt service in cash, the 1 percent exhaustion buffer is 1.3 percent in the base case and \$5 billion supports about \$390 billion, not \$1 trillion; the stress case, where the ultimate loss exceeds a year's debt service, is unchanged. *Fix:* add Proposition 1(d) (buffer = quantile calls × max(L, m)/n), report the numbers, and recommend that the AAA backer provide a liquidity line so the buffer can be sized on losses. Keep "about \$1 trillion" only as a solvency ceiling on paper.

**M4. The crux, preferred creditor treatment, needs its literature and its weakness stated.** The difference between the base and stress cases is whether obligations to the Trust are honored like obligations to development banks. The paper asserts this but cites nothing on why the banks' record is so good. The G20 capital adequacy review says preferred creditor treatment is *informal*, with no binding statutory or contractual status; Schlegl, Trebesch and Wright (2019) show from repayment data that the IMF and World Bank are de facto senior; Perraudin, Powell and Yang (2016) show that rating agencies give too little credit for it. The GEMs default rate also partly reflects a change in reporting method (EIB 2025). *Fix:* add these, state that a Trust inherits preferred treatment only if its claims are booked as claims of (or through) a development bank, and make that the first design question.

**M5. The private co-investment assumption is the weakest number and should be framed against real vehicles.** \$95 billion of construction-stage equity, 19 times the launch capital, paying 25 percent of a 10 percent gross return (7.5 percent net) is an upper case. The closest working vehicle, Climate Investor One, closed its development and construction equity funds at \$850 million in 2019; deal-by-deal blended finance mobilized \$0.75 of private money per dollar in 2012–2015 (Attridge and Engen 2019); and the IEA finds the cost of capital for solar in emerging economies well over twice that in advanced economies, so a 7.5 percent net return is low. *Fix:* cite these; present \$95 billion as the book's indicative upper case and \$10 billion as the realistic lower case throughout.

**M6. The working paper needs an executive summary, and the brief must become a brief.** Add a non-technical chapter-1 summary (500–700 words, bold leads, real numbers). Rewrite the brief to about 2,000 words: key messages, claim headings, bold lead sentences, one table and one figure with the working paper's numbers, links into the working paper's sections, related papers with links.

## Minor points

1. **Introduction arithmetic.** "A \$100 per tonne global carbon tax would raise nearly \$5 trillion a year and require transfers of about \$1 trillion a year from China" is cited only to the author's manuscript and mixes greenhouse gases with CO₂. Re-base on the Global Carbon Budget 2025: fossil CO₂ of about 37.8 Gt in 2024, 12.3 Gt in China, so \$3.8 trillion a year, \$1.2 trillion of it in China; a reward for cutting a tenth costs about \$380 billion a year.
2. **TFFF sponsor capital.** "40-year loans" (from WRI) should follow Concept Note 3.0: sponsor capital (loans, notes, paid-in capital, guarantees or grants) repaid over 30 years after a 10-year grace period, at a cost near long-dated US Treasury yields. Add the 2026 position (UK and Netherlands pledges; operation expected by mid-2027).
3. **"The same four paths give K\* of \$28 billion, \$95 billion and \$2.9 billion"** lists three numbers. Superseded by M2.
4. **Participation constraint.** The model subtracts the total carrying cost of all country stakes (\$40 million) from each country's gain; per country it is \$4 million. Thresholds change from \$52, \$11 and \$3.7 per tonne to \$52.6, \$11.6 and \$4.3.
5. **The base allocation** ($a$ = \$1 billion a year, \$100 million per country) is used in the model but not stated in the text. State it.
6. **Feasibility bound.** Proposition 3(c) includes the year's base allocation; the calibration uses balances only. Say the calibration is the conservative version.
7. **Vasicek (2002)** is titled "The Distribution of Loan Portfolio Value", not "Loan Portfolio Value" (Risk.net's page title). Align with 3.2's entry.
8. **Unsupported attributions to the manuscripts.** "A trust that begins informal does not become formal later" is not in *Time and Chance* ch. 20; make it the author's argument. Appendix C says ch. 8 once called the equity fund a "first-loss fund"; the current manuscript does not; cut. "Climate lending that is not additional to existing envelopes is opposed by whoever loses envelope [ch. 17]" overstates ch. 17; ch. 18 says incentives work only if additional to the existing envelope.
9. **Cross-references.** Sections are referred to by typed numbers ("Section 4.4", "Section 5.4"), tables by typed captions, equations by typed "(1)". Give every section an identifier and use Quarto references, so the brief can link into the working paper and numbering survives edits.
10. **Roadmap paragraph** in the introduction: cut to one sentence.
11. **Initial growth.** "Doubles in about two years" now describes only the launch year under full leverage; with the stress buffer and record losses, capital grows by half in the first year. Say "growth slows as capital catches up with fee income" and drop doubling claims except as lineage.
12. **Section 3 vocabulary** is consistent with 3.2 (regional fund holds the first-loss tranche; 5–8 percent cushion beneath a near-AAA senior bond in a large pool, about 30 percent in first real deals). Add 3.2's estimate of first-loss capital needed at scale (\$50–160 billion over a decade) to show the regional funds need private equity and recycling, not the buffer.
13. **Section 3 references to series 3.** If 3.1's headline is quoted it is "about \$2 per dollar of expected public cost", and 3.2's "about a fifth (14–19 percent)". v0.2 quotes neither; keep it that way except for 3.2's cushion, and keep the series 4 multiplier apart from both.
14. **Disclaimer and first line.** Use the series form: first italic line links to the brief with `[Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 4.2](brief:)`.

## What the brief needs

About 2,000 words; key messages first (four or five bold one-or-two-sentence paragraphs); claim headings; every paragraph's first sentence in bold; one table (indicative scale, with the corrected growth and liquidity numbers) and one figure (multiplier by case, capital paths under the prudent buffer); no propositions; links to the working paper's sections for the model, calibration and design questions; the TFFF term corrected; "What to do"; related papers with links. Drop the long precedents section to one paragraph per precedent at most, and do not repeat the working paper's derivations.

## What the deck needs

Four slides is too few. About ten: title and message; the problem in numbers; what the Trust is (funding, control, benefit); the loss stack (who loses first); why holding beats spending (multiplier by case); the conditions (carry, liquidity, preferred creditor treatment); growth under the prudent buffer; the feebate between accounts; precedents; what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.3 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). M1 keeps the series headline (about 80 per donor dollar, 2.7 stress; 63 and 2.3 at a 2 percent carry) with its conditions stated and corrects the claim about holding the stress buffer (about 13, not 80). M2 replaces the long-run capital of \$57 billion (\$28 billion stress) with growth under the stress-case buffer: \$34 billion after 10 years on the record with \$95 billion of co-investment, about \$28 billion in the long run in the stress case; the series plan and 4.0 overview quote \$57 billion and must change (coordinator). M3 adds a liquidity condition (\$5 billion supports about \$390 billion if calls are paid in cash before recovery). The deck now has ten slides.
