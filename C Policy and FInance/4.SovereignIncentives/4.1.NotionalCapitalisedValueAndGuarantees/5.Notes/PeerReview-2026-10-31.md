# Peer review of 4.1, working paper v0.1 and policy brief v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 4.1 ("Reward the Stock as Well as the Flow: Notional Capitalized Value and Guarantee-Based Climate Incentives") before the v0.2 working paper and v1.0 brief. Every derivation in Appendix A was re-done by hand and every number was recomputed with `2.Source/model.py`. The sources were checked separately (`BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`).

## Summary judgement

The paper has a clear question (how can the international system give a government a large, early and credible incentive to protect carbon stocks and cut emissions without cash transfers?) and a clear answer (a notional balance, opened by the carbon stock and debited per tonne, used as guarantee capacity). The three propositions are correct as stated, and the separation of the stock price from the debit price is a real and well-argued contribution. The calibration reproduces exactly from the code.

Five things stop it from being at the standard. (1) The headline ratio of about 80 counts the whole spread as the country's gain, but part of the spread is the market's fair charge for the default option the country gives up when it counter-indemnifies an MDB; the economic gain is smaller, and the paper does not say so. (2) The paper misdescribes its closest theoretical precedent (Harstad 2016 finds that markets for conservation *fail*, not that they beat demand policy) and omits the closest policy precedents: ART-TREES's high-forest, low-deforestation (HFLD) adjustment, which already rewards a sliver of the stock, and the "no forest left behind" proposal of da Fonseca et al. (2007). (3) Several factual errors in the introduction and literature, including the Hess numbers and the TFFF's sponsor terms. (4) Two results are stated more strongly than the model allows (a $30 carbon-price equivalent that needs an open-ended scheme and a binding balance; "never acts" without an incentive). (5) There is no non-technical executive summary, and the brief (about 3,100 words, no bold lead sentences, an "Executive summary" in place of key messages) is not yet a brief.

## Major points

**M1. The incentive value overstates the country's economic gain; say by how much.** Proposition 1 values the guarantee at the full spread saved, $\Delta s$. That is the right measure of the *budget* saving a finance ministry sees each year. But a sovereign that borrows through a guarantee backed by its own counter-indemnity gives up its default option on that debt: in a crisis it owes the MDB, which it repays (95.1 percent recovery on the GEMs record), instead of bondholders, who would take a haircut. Part of the market spread is the fair price of that option. If the spread is expected loss to market creditors plus a risk premium, and the guarantor is called whenever the country would have defaulted, the country's economic gain per dollar of exposure is the risk premium plus the guarantor's own expected loss, and the ratio becomes $1 + \text{premium}/\text{expected loss}$. Longstaff, Pan, Pedersen and Singleton (2011) estimate the risk premium at about a third of sovereign credit spreads. With a 3-point spread that gives about 27 on the MDB record, 4.3 in the middle case and 1.9 in the stress case (recomputed in `model.py`). The headline of 80 (and 2.7) is a budget ratio and can stay as the series headline, but the paper must state the economic ratio as a lower bound, and the brief must say in one sentence that part of the saving is the country giving up the option to default on that debt. The same mechanism means guaranteed debt is effectively senior, so unguaranteed creditors may charge more (Chamon et al. 2022 make the parallel point for debt-for-climate swaps); this belongs in the risks section. *Fix:* add Proposition 1(b) with proof; add the dashed line to Figure 2; add a "subordination" risk.

**M2. The closest work is misdescribed or missing.**
- Harstad (2016), "The market for conservation and other hostages", is cited for the claim that "buying conservation directly can beat taxing demand". Its result is the opposite: when a buyer pays an owner to conserve, the owner's ability to threaten exploitation undermines the sale, conservation is less likely the more valuable it is, and the forest can be lost with probability one. This is the strongest theoretical objection to paying for stocks, and the NCV has an answer the paper should give: the opening balance is fixed at entry from a verified stock and does not rise with the threat; the reward per tonne is set by a uniform debit price, so posing a threat earns nothing. *Fix:* re-word the citation and add the answer in the literature and risks sections.
- **ART-TREES already pays a little for the stock.** Its HFLD crediting approach lets a high-forest, low-deforestation jurisdiction credit against less than 0.05 percent of its standing carbon stock a year on top of measured reductions (ICVCM 2026). Guyana's 2016–2020 issuance used it (ART validation report, 2022). So "Guyana is paid for none of [its stock]" is wrong; the accurate statement is that the stock is rewarded only through a contested baseline adjustment worth a few hundredths of a percent of the stock a year. This also strengthens the paper's case: the HFLD adjustment is a stock reward disguised as an avoided-emissions credit, which is why it is contested on additionality.
- **da Fonseca et al. (2007)** proposed "preventive credits" for HFLD countries, credited against a reference rate indexed to the global deforestation rate. It is the policy precedent for rewarding countries that never cleared.
- **Ecuador's Yasuní-ITT initiative** asked the world for $3.6 billion to leave oil in the ground and raised about $13.5 million before it ended in August 2013 (Hernández Rentería 2020). It is the clearest evidence for the paper's premise that donors will not pay cash for stocks.
*Fix:* restate the contribution against these four, not only against Harstad and the TFFF.

**M3. The carbon-price equivalent of $30 per tonne holds only under two conditions the calibration does not state.** Proposition 2(a) assumes capacity binds from the tonne's date *forever*. (a) If the scheme ends in 2050, a tonne emitted in 2027 costs $20 (23 years of lost spread at 5 percent), and one emitted in 2040 costs $12; the price falls toward zero as the end approaches. (b) In the forest calibration the "acts now" balance rises above the $5 billion borrowing need from 2037 (it peaks near $5.8 billion in 2045), so by Proposition 2(d) a tonne then costs nothing unless the surplus flows somewhere of value. *Fix:* say both in the calibration; make "open-ended, or ending in a beneficiary account" and "surplus to a beneficiary account" explicit design rules.

**M4. Factual errors.**
- "33.47 million credits for 2016–2020 ... of which 37.5 million over 2016–2030 go to Hess": 37.5 million cannot be "of which" 33.47 million. Hess buys 2.5 million credits a year of vintages 2016–2030 (37.5 million in all) for at least $750 million, paid 2022–2032; the first payment, $75 million, was for 2016 and 2017 vintages at $15 a tonne (DPI 2023).
- TFFF sponsor capital is described as "40-year loans at rates comparable to US Treasury yields" (from the WRI article). Concept Note 3.0 says the refined model has "a reduced repayment term (30 years), a 10-year grace period"; its projections assume sponsor funding "repaid over a 40-year period, with a 10-year grace period", that is, repaid over 30 years from year 10, and sponsor funding may take several forms (paid-in capital, notes, concessional loans, guarantees), at a cost assumed not to exceed US Treasury yields. *Fix:* "repaid over 30 years after a 10-year grace period, at a cost assumed not to exceed US Treasury yields". The TFFF also invests in a diversified global bond portfolio, not only emerging-market bonds.
- Gabon's bond: the cited Reuters page (a BusinessDay reprint) does not contain the 6.097 percent, the Aa2 rating or the 9–10.5 percent yields. Verified replacements: 6.097 percent coupon and Aa2 against Caa1/B (African Sovereign Debt Justice Network 2023); 200 basis points over US Treasuries and Gabon's 2031 bonds yielding about 10 percent (Smith 2023). The saving is therefore about 3.9 points against the 2031 bonds, gross of the insurance premium; "9–10.5 percent" is cut.
- IBRD's 18 percent equity-to-loans minimum was cited to a FY2026 SEC filing that could not be opened; the World Bank's press release of 15 October 2024 confirms the cut from 19 to 18 percent.
- Steffen (2020) finds that developing countries face "substantially higher" capital costs; "several percentage points" is not in the abstract. Re-word.
- Norway's agreements were "the largest such programs": not in Angelsen (2017). Cut.
- "A results-based payment of $1 billion a year would be among the largest climate transfers ever voted" is unsourced. Replace with the TFFF concept note's figure: multilateral REDD+ funds pledged $6.4 billion from 2008, about $400 million a year.
- Collier and Venables (2014) and Asheim et al. (2019) do not argue for *compensating* owners; Collier and Venables argue for closing coal in sequence, starting in rich countries, and Asheim et al. for a treaty limiting fossil fuel supply. Re-word.

**M5. Proposition 3's discussion contains an error and an inconsistent number.**
- "Without an incentive and with $b < \omega + (r+g)K_0/\Delta E$, the government never acts." Wrong when $b > \omega$: then $h(T)$ falls over time and the government acts at $T^* = \frac1g\ln\frac{(r+g)K_0}{\Delta E(b-\omega)}$, once the cost has fallen enough (28 years for a $5 co-benefit in the stylized reform). It never acts only if $b \le \omega$. *Fix:* state both cases.
- The option-value factor is computed as 1.6–1.9 for volatility of 15–20 percent (re-derived: $\beta = 2.67$ and $2.16$), but the text then says "half as large again, or twice as large" and the calibration uses 1.5 ($30 per tonne). Use the computed range: $32–37 per tonne.

**M6. MDB capital is a cost the base case leaves out.** The base case charges only expected loss and a 0.5 percent buffer. If the guarantees instead consume MDB capital at IBRD's 18 percent equity-to-loans minimum with a 2 percent carry, the ratio is 7.5, not 80 (`model.py` already computed this, but the text did not report it). That is the realistic case for a scheme run inside an MDB balance sheet without the trust, and it is one more reason the Climate Investment Trust's buffer, and the G20 capital-adequacy reforms, matter. *Fix:* report it in the sensitivity paragraph.

**M7. The paper needs a non-technical executive summary, and the brief needs to become a brief.** Add `# Executive summary {#sec-summary}` as chapter 1. Rewrite the brief to about 2,000 words with key messages, bold lead sentences, claim headings, one table and one figure with the working paper's numbers, links into the working paper's sections, "What to do", and related papers.

## Minor points

1. Introduction lists four contributions; the literature section's "gap" lists three strands. Make one list, stated against the named precedents (M2).
2. "We write" in Proposition 3's setup: single author. Use "written".
3. Proposition 2 calls $\tau$ the "carbon-price equivalent ... the uniform carbon price that gives the same reward for abating one tonne". The series term (6.1) is the uniform carbon tax that cuts emissions by as much. Say that a government that cuts until marginal cost equals marginal reward makes the same cut, so the two definitions agree.
4. TFFF's capitalized stock price: $0.074 against $0.256 is "under a third", not "about a quarter".
5. "Each $1 of debit price converts to only 20 cents" (coal) and "60 cents" (Proposition 2) are right; state the general rule $\Delta s/(r+\theta)$ once.
6. The section on scale says capacity "costs donors about $13 million a year". The table has $12.6 million; round consistently ($13 million in text, $12.6 million in the table is fine with a note).
7. "Guyana ... with oil revenue flowing since production began in 2019": unsourced date; drop the year.
8. "Brazil ... invests in emerging-market bonds and pays the spread over its funding cost": TFFF pays from the difference between its funding cost and portfolio return, after senior debt service and sponsor interest (Concept Note 3.0). Word it so.
9. The executive summary of the brief calls the ratio "$80 of interest savings" per dollar of donor cost, which mixes up expected loss and donor cost when a carry is charged. Use "dollar of expected loss" or state the zero-carry assumption.
10. Section ids are missing on the definition subsections and the brief cannot link to them. Add `{#sec-...}` ids throughout.
11. The README's `5.Notes/notes.md` says "Working notes for 3.1"; `model.py` and `make_wp_figures.py` said "working paper 3.1" and cross-referenced 3.2/3.3 for the companion papers (stale numbering). Fixed in the scripts.

13. Section 3's headlines, if quoted: the 3.1 contract guarantee saves about $2 per dollar of expected cost; 3.2 saves about a fifth of the price of solar power. 4.1 quotes neither, but it should tell readers that 3.1's ratio is a different quantity from Proposition 1's.

## Strongest objection, and whether the paper answers it

The strongest objection is that the opening balance pays for stocks that were never at risk, and that pricing the stock invites hold-up (Harstad 2016). The paper's answer (the balance is cheap because it is capacity, it is what makes the debit enforceable, and the margin is set by the debit price) is good but incomplete: it should add that the balance is fixed at entry and so cannot be raised by threatening to clear, and that the HFLD adjustment shows the alternative, a stock reward smuggled into a baseline, is worse for integrity. With M1, the second strongest objection is that the guarantee's value to the country is smaller than its budget value; the paper should concede the point and show that the conclusion (more incentive per donor dollar than a grant on the record) survives, while in the stress case net of the default option the guarantee is barely better than a grant.

## Disposition

All major points (M1–M7) and minor points 1–11 and 13 are addressed, plus two points from the 4.2 review (carry: pricing on the record while holding the stress buffer at a 2 percent carry gives about 13, not 80; liquidity: calls need a liquidity line, or \$5 billion supports about \$390 billion rather than \$1 trillion), in working paper v0.2 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). Minor point 12 is for the coordinator (shared series file). The series headline (about 80 on the MDB record; 2.7 stress) is kept as the budget ratio, with the economic lower bound (about 27; 1.9 stress) reported beside it in both forms.
