# Series 4 architecture: Sovereign Incentives

The plan for section 4 as one coherent series: its argument, its papers, their abstracts and outlines, and the terms they share. Agreed with Stephen on 29 Oct 2026. Briefs follow `E Writing Instructions/Writing Standards/briefing-series-spec.md`; working papers follow `paper-standard.md`.

## The series argument

**Mitigation pays off globally, while its costs fall on national governments, so incentives for national climate policy must come from outside. Cash transfers on the required scale are politically impossible. The international system can instead reward governments with what they value and what it can supply cheaply: lower borrowing costs, delivered through guarantees and collateral. A guarantee is worth its full spread saving to the borrower but costs the guarantor only its expected loss, and that gap is what makes a large incentive affordable.**

The international incentive routes, compared:

| Route | What the government gets | What it costs the giver | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon pricing and policy crediting | Payment per tonne cut by a policy, measured as a carbon-price equivalent | Cash, per tonne | Series 1 (1.1, 6.1) |
| Conditional de-risking | Guarantees and intermediaries for its clean power pipeline, on condition of reform | Expected loss on contingent exposure | Series 3 (3.1, 3.2) |
| Sustainability-linked lending | A lower interest rate if agreed indicators are met | Forgone interest; small per loan | 4.3 |
| Notional capitalized value | A climate balance, credited for carbon stocks and low emissions, usable as guarantee capacity | Expected loss on the guarantees drawn, held as a buffer | 4.1 |
| Climate Investment Trust | The capital pool that stands behind those guarantees, invests, and holds national beneficiary accounts | Donor capital that is held and earns, not spent | 4.2 |

The series' claims:

1. Paying for marginal tonnes against an unobservable counterfactual rewards weak plans, pays late and discourages early action. Reward stocks and long-run paths too.
2. The form of the reward matters more than the headline amount. A balance usable as guarantee capacity arrives in advance and can be borrowed against; cash on results arrives after the spending it was meant to induce.
3. Sustainability-linked loans are the working prototype, and they are too small. Uruguay's $350m World Bank loan (2023) cuts the rate by up to 100 basis points a year, worth at most about $12.5m over its life. Codify them and they scale in number; combine them with guarantee capacity and they scale in size.
4. Notional capitalized value supplies the size: a country's balance (for example, carbon stock times a notional price) becomes a cap on guarantee exposure for its green borrowing, drawn down by business-as-usual emissions. The cost to donors is the expected loss on the guarantees, not the balance.
5. The Climate Investment Trust supplies the institution and the capital: it holds a buffer against guarantee calls rather than paying out, earns on the capital, and holds national beneficiary accounts. Holding instead of spending is the leverage.
6. Timing is the distinctive problem. Falling technology costs, immature carbon markets and high dollar rates all reward waiting. An incentive architecture must pay a premium for acting early, and both the decaying balance (4.1) and periodic account allocation (4.2) do so by design.

**Boundary with series 3.** Series 3 decides who bears a loss once a project exists. Series 4 changes whether the government chooses the policy and the pipeline. The pieces compose: guarantee capacity earned under 4.1 can stand behind the intermediary's waterfall in 3.1 or the senior bonds in 3.2; the Trust's regional funds (4.2) can hold the first-loss tranche in 3.2.

**Out of scope.** The MDB climate envelope (8.AdditionalPolicyPapers, 8.3.1) and Reforming MDBs (8.3.2) stay in section 8. The 8.3.1 note is Dirk Heine's internal note and must not be quoted. ### 4.1 Reward the Stock as Well as the Flow: Notional Capitalized Value and Guarantee-Based Climate Incentives

*Subtitle: Turning carbon stocks and low-emission paths into cheaper borrowing.*

**Question.** How can the international system give a government a large, early and credible incentive to protect carbon stocks and cut emissions, without a cash transfer that donors will not vote for?

**Answer.** Credit each participating country with a notional capitalized value: its carbon stock (forest and, optionally, fossil reserves left unburned) times a notional price, plus credit for an emissions path below an agreed benchmark, minus a debit for business-as-usual emissions each year. The balance is not paid out. It sets the country's guarantee capacity: the maximum outstanding guarantee exposure that multilateral development banks (backed by the Climate Investment Trust) will provide on the country's eligible green borrowing. The value to the country is the spread saved on guaranteed debt; the cost to donors is the expected loss on that exposure, funded as a buffer. Because emissions shrink the balance, the incentive acts from year one, and a declining release schedule pays a premium for acting early.

**Abstract (draft, about 150 words).** Climate finance pays countries for marginal emission cuts measured against counterfactuals nobody can observe, and pays almost nothing for carbon stocks that were never released. This paper proposes a notional capitalized value: a balance credited to each country for its carbon stocks and low-emission path, debited each year for business-as-usual emissions, and used not as cash but as guarantee capacity on the country's green borrowing. I show that because a guarantee costs its provider the expected loss while it saves the borrower the full credit spread, a balance funded by a modest buffer can deliver an incentive several times larger than the same money paid as grants, and I derive the conditions under which the incentive is large enough to bring forward policy that a government would otherwise delay. A worked example for a forest-rich country gives the incentive value, the donor cost and the carbon-price equivalent. The risks are moral hazard, valuation disputes and hidden fiscal exposure.

**Brief outline.** Executive summary; 1 Climate finance pays for flows and ignores stocks; 2 The notional capitalized value: definition and worked example (*Time and Chance* ch. 21: 100 MtCO₂ at $100/t gives $10bn); 3 From balance to guarantee capacity: why a guarantee is worth more than it costs; 4 Paying for acting early; 5 How big can it be? (value to country, cost to donors, carbon-price equivalent); 6 Risks and safeguards; 7 What to do; Related papers.

**Working paper outline.** 1 Introduction. 2 Literature: results-based climate finance and REDD+ (jurisdictional REDD+, ART-TREES, Guyana's credit sale); the Tropical Forest Forever Facility; payment for ecosystem services; additionality and baselines; guarantees and the cost of capital; climate clubs (Nordhaus 2015); real options and policy delay (Dixit and Pindyck 1994). 3 Definition: balance B_t = p·S_t + credit for path below benchmark − p·E_t^BAU; the four earlier formulations reconciled (interest on a notional value; NCCV guarantee capacity; climate allowance) and the choice made. 4 Model: value of guarantee capacity to the sovereign (spread × duration × utilisation) vs cost to the guarantor (PD × LGD × exposure, plus capital charge); Proposition 1 (incentive-to-cost ratio); Proposition 2 (early action: with a decaying balance and falling technology costs, the condition under which acting now beats waiting); Proposition 3 (incentive compatibility: why the debit must be per tonne emitted); carbon-price equivalent of the incentive. 5 Calibration: a forest-rich country (stylized, with public stock data for a named country where verifiable), a coal-reserve variant; sensitivity to spread, default probability and notional price. 6 Institutional delivery: who issues the guarantees (MDBs), who funds the buffer (the Trust, 4.2), links to 3.1/3.2 and to sustainability-linked lending (4.3). 7 Risks: moral hazard, valuation, permanence and leakage, fiscal exposure, sovereignty and fairness to low-stock countries. 8 Policy implications. 9 Conclusion. Appendices: proofs, code.

### 4.2 The Climate Investment Trust: Hold Capital, Lend Its Strength

*Subtitle: A buffer fund that insures policy guarantees, invests in clean energy and holds national climate accounts.*

**Question.** What institution can stand behind large guarantee-based incentives, at a donor cost that is politically possible, and grow without fresh donations?

**Answer.** A trust that holds capital instead of disbursing it. Donors hold the votes; the formal beneficiaries are the post-2050 populations of participating countries, through national beneficiary accounts that can be pledged as collateral now and withdrawn only later. The trust does three jobs with one pool: it takes second loss on policy guarantees (government first loss, trust second, a AAA entity behind), it invests equity in the development stage of clean energy projects and in regional first-loss funds, and it allocates returns to national accounts according to performance. A buffer sized to expected and stressed losses costs far less than paying every reward in cash; returns let it grow.

**Abstract (draft, about 150 words).** Large climate transfers fail because donors will not vote for them and recipients cannot borrow against promises. This paper proposes a Climate Investment Trust that holds capital rather than spending it, and uses the same pool three times: as second-loss insurance behind the guarantees that reward climate policy, as equity in clean energy development and in regional first-loss funds, and as national beneficiary accounts that countries can pledge as collateral now but withdraw only after 2050. I show that a buffer sized to stressed guarantee losses supports guarantee exposure several times its own size, and I set out the conditions under which investment returns let the trust grow without new donations. Indicative arithmetic, with explicit assumptions, compares the trust with the IMF's Resilience and Sustainability Trust and the Tropical Forest Forever Facility. Governance, the price of first loss and the recycling rule decide whether it works.

**Brief outline.** Executive summary; 1 Climate money is spent once; 2 What the trust is: funding, control and benefit separated; 3 Three jobs: insure guarantees, invest, hold accounts; 4 Why holding beats spending (buffer arithmetic); 5 Indicative scale (clearly labelled assumptions); 6 Precedents (IMF RST, TFFF, IFFIm, hybrid capital); 7 Design questions: governance, price of first loss, recycling; 8 What to do; Related papers.

**Working paper outline.** 1 Introduction. 2 Literature and precedents: MDB capital adequacy and the G20 CAF review, hybrid capital and SDR rechannelling, IFFIm front-loading, the IMF Resilience and Sustainability Trust, the Tropical Forest Forever Facility (junior/senior structure), portfolio guarantee platforms, insurance float economics. 3 The trust: structure, control and benefit, beneficiary accounts, the unallocated global account. 4 Model: buffer capital needed for a target probability of exhausting it, given guarantee exposure, default probabilities and correlation (Proposition 1: leverage of a buffer vs a payout fund); growth dynamics with return r, fee share and allocation rule (Proposition 2: self-funding condition); allocation rule with participation and performance incentives (Proposition 3). 5 Calibration: indicative scale with all assumptions stated; reconcile the versions (25% fee, 10% return in the master; 10% fee, 15% return in older notes) and use one set; label as indicative. 6 Regional funds and the equity layer (link to 3.2's first-loss tranche). 7 Design questions: governance, price of first loss, recycling, private capital on limited rights. 8 Policy implications. 9 Conclusion. Appendices: derivations, code, lineage (Climate Incentives Trust, May 2024; *Time and Chance* chs. 19–20).

### 4.3 Codify Sustainability-Linked Sovereign Lending, Then Give It Scale

*Subtitle: Standard indicators, verification and pricing, and why the reward must be bigger than an interest margin.*

**Question.** Can sustainability-linked sovereign lending become a scalable incentive instrument, and how large must the reward be to change policy?

**Answer.** Codify it: a menu of standard indicators tied to physical and fiscal outturns, verification against published data, and a pricing schedule rather than case-by-case negotiation. Separate the investor's return from the borrower's incentive, so investors receive a fixed coupon and the reward flows through a third party (the SLIDI design). But a margin step-down of 25–100 basis points on a few hundred million dollars is worth millions, against policy costs in the hundreds of millions. To matter, the reward must be leveraged: make guarantee capacity under the notional capitalized value (4.1) or access to the Trust's collateral (4.2) the prize for meeting the indicators.

**Abstract (draft, about 150 words).** Sustainability-linked loans and bonds tie a sovereign's interest rate to climate and development indicators, but they remain rare, bespoke and small. Uruguay's 2023 World Bank loan, the leading example, can save the country at most about $12.5 million in interest. This paper proposes codifying the instrument: a standard menu of indicators tied to outturns rather than process, verification against published data, and a published pricing schedule. It separates the investor's return from the borrower's incentive, so investors hold a fixed coupon while a trusted intermediary pays the performance reward. I then compare the size of the reward with the fiscal and political cost of the policies it is meant to induce, and show that interest margins alone are an order of magnitude too small. Linking the indicators to guarantee capacity and collateral gives the instrument the scale it lacks.

**Brief outline.** Executive summary; 1 Sustainability-linked sovereign debt exists and stays small (Chile 2022, Uruguay 2022 bond and 2023 loan, Thailand 2024; market data); 2 Why it does not scale: bespoke indicators, verification costs, variable coupons, no pricing method, the runway problem; 3 Codify: indicator menu, verification, pricing schedule; 4 Separate investor return from incentive: fixed coupon, intermediary rebate (SLIDIs); 5 The size problem (worked comparison); 6 Give it scale: guarantee capacity and collateral as the reward; 7 What to do; Related papers.

**Working paper outline.** 1 Introduction. 2 Literature and market evidence: SLB market data (Climate Bonds Initiative, ICMA Sustainability-Linked Bond Principles), sovereign SLB cases, studies of the "sustainability premium" (greenium) and step-up sizes, critiques of target ambition. 3 Design: indicator menu by sector (outturns, not process), baselines, verification from published national data, timing (the runway problem as a general concept). 4 Model: borrower's incentive from a step-down Δ on debt D over T years vs the cost of the policy; Proposition 1 (minimum reward to induce action); Proposition 2 (why a fixed-coupon, third-party-rebate design is cheaper for the borrower than a variable coupon, given investor dislike of coupon uncertainty); Proposition 3 (leverage: reward delivered as guarantee capacity). 5 Illustrations: Uruguay loan arithmetic; a stylized coal-to-renewables reform. 6 Institutional delivery: trusted intermediary, links to 4.1 and 4.2, and to series 1 policy crediting. 7 Risks: weak targets, greenwashing, accounting treatment of KPI-linked rates (in general terms), moral hazard. 8 Policy implications. 9 Conclusion. Appendices: indicator menu, code.

## Shared terms (series 4; use exactly these)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| sovereign incentive | A reward to a national government, from outside, for adopting and keeping climate policy |
| notional capitalized value (NCV) | A country's climate balance: carbon stock times a notional price, plus credit for a path below benchmark, minus business-as-usual emissions each year. Not paid out |
| guarantee capacity | The maximum outstanding guarantee exposure that multilateral development banks will provide on a country's eligible green borrowing, set by its notional capitalized value |
| expected loss | Probability of default × loss given default × exposure: the economic cost of a guarantee to its provider |
| incentive value | The present value of the spread a government saves on guaranteed borrowing |
| buffer fund | A fund that holds capital against guarantee calls rather than paying rewards out |
| Climate Investment Trust | The buffer fund proposed in 4.2 (originally the Climate Incentives Trust, May 2024) |
| beneficiary account | A national account in the Trust, credited by performance, pledgeable as collateral now, withdrawable after 2050 |
| sustainability-linked loan / bond | Debt whose price depends on agreed key performance indicators |
| key performance indicator (KPI) | A measured outturn that triggers the reward |
| early-action premium | The extra reward for acting now rather than later, built into the release schedule |
| carbon-price equivalent | As in series 1: the uniform carbon price giving the same emission cut |

## Facts to settle (from the source audit, 29 Oct 2026)

- **NCV exists in four versions** (interest on a notional value paid into an account; NCCV as guarantee capacity in *Time and Chance* ch. 21; a balance on stocks plus long-run emissions in `research.qmd`; a "climate allowance" in the DPhil proposal). 4.1 reconciles them and chooses guarantee capacity as the main form, with interest-into-account as the Trust variant.
- **Ch. 21's arithmetic** ($1bn of guarantees a year at a 3% saving gives $30m a year) is unsourced, and it is unclear whether the $1bn is new guarantees each year or a cap on exposure. 4.1 defines guarantee capacity as a cap on outstanding exposure and derives the value properly.
- **CIT numbers** differ across versions. 4.2 uses one consistent, labelled set.
- **Co-authors.** SLIDIs: Marcello Estevão and Nils Zimmermann. Acknowledge them in 4.3.
- **Guarantee loss cases (settled in the 29 Oct 2026 cross-review; headline changed by Stephen the same day).** All three papers use the same three cases, each with 20 guaranteed programs, asset correlation 0.2, a buffer sized for a 1 percent annual chance of exhaustion and held in safe assets at no net carry, and a 3-point spread saved:
  - **Base case: the MDB sovereign loss record** (GEMs 2025: sovereign and sovereign-guaranteed lending 1984–2024, default rate 0.77 percent a year, recovery 95.1 percent). Call probability 0.77 percent a year, loss per call 4.9 percent, expected loss 0.038 percent a year. Buffer 0.5 percent of exposure (two calls); $5bn supports about $1tn on paper. Incentive per donor dollar about 80 (63 with a 2 percent carry).
  - **Middle case:** 3 percent calls, 10 percent loss. Buffer 2.5 percent; $5bn supports $200bn. Ratio 10.
  - **Stress case:** 3 percent calls, 37 percent loss [@CrucesTrebesch2013]. Buffer 9.3 percent; $5bn supports $54bn. Ratio 2.7 (2.3 with a 2 percent carry).
  - Every paper says the MDB record reflects preferred-creditor status, which a climate guarantee facility might not inherit, which is why the stress case is reported throughout; and that because a held buffer costs nothing net, a facility can hold the stress-case buffer until it has its own record.
  - Headline numbers: 4.1 forest country donor cost about 40 cents per tonne ($11 stress); each $1bn a year of incentive needs $33bn of capacity, a $0.16bn buffer and about $13m a year of expected loss ($3.1bn and $370m stress); 4.2 Trust capital about $34bn after 10 years on the record with $95bn of co-investment, holding the stress buffer, levelling off near $28bn in the stress case (the earlier $57bn, computed at the base-case buffer, is retired, 31 Oct 2026); 4.3 coal reform funder cost about $10m ($286m stress).
