# Peer review of 4.3, working paper v0.1 and policy brief v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 4.3, *Codify Sustainability-Linked Sovereign Lending, Then Give It Scale*, written from a full reading before the v0.2 working paper and v1.0 brief. Every number in Sections 5 and 7 was recomputed from `2.Source/model.py`; every derivation in Appendix B was checked by hand; the Uruguay loan terms were checked line by line against the Ministry of Economy and Finance's public briefing note (December 2023), and the bond terms against the SEC prospectus supplement and the issuers' and arrangers' public releases. The references are checked separately in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`. Only public sources and the public SLIDIs paper were used; the confidential World Bank material in `9.Archive/` was not opened.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a good question (can sustainability-linked sovereign lending become a scalable incentive for national climate policy, and how big must the reward be?) and gives a clear, three-part answer: codify the instrument; pay the reward through a third party rather than through the coupon; and, because an interest rebate is bounded by the interest bill, make guarantee capacity the prize. The size argument (Proposition 3 and the coal example) is the paper's strongest and most original result, and its arithmetic is right. The Uruguay section is careful and reproduces the published caps from the amortization schedule.

It has five real problems. (1) Proposition 2 is stated so that a transfer from the funder looks like a gain from design, and it says a market bond gives no incentive when in fact it gives the same ex post incentive, paid for by the government itself. (2) The literature review misreads the pricing evidence: it says investors "price the label rather than the target", but Feldhütter, Halskov and Krebbers (2024) find prices depend strongly on the size of the penalty and find no mispricing; only Kölbel and Lambillon (2023) find the premium unrelated to the step-up. The two disagree and the paper must say so. (3) The contribution claimed for Proposition 2 ignores the closest prior work, Lefournier (2023), which shows that a sustainability-linked bond is priced at the issuer's ordinary yield and that its parameters cannot lower the issuer's cost of capital. (4) The market facts are out of date: Slovenia issued the first European sovereign sustainability-linked bond in June 2025 with ±50 basis point steps, Thailand's bond (November 2024, not "2024" only) was reopened to more than Bt230 billion and followed by a second in September 2026, and the Uruguay bond's step is not a symmetric ±15 basis points. (5) The headline leverage of about 80 combines a market spread (300 basis points saved) with development banks' preferred-creditor loss record; that is the strongest objection to the paper and it is stated only in passing.

The brief, at about 3,000 words with a sectioned executive summary, italic lead-ins and no bold first sentences, is a shorter working paper rather than a brief. The deck spec is in the old three-slide format.

## Major points

**M1. Proposition 2 conflates a transfer with an efficiency gain, and misdescribes the market bond's incentive.** Part (i) says that under a market sustainability-linked bond the government's expected net transfer is $-\theta\sigma$, which is right, and the text then reads this as "a market step-down is a bet a government places on itself" that gives no incentive. But once the bond is issued, the government that adopts the policy raises its chance of the step-down from $q_0$ to $q_1$ and saves $\Delta(q_1-q_0)$ a year: the market bond *does* give the ex post incentive. What it does not give is money: investors anticipate the outcome and charge for it in the issue yield, so the government pays for its own reward. The bond is a self-financed commitment device. That matters for the argument. A commitment device is useful when the government's problem is credibility (it wants to reform but cannot persuade investors or its successors that it will); it is useless when the problem is that the reform costs the country more than it gains, which is the problem of mitigation, whose benefits are global. Part (iii) then says the government is better off under a third-party reward by $q\Delta + \theta\sigma$. Of that, $q\Delta$ is a transfer from the funder, not a gain from design; only $(\theta - \theta_I)\sigma$ is a saving to society. *Fix:* restate Proposition 2 in three parts: (a) both designs give the same ex post incentive $\Delta(q_1-q_0)$; (b) under the market bond the government finances it and pays $\theta\sigma$ for the volatility, so its expected net transfer is $-\theta\sigma$; under a third-party reward it receives $q\Delta$, paid by the funder; (c) the social saving from moving KPI risk to the intermediary is $(\theta-\theta_I)\sigma$. Then draw the right conclusion: a market sustainability-linked bond can buy credibility but cannot pay for a reform whose benefits accrue abroad; only an outside funder can.

**M2. The pricing evidence is misread.** The introduction says "investors price the label rather than the target" and Section 4.3 builds on it. Feldhütter, Halskov and Krebbers (2024, read in full) find a label premium of 1–2 basis points, that "prices of SLBs depend strongly on the size of the potential penalty", and "no evidence of mispricing"; their average probability of missing a target is 14–39 percent. Kölbel and Lambillon (2023), using issue yields rather than same-day secondary prices, find a premium of about 9 basis points that does not vary with the step-up and exceeds the expected penalty. Feldhütter et al. say explicitly that their results differ from Kölbel and Lambillon's. *Fix:* report both, say they disagree, and note that Proposition 2 needs only the Feldhütter result (the step is priced in); if Kölbel and Lambillon are right, the label, which rewards issuance rather than results, is what issuers gain.

**M3. Corollary 1 misuses the evidence on missed targets.** It cites the 14–39 percent probability of missing as evidence that $q_0$ is "often high". That probability is measured under whatever the firm actually does; it is $1-q_1$ if firms act, or $1-q_0$ only if they do nothing. It shows targets are easy, which is consistent with a high $q_0$, but does not measure it. *Fix:* say so.

**M4. The contribution is not stated against the closest prior work.** (a) Lefournier (2023) shows that a sustainability-linked bond and a vanilla bond from the same issuer are priced at the same issuer yield and that no choice of step size, step period or trigger probability can lower the issuer's cost of capital materially: a "design flaw" that leaves issuers and investors in a no-win position. That is Proposition 2(i) in words. (b) The SLIDIs paper already proposed the fixed-coupon, third-party-reward design. (c) Results-based aid has a long literature: Svensson (2003) on why conditional aid fails (donors cannot commit to withhold it) and on committing aid to a group of countries and allocating it ex post by performance; Birdsall and Savedoff (2010) on paying governments cash on delivery of measured outcomes. (d) The World Bank Group's Evolution Roadmap (December 2022, public) already proposes "standardized approaches to providing concessionality through mechanisms like interest buy-downs and performance-based incentives", and the Bank's Framework for Financial Incentives (approved April 2024) offers price incentives for projects with cross-border benefits. (e) The World Bank's own sovereign KPI framework (Flugge, Mok and Stewart 2021) is the closest work on indicator choice. *Fix:* the contribution is (i) the formal statement that a market step is a self-financed commitment device, with the condition under which a third-party reward is cheaper; (ii) the interest bound and its size, with the Uruguay reconstruction and the coal example; (iii) the leverage of guarantee capacity as the reward, with the series' three loss cases; (iv) a concrete codified menu and pricing rule in carbon-price-equivalent terms. Name the prior work for each.

**M5. The strongest objection must be stated in full: the base case mixes a market spread with a preferred-creditor loss record.** The 300 basis point saving is a market spread on a sub-investment-grade sovereign's borrowing. The base-case expected loss of 0.038 percent a year is development banks' record on their own sovereign loans, which reflects preferred-creditor treatment. A guarantee of market debt is a claim of the same kind the market prices at 300 basis points; if the guarantor's claim ends up treated like a market claim, its losses look like the stress case (3 percent calls, 37 percent loss, expected loss 1.1 percent a year), and the ratio of spread to expected loss, 2.7, is about what the evidence on spreads implies for high-yield debt (Huang and Huang 2012: credit risk is a small share of investment-grade spreads and a much larger share of high-yield spreads). The base case is therefore the case in which the guarantor's claim is honored like a development bank loan, a counter-indemnity that keeps preferred-creditor status. The paper must say this in Section 7.1, in the executive summary and in the brief, and the stress case must be stated wherever 80 is. The series has settled the three cases (4.0 cross-review, 29 Oct 2026), so the numbers stay; the framing changes. A further check: a 300 basis point saving is at the top of what guarantees are reported to deliver (the World Bank's reported expectation for Kenya's planned guaranteed sustainability-linked loan in 2026 is 200 basis points). Report the 200 basis point case: capacity needed rises to \$5.0 billion, funder cost \$15 million (base) and \$429 million (stress), ratio 53 and 1.8.

**M6. Market facts need correcting and updating.** Checked against the sources:
- *Uruguay bond.* Not "±15 bp per KPI". Each KPI has two targets: the coupon rises 15 basis points if the lower target is missed and falls 15 if the higher is met, from 28 October 2027 (prospectus supplement, 2023). \$2.2 billion was outstanding after the November 2023 reopening, repaid in three instalments 2032–2034. The maximum step-down is worth about \$40 million nominal (\$27 million in present value), more than the World Bank loan's \$12.5 million, but it is priced in at issue (M1). This comparison sharpens the paper's point and should be added.
- *Chile.* \$2 billion, 4.34 percent, 2042; two step-ups of 12.5 basis points, applying from March 2034. Add the date.
- *Thailand.* Bt30 billion (about \$874 million), 15.5 years, 2.70 percent, priced in November 2024, ±2.5 basis points; KPIs a 30 percent cut in emissions against business as usual by 2030 and 440,000 zero-emission vehicle registrations a year by 2030. Reopened to more than Bt230 billion; a second bond (Bt25 billion, September 2026) adds a biodiversity KPI.
- *Slovenia.* €1 billion, 3.125 percent, 2035, priced 24 June 2025, the first European sovereign sustainability-linked bond, with steps of +50 or −50 basis points against 2030 emission targets. The paper's "steps of 2.5 to 15 basis points" must become "2.5 to 50".
- *Who pays Uruguay's reward.* The World Bank's factsheet (November 2023) says it "will come from the Fund for Innovative Global Public Goods Solutions". The briefing note the paper cites does not say so directly; cite the factsheet.
- *IBRD terms.* The briefing note gives the standard alternative as SOFR plus a variable spread of 109 basis points. The 100 basis point step-down nearly removes the spread: a fact worth stating, because it shows the step is large relative to the Bank's margin and still small relative to policy costs.

**M7. Sources that cannot be cited.** Estevão's note on bonds with KPIs is unpublished and undated, and the archive copy is marked "for discussion". Under the series rule, draft-for-discussion documents are not cited, and a reader could not find it anyway. Remove it from the bibliography; keep the acknowledgement in the first-page note; state "Model 2" (a KPI as a precondition for a further concessional loan) as a design option in the author's voice. The claim that "standard weights for policy changes, analogous to the standardized risk weights of bank capital rules" comes from *Time and Chance* is not supported: the manuscript has no such passage. State it as the author's proposal.

**M8. A non-technical executive summary is missing**, and the paper has no section identifiers, manual section numbers and manual table numbers. Add the summary as chapter 1, turn on `number-sections`, give every section an id, and use cross-references.

## Checks of each result

- *Proposition 1.* Correct. Corollary 1 values (1.0, 1.5, 3.0 at $q_0$ = 0, 0.3, 0.6 with $q_1$ = 0.9) reproduce. Corollary 2: $(1.10/1.05)^9 = 1.52$; correct. The midpoint of the performance period, 2032.5, is 9.5 years after approval, so "about nine" is fine.
- *Proposition 2.* Algebra correct given its definitions; the interpretation is not (M1). The proof should also state that investors know the government's choice (rational expectations), which is what makes the step priced in.
- *Proposition 3.* Correct. The "no plausible loan meets it" claim in the introduction contradicts the paper's own figure: a \$1.7 billion loan with every dollar of interest waived would meet it. What is implausible is a step-down at sizes in use: at 100 basis points the loan must be \$10 billion, nearly thirty times Uruguay's. Re-word.
- *Proposition 4.* Correct. Thresholds reproduce: $b\,r_b$ < 296 basis points (base), 270 (middle), 189 (stress). Section 7.1 still says a guarantee "looks nearly three times cheaper than cash", a remnant of the earlier base case; replace.
- *Table 2 (Uruguay).* Reproduces: \$6.8 million and \$6.1 million against published caps of \$6.7 million and \$5.8 million; total 12.9 against the 12.5 cap; present value \$8.4 million at 5 percent to 2023. The note explains the gap correctly: the briefing note applies each year's step to the next two semi-annual payments, on lower balances. The cap is 3.6 percent of principal (12.5/350); the model prints 3.7 percent because it uses the uncapped 12.9. Use 3.6 and say it is the cap.
- *Table 3 and Figure 1.* All numbers reproduce: \$77 million, \$463 million, \$880 million, \$3.3 billion of capacity worth \$772 million, funder cost \$10 million (base), \$77 million (middle), \$286 million (stress); buffers \$16 million and \$310 million; ratios 80, 10, 2.7, and 63 and 2.3 with a 2 percent carry.
- *Coal reform.* 2 GW × 6,000 h × 0.95 t/MWh = 11.4 MtCO₂ a year; \$100 million a year for ten years is \$772 million at 5 percent, \$8.8 per tonne. Correct.

## Minor points

1. The introduction's roadmap paragraph and the "This section asks three questions" sentences are announcing; cut.
2. "Four sovereign deals define the instrument" is now six (Chile, Uruguay bond, Uruguay loan, Thailand ×2, Slovenia); restructure Table 1 to the five instruments with distinct designs.
3. The paper discusses loans but cites only the bond principles; the Loan Market Association, APLMA and LSTA's Sustainability-Linked Loan Principles (March 2025) require post-signing external verification of every target, which supports Section 3.3.
4. Section 2.2: cumulative issuance is \$226.6 billion at the end of the first quarter of 2023 (World Bank blog, citing the Climate Bonds Initiative); the Climate Bonds Initiative's 24.8 basis point average and 58 percent share are for the market to November 2023. Say so.
5. Section 3.4 cites "Estevão's Model 2" (M7).
6. The brief's executive summary uses italic run-in heads; the brief needs Key messages with bold first sentences.
7. `model.py` says the loss cases are shared with 3.1 and 3.2; they are shared with 4.1 and 4.2. `make_figures.py` calls itself "Brief 3.3". Fix both.
8. The book key `StrettonTimeAndChance` differs from the series key `StrettonTC` (cross-review open item 5). Use `StrettonTC`.
9. "The most Uruguay can save is \$12.5 million over fifteen years": the reward is paid over ten performance years (2028–2037), plus one buffer year.
10. Kenya's planned guaranteed sustainability-linked loan (reported July 2026: \$500 million first tranche, a World Bank credit enhancement, KPIs on forest loss and rural electricity access) is the first public sign of the combination this paper proposes; mention it as reported, not as done.

## The brief

Rewrite as a brief of about 2,000 words: Key messages first, bold first sentences, sections whose headings state claims, no equations, one table (the size comparison, with the same numbers as the working paper's Table 3) and one figure, links into the working paper's sections, "What to do" and the related papers. It should carry the corrected Proposition 2 message in plain words (a market bond buys credibility, not money) and the stress case wherever 80 appears.

## The deck

Rewrite `slides.json` in the current format, about ten slides: title; the instrument exists (deal table); it is small (stats); why it does not scale; codify (indicator menu); separate the investor from the incentive; the size problem (figure); guarantee capacity as the reward (table of three cases); the objection and the safeguards; what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.2 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). M5 keeps the series' three loss cases, as settled in the 4.0 cross-review, and changes their framing: the base case is now described as the case in which the guarantor's claim keeps preferred-creditor treatment, the stress case as the market-consistent case, and the 200 basis point sensitivity is reported. Kenya's planned facility is mentioned as reported in the press, with its terms not yet confirmed by a World Bank document.
