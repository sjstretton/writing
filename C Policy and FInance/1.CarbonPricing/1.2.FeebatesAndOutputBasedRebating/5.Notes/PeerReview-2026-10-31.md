# Peer review of 1.2, working paper v0.3 and policy brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 1.2 before the v0.4 working paper and v1.1 brief. The earlier review (`PeerReview-and-WPOutline-2026-10-29.md`) was of the brief alone and set the outline that v0.3 follows; most of its points are fixed in v0.3. This review is of what is there now. Every number in the working paper was recomputed by running `2.Source/make_wp_figures.py` and `2.Source/check_propositions.py` in a scratch copy; all reproduce.

## Summary judgement

The working paper asks a clear question (when does a revenue-neutral feebate keep power cheap, and what does it do to dispatch, investment and dominant utilities?) and gives a clear, correct answer: the same merit order and investment incentive as a tax, a tariff that barely moves under a single buyer that nets the pool, and a wholesale-price rise of $t(e_m - \bar e)$ in a uniform-price market. The propositions check symbolically and numerically, and the calibration to India, Indonesia and South Africa is reproducible from Ember data. It is close to the paper standard. The problems are: one proposition that needs a condition it does not state; a contribution claimed as new that Fischer (2011) has; closest prior work on the market-design result not cited; an unresolved tension with the consumption track over household electricity prices; two items the companion paper 1.1 cites 1.2 for but 1.2 does not contain; a Canadian price path that changed in May 2026; a handful of misquoted numbers; one `[VERIFY]` left in the text; and no executive summary. The brief is accurate in substance but breaks the series format: it has equations, no bold lead sentences, an executive summary instead of key messages, and a stylized table whose numbers (solar at \$40/MWh, an 87 percent rise) appear nowhere in the working paper.

## Major points

**M1. Proposition 1 (dispatch) needs a price-taking condition.** It says a feebate and a tax give the same merit order "whatever the level of the benchmark". That is true when each generator takes $\bar e$ as given. A firm with output share $s_i$ that internalizes its effect on the current-average benchmark sees, for one more MWh from its plant $j$, a cost of $c_j + t(1 - s_i)(e_j - \bar e)$: the carbon spread among its own plants and against rivals is scaled down by $(1 - s_i)$. That is the dispatch counterpart of Proposition 5. For Eskom ($s \approx 0.86$) the self-dispatched merit order would barely respond. *Fix:* state the condition in Proposition 1, and make the existing remedy (the system operator ranks units on $c_j + t e_j$) part of the result.

**M2. Proposition 5 (dominant firm) is close to Fischer (2011), which the paper says it did not review.** Fischer's abstract: under output-based refunding, firms with large market shares see weaker incentives to cut emissions, marginal abatement costs diverge across firms, and in an asymmetric Cournot duopoly endogenous refunding gives higher output and emissions than a fixed rebate at the same intensity. That is the $t(1 - s_i)$ result, under oligopoly. *Fix:* cite Fischer (2011) as the closest prior work; claim only what is new here: the effect of the benchmark's timing ($t(1 - \delta s_i)$), the leave-one-out repair and its fiscal cost, and the calibration to NTPC and Eskom. Remove Appendix A.7's statement that the oligopoly theory was not reviewed.

**M3. The market-design result has a precedent that should be named.** Burtraw, Palmer, Bharvirkar and Paul (2001) model three allowance allocations in the US power sector and find that a generation performance standard, which is updating output-based allocation, gives the lowest electricity price of the three. Goulder et al. (2022) find the same direction for China. What is new in Proposition 3 is the exact decomposition (the feebate is a tax plus a per-MWh consumer rebate of $t\bar e$; every generator's margin is unchanged), the price effect $t(e_m - \bar e)$, and the contrast with the single-buyer case. *Fix:* add the precedent and state the contribution against it.

**M4. The consumption track and household electricity prices are not reconciled.** The paper says electricity sold to final users bears the consumption-track charge on its embodied carbon. If that charge is at the feebate rate, the two together put the household price back where a tax would: $R + t\bar e$ under a single buyer and $t e_m$ in a market. That is 1.1's equivalence result, and it means the "keeps power cheap" case holds only if the consumption rate on electricity is lower than the production rate, or deferred. The paper needs to say this and argue for it. The argument exists: the job of a consumption charge is to make buyers use less carbon, and for electricity the relevant choice is between electricity and the fossil fuels it replaces in heating and transport, which bear their own consumption-track obligation; a low consumption rate on electricity while the grid is coal-heavy speeds electrification without leakage, because power is barely traded. *Fix:* add this to the section on why power belongs on the production track and to the political-economy section; align with 1.1's wording (net of any upstream carbon price, which under a revenue-neutral feebate is zero on average).

**M5. Two things 1.1 cites 1.2 for are missing.** 1.1 cites 1.2 for (a) Canada's output-based pricing and the EU's free-allocation benchmarks as precedents, and (b) "benchmark updating, and excluding new entrants' output from the benchmark for an initial period". 1.2 has Canada and updating, but not the EU benchmarks and not the new-entrant rule. *Fix:* add both to the benchmark section, with the analysis. The new-entrant rule has three benefits (a large entrant faces the full rate, as under leave-one-out; its early rebate is fixed by published incumbent data, which lenders need; a new coal unit cannot raise the benchmark and dilute the fee on existing coal) and one cost (while excluded, a clean entrant's rebate is not offset by a fall in the benchmark, so the scheme runs a deficit and the output subsidy is larger). The cost runs *against* 1.1's sign condition, which 1.1 says the rule answers. **For the coordinator:** 1.1 (working paper, Proposition 5 paragraph) should credit benchmark updating as the response to the sign condition, and present entrant exclusion as a separate rule for entrants' incentives and the bankability of rebates, kept short because it delays the fall in the benchmark.

**M6. The strongest objection is revenue, and it should be met head on.** At \$50 a tax on India's power sector would raise about \$70 billion a year (calibration); the feebate raises nothing. For fiscally constrained governments that is the main argument for the tax. The paper mentions revenue in passing. *Fix:* state the objection in the political-economy section with the number, and answer it: the tax at that rate is not on offer politically; the benchmark can be lowered on a published path, as Canada's standards are set below the average; and the consumption track raises revenue on what is consumed.

**M7. Canada's price path is out of date.** The paper says the OBPS excess-emissions charge rises to C\$170 in 2030. On 15 May 2026 the federal government revised the headline industrial price to C\$95 in 2026, C\$100 in 2027, C\$115 in 2030 and C\$130 in 2035 (ICAP, 19 May 2026). *Fix:* give the revised path, and note that Canada lowered it after the consumer charge went: evidence that the industrial system survives politically, but also that its rate is negotiable.

**M8. No executive summary, and the brief is not in series format.** Add a non-technical executive summary as chapter 1. Rewrite the brief: key messages, bold lead sentences, no equations, the working paper's calibrated numbers (country cases) instead of the separate stylized grid, links into the working paper's sections.

## Minor points

1. **Unresolved `[VERIFY]`** on market structure in India and Indonesia. CERC's 2023–24 market monitoring report puts short-term transactions at about 12.5 percent of generation; the rest is under long-term contracts. Indonesia's state utility PLN buys from independent producers, and the ADB's restructuring road map moves toward a single buyer, not a market. Replace the marker with these.
2. **Gugler et al. (2023)** report a cut of 38.6 MtCO₂, not 36.3. The bib note for this entry also holds Hintermann's pass-through finding by mistake.
3. **Goulder et al. (2022)**: the 18 percent figure is "private cost per ton of reduced emissions" for a three-benchmark against a one-benchmark standard (NBER WP 26537, p. 3). Say "private cost per tonne".
4. **The brief's claim that "the cheapest early retirements are in electricity and industry"** (Tong et al. 2019) is not in the abstract. Cut it.
5. **Sweden's NOx charge**: the brief says "in six years", 1.1 says "seven years". Both rest on mean emission rates 1992–98. Say "between 1992 and 1998".
6. **The brief says the market effect is \$8–18; the working paper \$7–18** (South Africa \$7.5). Use \$7.5–18 or \$7–18 consistently.
7. **The fiscal true-up**: "\$2–4 billion a year" should be \$2.4–4.4 billion (script).
8. **Related literature says "five literatures"** and lists seven. Fix.
9. **Pizer and Zhang (2018)** say the system "will more than double" covered emissions (confirmed); keep. Goulder et al. are the source for its being a tradable performance standard; attribute accordingly.
10. **Key `OBPSRegs` and `ECCC_OBPS`, `Black2022`, `BohringerRosendahlStorrosten2017`** differ from 1.1's keys for the same sources. Use 1.1's keys (`CanadaOBPS2019`, `black2022framework`, `BoehringerRosendahlStorrosten2017`) so the series bibliographies merge.
11. **The working paper's first line** cites the brief as a reference; make it a link (`brief:`). The brief's first line must link to the working paper.
12. **Appendix subsections** have no section identifiers; add them so the brief can link.
13. **The IMF's Mexico proposal** (Black et al. 2021) is the closest policy precedent for an industrial feebate and is cited in 1.1 but not in 1.2. Add it.
14. **The EU's free-allocation phase-out** for CBAM sectors (2026–2034 under Directive 2023/959; a July 2026 proposal would extend it to 2038) is the reason a feebate matters for EU exporters' competitors; add a sentence in the industry section.

Rewrite to the series format (about 2,000 words): key messages; claim-stating section headings; bold lead sentences; no equations; one table and one figure with the working paper's calibrated numbers (India, Indonesia, South Africa at \$50/tCO₂); the market-design qualification up front, since it is the paper's main result; the household-price point (M4) in plain words; links to the working paper's sections and to Briefs 1.1, 1.3, 1.4 and 6.1.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.4 and brief v1.1 (31 Oct 2026): Proposition 1 carries the price-taking condition; Proposition 5 is stated against Fischer (2011); Burtraw et al. (2001) is cited for Proposition 3; a new section explains why power belongs on the production track, including the household-price point (M4); the benchmark section adds EU precedents and the new-entrant rule with its cost; the revenue objection is stated and answered; Canada's 2026 price path is updated; the `[VERIFY]` is resolved; numbers corrected. One item is for the coordinator, not this folder: the sentence on new entrants in 1.1's Proposition 5 paragraph (M5).
