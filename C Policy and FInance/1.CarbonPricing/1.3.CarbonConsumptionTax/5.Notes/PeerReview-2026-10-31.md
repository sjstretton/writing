# Peer review of 1.3, working paper v0.2 and policy brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 1.3 before the v0.3 working paper and v1.1 brief. The earlier review (`PeerReview-and-WPOutline-2026-10-29.md`) was of brief v1.0 and set the plan for working paper v0.2; most of its points are met in v0.2. This review is of what is there now. Every number in the working paper was recomputed from the scripts in `2.Source/` (`make_wp_figures.py` reruns from the saved CSVs and reproduces every figure and fiscal number; the input–output calibration itself needs a 72 MB download that was not repeated) or by hand.

## Summary judgement

The working paper asks how the EU could price the carbon in what it consumes, wherever that carbon is emitted, using institutions it already has. Its answer is a carbon component inside VAT, credited along the invoice chain like VAT itself, with rebuttable product-category defaults and a value component that falls by a neutrality rule, plus a forward-announced obligation on the few suppliers of fuel for homes and vehicles. The arithmetic is right, the input–output calibration is careful and validated against Eurostat's own footprint, and the legal analysis is candid. Three things stop it from meeting the standard. The novelty claim is wrong: a carbon tax run through the VAT credit chain with default footprints per product class has been proposed and analyzed before, and the paper does not cite that work. A real gap in the mechanism: the paper never says how a verified low figure upstream reaches the consumer price, and by its own Proposition 1(i) it does not unless downstream firms can carry the verified base forward. And the brief is out of date against the working paper: it still presents the stylized fixed path, an unsourced country illustration and three `[VERIFY]` markers, and contradicts the working paper on electricity.

## Major points

**M1. The closest prior work is missing, and the contribution is overstated against it.** "What is new" says the literature "does not have a carbon charge run through the VAT chain with input crediting". It does. McAusland and Najjar (2015, *Environmental and Resource Economics* 61: 37–70) propose a carbon footprint tax modeled on a credit-method VAT, find a pure version (a footprint for every product) prohibitively costly, and propose a hybrid in which firms either compute their own footprints or use default footprints per product class; that is this paper's design. Courchene and Allan proposed a destination-based "carbon-added tax" in *Policy Options* (2008), and McLure (2010, *Carbon & Climate Law Review* 4: 250–259) argued against it on compliance cost and border-adjustment grounds, preferring an upstream tax with border adjustment for a few basic products. McLure is the strongest objection to the whole proposal and must be stated and answered. *Fix:* cite McAusland and Najjar as the closest precedent and McLure as the main objection; restate the contribution as (i) the formal chain results, including the exempt-stage cascade and the one-price-per-tonne deduction that links the consumption track to the ETS, CBAM and a feebate; (ii) calibrated defaults for the EU household basket; (iii) the swap inside the existing VAT with a neutrality rule; (iv) the forward obligation for fuel that never enters a goods chain; (v) the EU legal map. Add an "Objections and limits" section that answers McLure.

**M2. The mechanism by which rebuttal reaches the consumer is not specified, and as written it does not work.** Proposition 1(i) shows that only the base at the final sale fixes what the consumer pays. If the retailer charges the retail default on the retail value, an importer that verifies a low intensity lowers only its own remittance; the retailer remits more, and the consumer price is unchanged. The paper's phone example and its "Incentives" paragraph claim that rebuttal "lowers its customers' tax and gains market share", which holds only if every downstream firm can compute its own base as its supplier's verified base plus its own carbon added. *Fix:* state a carry-forward rule (a firm may set its base equal to the base on its purchase invoices plus its own carbon added, verified or at a published default per euro of value added for its sector), state that this is what makes rebuttal reach the consumer, and note the cost: under carry-forward the final-sale default no longer polices understatement upstream, so verified bases need audit (the paper already says so; the two statements now connect).

**M3. The import comparison mixes composition and intensity.** "Imports embody about twice as much carbon per euro as EU-made goods" compares 0.76 kg per euro of extra-EU purchases, which are mostly manufactured goods, with 0.38 kg per euro of EU-made purchases, which include low-carbon taxable services (trade, hospitality, communication). Recomputed from `2.Source/defaults_by_category.csv`: the EU-made versions of the same mix of products that the EU imports embody 0.45 kg per euro, so like for like imports carry about 1.7 times as much carbon per euro; the category ratios run from 1.3 (chemicals) to 3.0 (furniture). *Fix:* report 0.76 against 0.45 and "about 1.7 times like for like" in the abstract, introduction, defaults section and brief; keep the 0.76 and 0.38 figures in the table with their meaning. The computation is added to `make_wp_figures.py`.

**M4. The brief contradicts the working paper.** It presents the stylized fixed path (value component 15 to 10 percent, break-even 0.25 kg per euro) as the proposal, with two stylized tables, while the working paper shows that path can lose or gain over €100 billion a year and replaces it with the neutrality rule. It keeps the "country-level rates" illustration (EU 0.20 to 0.15, China 0.40 to 0.25 kg per euro) that the working paper's Appendix D shows rests on unsourced intensities. It says electricity suppliers "can carry the same obligation", while the working paper (and paper 1.1) say electricity carries its generation carbon through the VAT carbon component and the obligation is extended to electricity retailers only where no carbon component exists. It cites Steininger et al. (2014) for consumption-based approaches being "both fairer and more cost-effective", which the source does not establish (the research program behind it concludes the ranking depends on coalition size, trade parameters and instrument design), and it carries three `[VERIFY]` markers. *Fix:* rewrite the brief to the series format (Key messages, bold lead sentences, about 2,000 words) with the working paper's numbers: the 2022 basket at 0.41 kg CO₂e per euro, the neutrality rule ending at 10.8–12.7 percent, the central-case total rates (13.8 percent at 0.10 kg per euro, 27.8 percent at 0.80), and the distribution table.

**M5. Two `[VERIFY]` items in the working paper and several unverified legal facts.** The ETS2 start date and price trigger, and the national standard rates, are marked `[VERIFY]`; the CBAM legal basis, the Court of Justice cases on Article 401, the Financial Transaction Tax decision and the Social Climate Fund regulation were written from memory. All are checkable and are now checked (see `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`). Two findings change the text. ETS2 was postponed to 1 January 2028 by the 2026 amendment of the European Climate Law, Regulation (EU) 2026/667. And Poland's action to annul the CBAM Regulation (Case C-512/23), on the ground that it is "primarily fiscal" and needed unanimity under Article 192(2)(a), was still pending in late 2026; the legal section must say so, because it bears directly on whether the forward obligation can be adopted by qualified majority.

## Derivations and numbers checked

- Propositions 1–3 and their proofs: correct. In Proposition 2(i) the exempt final seller's value added and carbon added both go untaxed, as stated; in 2(ii) the excess is $(1+v)(v\,p_{j-1} + P\,E_{j-1})$, as stated.
- Proposition 3 needs one more condition when bases are defaults rather than verified: if a default base understates embodied carbon in an ETS-heavy chain, $P\,C_k - Q_k$ can be negative and the deduction would draw a refund. Cap the deduction at the carbon component due, $\min(Q_k, P\,C_k)$.
- Worked example (mill, appliance maker, retailer): value component €25 + €37.5 + €37.5 = €100; carbon component €8 + €10 + €2 = €20; with €32 of ETS, 520 kg bear €52. Correct.
- Break-even 0.25 kg per euro on the fixed path ($P e/10$ points; 200 × 0.25/10 = 5 = 10 × 0.5). Correct.
- Fiscal table: every cell reproduced by `make_wp_figures.py` (for example 0.15 kg per euro: 3.00 points gross, 2.66 net, value component 12.34 percent, −2.34 points, −€120 billion).
- Neutrality rule: value component 13.20/13.51/13.77 percent in 2032 and 10.76/11.83/12.65 percent in 2037 for intensity falling 3/5/7 percent a year; break-even 0.158 kg per euro in 2037 in the central case; totals 13.8 and 27.8 percent at 0.10 and 0.80 kg per euro. Correct.
- 40 percent of the taxable basket's footprint emitted abroad: 851/2,116 Mt = 40.2 percent. Correct, but the abstract states it of "the carbon in what Europeans buy", which is wider than the taxable basket. Narrow it.
- Forward obligation: 706 Mt × €200 = €141 billion = 1.7 percent of €8.2 trillion; €316 per resident (446 million); gas 56,100 kg/TJ = 0.202 t/MWh; diesel 74,100 kg/TJ × 43.0 TJ/Gg × 0.84 kg/l = 2.68 kg/l. Correct. But "a household that burns 12 MWh … would pay €480, more than a person's equal share" compares a household with a person; with about 2.3 people per EU household the household's share would exceed its bill. Restate for a one-person household.
- Social cost of carbon: Rennert et al. give \$185 per tonne in 2020 dollars at a 2 percent near-term discount rate; add the price year.
- Distribution table and text agree with `2.Source/distribution_quintiles.csv`.

## The strongest objection

McLure's: an economy-wide credit-method carbon tax needs a carbon footprint on every invoice, at a compliance and administrative cost out of proportion to the gain over an upstream tax with border adjustments for a few basic materials. The answer the paper can give is in its own design but is not drawn together: published defaults by customs category mean that most firms apply a number as they apply a VAT rate, so the footprint calculation is done once by the Commission, not by every firm; the upstream alternative cannot reach the 40 percent of the basket's carbon emitted abroad except through border adjustments covering every imported product, which need the same default footprints; and the paper's own sequencing (obligation first, a materials charge next, the VAT component last) concedes McLure's point for the short run. Say so.

## Minor points

1. Body references to "v0.1" ("the illustrative path of the brief and of v0.1", "v0.1's national scores", "the claim of v0.1", "v0.1 proposed …") are to a draft the reader has not seen. Move them to the appendix on corrections and keep the body self-contained.
2. The first line says "A short version is Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 1.3"; make it a link (`brief:`), as in 1.1. Add a non-technical executive summary as chapter 1.
3. Remove the duplicate keywords/JEL line in the body; the YAML carries them.
4. Use the series' shared terms: "consumption track", "destination basis", "embodied carbon", "forward-announced obligation"; say once that the default for domestic goods is the sector (product-category) average, as 1.1 states.
5. Make the electricity case explicit in one paragraph: electricity is a taxable supply, so the carbon component carries generation carbon to the final user, net of the production-track price paid; under a revenue-neutral feebate generators pay nothing net on average, so on average nothing is deducted (1.1's wording).
6. Trade law: "an imported product can pay less than the default, never more than a like domestic product" is not quite right if domestic producers can also rebut below the default; the point is that every seller, domestic or foreign, has the same right of rebuttal.
7. Article 401 has a second condition the paper omits: the national tax must not give rise, in trade between member states, to formalities connected with crossing a frontier. A national carbon charge on intra-EU purchases would have to be collected through the buyer's return, as VAT on intra-Community acquisitions is.
8. Headings "Settled: …" overstate the first two legal points while Case C-512/23 is pending; call them the stronger reading.
9. Appendix A.8 keeps a sentence saying a source "could not be checked" and cites works only to say they do not support v0.1. Cut. D'Haultfœuille et al. (2014) find the French bonus-malus raised total emissions; say so, as 1.1 does.
10. Pollitt, Neuhoff and Lin (2020): use the verified finding, "up to 10 percent of EU CO₂ by 2050, with the charge rising to about €80 per tonne by 2050".
11. Keen and Lockwood (2010) document VAT's spread, not the destination principle; cite OECD (2017) for the latter.
12. Seven summations written as `\sum_{j \le k}` do not convert to Word equations under pandoc; write them with explicit limits.
13. Appendices have no section identifiers; add them so the brief can link to them.

## What the brief and the deck need

The brief needs the series format (Key messages; claim headings; bold first sentences; no equations; one table and one figure with the working paper's numbers; links into the working paper's sections; "What to do"; "Related papers in this series") and the working paper's numbers throughout (M4). The deck has three content slides built for the old four-slide format; it needs about ten slides: title; the problem in numbers (40 percent abroad, 1.7 times, six CBAM sectors); how the carbon component works in the chain (worked example); default intensities (figure); the neutrality rule (figure); distribution (table); the forward obligation; electricity and the link to the production track; the legal routes; what to do.

## Disposition

All five major points and the thirteen minor points are addressed in working paper v0.3 and brief v1.1 (31 Oct 2026). M1: McAusland and Najjar (2015), Courchene and Allan (2008, via their 2011 reply) and McLure (2010) added to the introduction, literature review and Appendix A; contributions restated; new section "Objections and limits" answers McLure. M2: carry-forward rule added to the chain section, with the audit consequence. M3: like-for-like comparison (0.76 against 0.45 kg per euro, about 1.7 times) added to the abstract, introduction, defaults section, brief and deck, and to `make_wp_figures.py`. M4: brief rewritten from the working paper's numbers. M5: every `[VERIFY]` resolved; ETS2 date and C-512/23 added. One item remains outside the paper: the Courchene and Allan (2008) article itself could not be retrieved, so it is cited through their 2011 reply, which names it.
