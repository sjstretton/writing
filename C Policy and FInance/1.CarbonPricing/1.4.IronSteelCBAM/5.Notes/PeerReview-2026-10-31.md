# Peer review of 1.4, working paper v0.3 and policy brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 1.4, "Credit the Price Signal, Not the Payment", written from a full reading before the v0.4 working paper and v1.1 brief. The earlier review (`PeerReview-and-WPOutline-2026-10-29.md`) was of the brief and the v0.2 consultation submission; its points are largely met in v0.3. This review is of what is there now. Every derivation was re-checked by hand and with `2.Source/check_propositions.py`, and every number in the calibration was recomputed from the default values in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, re-read from the Official Journal text on 31 Oct 2026.

## Summary judgement

The working paper asks a clear question (how should the EU's border mechanism measure embodied carbon and foreign carbon prices so that it rewards decarbonization rather than routing?) and gives a clear answer (measure both by exporting country and sector, and credit the foreign price signal alongside the price paid). The shuffling model, the liability formula and the calibration to the EU default values are correct in their algebra, and the legal account of Article 9 is accurate against the text. The paper is close to the standard. Its real problems are one proposition that confuses the country's incentive with the plant's (Proposition 3, and the options table built on it), a missing condition that the paper's own companion (1.1) states, two closest-prior-work papers it does not engage, a misattributed pair of route intensities, a calibration column that mixes two effects, and a phase-in timetable that the July 2026 Commission proposal may overtake. The brief is accurate in substance but is not yet a brief: about 3,000 words, an "Executive summary" in place of Key messages, an equation, headings that name topics rather than state claims, and one wrong number.

## Major points

**M1. Proposition 3 states the country's incentive as if it were each plant's.** Under country-sector measurement the CBAM bill is $\tau\bar e Q_{EU}$. The text says a plant that cuts one tonne per tonne of output "lowers the bill by $\tau\mu$ per tonne of that plant's output", and the options table lists "$\tau\mu$ on all output" as the plant-level incentive. That is the derivative of the *country's* bill. A single firm's own bill is $\tau\bar e\,Q_{EU,f}$, so its private reward for cutting its intensity is $\tau Q_{EU,f}/Q$ per tonne of its output: the EU share of the whole sector's output that this firm itself sells. For a firm with a tenth of the sector and a tenth of its output going to the EU, that is $\tau/100$. The rest of the reward goes to the country's other exporters. So country-sector measurement without a domestic instrument leaves plants with almost no incentive at all, which is what 1.1 says ("No single firm can move its country's value, so firms do not respond to the charge on their own; the country's policy does", 1.1, Proposition 7) and what Neuhoff et al. (2026) concede for their standardized values ("would not create direct incentives for climate-friendly material production ... in third countries"). The correction *strengthens* the paper's case: the domestic feebate is not a supplement to a diluted plant incentive but its only source. *Fix:* restate Proposition 3 with both the country's incentive ($\tau\mu$) and the firm's ($\tau Q_{EU,f}/Q$, close to zero); correct the options table, the stylized "€8 per tonne" sentence and the brief; add the check to `check_propositions.py`.

**M2. The country-sector measure carries an incentive only if it is updated from verified sector data.** Proposition 3's country incentive, and Proposition 5(ii)'s best response, require that the value charged fall when the sector's intensity falls. The Commission's default values do not: they are fixed in an implementing act and revised "in 2027 at the latest" (Implementing Regulation 2025/2621, Art. 2). 1.1 states this condition explicitly ("a default fixed once and never revised does not"). *Fix:* define the country-sector value in the liability formula as the verified, output-weighted sector average, updated each year (with a lag), and say that a default fixed once gives no incentive at all.

**M3. The two closest prior works are missing or under-used, and the contribution is not stated against them.** (a) Mehling and Ritz (2023), already cited for shuffling, also write the border charge as a "top up" $\Delta t \times \min\{z, Z\}$ with $\Delta t = \max\{t - t_k, 0\}$, the shortfall of the foreign *explicit* carbon price, and say it "does not attempt to incorporate 'effective' carbon prices due to non-price policies" (p. 127). That is exactly the liability formula with $\rho = 0$ at plant level; the paper's formula generalizes it to the price signal and to country-sector measurement. They also propose company-level regulation against reshuffling and note the problem of state-owned enterprises (p. 129), which the paper's firm-level verification route takes up. (b) Neuhoff et al. (2026, *Energy Policy* 216) propose standardized values, independent of production process and location, plus a standardized liability per tonne of material with export relief; they say this removes shuffling but creates no direct incentives abroad. That is the strongest alternative to the paper's design and should be engaged: the paper's answer is that a country-sector value plus recognition of the domestic signal removes shuffling *and* rewards the exporting country. *Fix:* add both to the introduction's contributions, the literature section and Appendix A.

**M4. Route intensities are misattributed.** The paper says its stylized intensities (2.0 tCO₂/t for BF-BOF, 0.4 for scrap-EAF) are "rounded down from commonly cited direct intensities of about 2.2 and 0.3" [IEA 2020], marked VERIFY. The IEA *Iron and Steel Technology Roadmap* (2020, Box 1.3, p. 43), read this session, gives 1.2 and 0.04 tCO₂/t *direct* and 2.2 and 0.3 tCO₂/t *direct plus indirect* (the IEA counts power made from steel off-gases as indirect). And 0.3 is rounded *up*, not down, to 0.4. *Fix:* cite the IEA values with their boundary, state that the stylized values are chosen so that the gap between routes (1.6) is smaller than the IEA's (1.9 on the wider boundary), so the example understates the shuffling gain, and note that the EU's default for Japan, a mainly blast-furnace producer, is 2.10.

**M5. The phase-in timetable is tied to dates that may move.** The recommendation sets the recognition weight rising "from zero in 2027 to one in 2034, the years in which free allocation to EU producers phases out". Free allocation is phased out by the CBAM factor in Directive (EU) 2023/959, Art. 10a(1a) (97.5 percent in 2026 to 14 percent in 2033, none from 2034), and the Commission's proposal COM(2026) 616 of 17 July 2026, under negotiation, would stretch the phase-out to 2038. *Fix:* tie the weight to the CBAM factor itself ($\rho_t = 1 - $ CBAM factor$_t$), so that recognition follows whatever schedule the co-legislators adopt; cite the directive and the proposal in place of the CBAM Regulation for the phase-out.

**M6. The calibration's "value of recognition" mixes two effects and holds intensity fixed.** The column is the fall from the *marked-up* default to the country-sector liability under full recognition. At €40 it combines the removal of the 30 percent mark-up (€103 per tonne for India) with recognition proper ($40 \times 4.28 = €171$), and it ignores the intensity cut the feebate itself induces (24 percent at €40 in this calibration). *Fix:* rename the column ("fall in liability on opting in"), give the decomposition in the text, and state that intensity is held at the default.

**M7. Recognition facts have moved since 29 October.** The Commission has contracted a consortium led by Technopolis Group (for DG TAXUD) to develop the default carbon price methodology and estimates for 2026–2028, starting from an inventory of emissions trading systems and carbon taxes assessed against eligibility criteria. So "no methodology in principle" is now "a methodology in preparation, built for explicit prices, not yet published or consulted on". The draft implementing regulation (Ares(2026)4841230) is still in the adoption workflow on the Commission's portal at 31 October 2026; the paper's description of its Articles 6 and 8 was re-checked against the draft and is accurate. *Fix:* update the legal section, Appendix C and the brief; cite the draft directly rather than the law-firm summary (ELP 2026).

**The strongest objection, and whether it is answered.** The strongest objection is the level playing field: under full recognition an exporter with a feebate at the EU price pays no border charge while EU producers pay on all their emissions after free allocation ends, which invites relocation to the exporter wherever its intensity after abatement stays above the EU producer's. The paper states this honestly (section on the formula, and Limits) and answers it with phasing and relocation reviews, and with the consumption track of 1.1 and 1.3. That answer is adequate if phasing follows the CBAM factor (M5). A second objection, that recognizing a signal invites governments to overstate it, is answered with published measures and an independent body. Both should be stated in one place as the main objections.

## Minor points

1. Proposition 1's saving $\Delta B$ is gross of the routing cost $c$; say so.
2. "Recognition raises the feebate rate ... by half to two and a half times": the ratios are 1.5 (at $\mu = 0.5$) to 2.6 (at $\mu = 0.1$). Say "1.5 to 2.6 times".
3. The implied price of recognition, about €15 per tonne abated, uses the post-abatement average intensity ($k\bar e = 167 - 21$); at the baseline intensity it would be €17. State which.
4. The abatement calibration is taken from 1.1, where it is stated in dollars (\$50 cuts intensity by 30 percent); 1.4 uses euros. Say the parameter is carried over as a stylized ratio.
5. Article 9(4) itself, not only recital 20, contains "including on a conservative basis". Cite the article.
6. The default values are to be revised "in 2027 at the latest" (Art. 2 of 2025/2621), not "by December 2027".
7. Free allocation's phase-out is in Directive 2023/959, not in the CBAM Regulation; cite it.
8. Unread sources: Zhong and Pei (2022) and Böhringer et al. (2022) are cited as "not read". Cut them. Clausing and Wolfram (2023) are cited for a trade-share figure that could not be re-checked against the source this session; keep only what the abstract supports. Branger and Quirion's (2014) range with border adjustment is "5 to 15 percent (mean 6)" in the published abstract; the text says "−5 to 15"; correct it, and drop "export rebates", which the abstract does not name.
9. Fowlie (2009): keep only the abstract's result (a third of the reductions at more than twice the cost); Hagem, Hoel and Sterner (2020): both refunding designs tilt effort toward abatement technology rather than output; Black et al. (2023) estimate efficient prices including environmental costs, which is all the text needs.
10. New evidence: Hamidani (2026, SSRN preprint) finds no stable fall in EU flat-steel imports from origins with higher default-based CBAM exposure in January–June 2026. That is evidence on reallocation across countries, not on routing within an exporter, and it should be cited as such; the claim "no empirical study of route shuffling in steel" survives a Crossref search on 31 Oct 2026.
11. Scrap scarcity (Proposition 2) needs a source: the IEA roadmap (2020, p. 30) says scrap availability "is finite and predetermined", limited by the rate at which steel products reach the end of their life, with a cost curve for collection and sorting.
12. Aluminum: the brief says country defaults run "to 3.0 for China"; the highest is Mozambique's 3.20 (China 3.00). The secondary-route value 0.36 applies to many countries, including Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam and Ukraine.
13. `StrettonWP11` should be version 0.4 (31 Oct 2026); proposition numbers 2, 6 and 7 are unchanged there.
14. The working paper's first line should link to the brief (`brief:`); appendix headings need `{#sec-...}` ids; Appendix A repeats the literature section and can be shortened.
15. Appendix D's route-code key, marked VERIFY, is confirmed by the legend in 2025/2621: (C) carbon steel by BF/BOF, (E) by scrap/EAF, (K) primary and (L) secondary aluminum.

## What the brief and the deck need

**The brief** should become the plain form of the argument: about 2,000 words; Key messages first; headings that state claims; a bold first sentence in every paragraph; no equations (the feebate formula goes); one table of the calibrated numbers (liability, shuffling gain and the value of opting in, for the main exporters) and one figure (the shuffling model); "What to do"; links into the working paper's sections and to Briefs 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 and 6.1; the M1, M4, M5, M7 and aluminum corrections. It should cite the draft regulation directly and drop the law-firm summary.

**The deck** (now six slides) should go to about ten: the problem in numbers; how CBAM credits foreign prices; shuffling (figure); stakes across exporters (table); price paid versus price signal; the liability formula in words; what recognition does to an exporter's incentive (figure); the trade-off and the two-route design; legal path; what to do.

## Disposition

All major points (M1–M7) and minor points 1–15 are addressed in working paper v0.4 and brief v1.1 (31 Oct 2026); references are recorded in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`. The level-playing-field objection remains a limit of the design, stated as such, not a defect fixed.
