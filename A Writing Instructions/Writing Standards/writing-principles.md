# Writing Principles

These are the principles we write by. They come from four authorities, each strong on a different part of the job. Pinker explains *why* prose is hard to read. Strunk and White give the rules of the sentence. McIntyre gives the rules of lucidity for technical material. McCloskey applies all of this to economics. Two supporting sources cover structure: Minto for documents and Williams for paragraphs.

The summaries below are paraphrases, not quotations. Where a point needs checking against the original, it is marked.

## 1. Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014)

**The core idea: write in "classic style".** The writer has seen something and points it out to the reader, who is an intelligent equal. Prose is a window onto the world, not a display of the writer's caution or cleverness.

- **Show the reader the thing.** Write about the world (prices, emissions, governments), not about the literature, the models or the paper itself. "Carbon taxes raise steel prices" beats "the literature has examined the price effects of carbon taxation".
- **Beware the curse of knowledge.** This is the main cause of bad expert writing: you cannot easily imagine not knowing what you know. So you skip steps, use jargon and abbreviations, and talk in abstractions. The fix is to define terms, give examples and test drafts on a real outsider.
- **Avoid "zombie nouns".** Nominalizations (implementation, utilization, consideration) hide who did what. Turn them back into verbs with agents: "the ministry implemented the tax", not "implementation of the tax occurred".
- **Don't hedge by reflex.** Qualify a claim only where you have a real reason, and say what the reason is. Compulsive hedging ("arguably", "to some extent", "may potentially") reads as evasion.
- **Build arcs of coherence.** Readers need to know the topic and the point before they get the detail. Say where you are going, mark the logical relation between ideas (because, but, so, for example) and keep the topic in subject position across sentences.
- **Old information before new.** Begin a sentence with what the reader already knows and end with what is new. The end of a sentence is where emphasis falls.

## 2. Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style* (4th ed., 1999)

**The core idea: vigorous writing is concise.** A sentence should contain no unnecessary words and a paragraph no unnecessary sentences.

- **Omit needless words.** "The fact that", "in order to", "it is important to note that" and "there is/are" can usually go.
- **Use the active voice.** It is shorter and names the agent.
- **Put statements in positive form.** "He forgot", not "he did not remember". Say what is, not what isn't.
- **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** "It rained every day for a week", not "a period of unfavorable weather set in".
- **Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** One topic per paragraph, opening with a sentence that states it.
- **Keep related words together, and put emphatic words at the end of the sentence.**
- **Express coordinate ideas in similar form** (parallel construction).
- **Revise and rewrite.**

## 3. Michael E. McIntyre, lucidity principles (DAMTP, University of Cambridge)

**The core idea: lucid writing works with the reader's unconscious perception, not against it.** McIntyre, an atmospheric scientist, argues that readers are sensitive to every change in pattern, so a writer must control which changes the reader notices. His principles, paraphrased:

- **Less is more.** Cut everything not needed for the point. Each extra idea competes for the reader's attention.
- **Don't vary what needn't vary.** Use the same word for the same thing every time. If you switch from "carbon price" to "carbon charge" to "emissions levy", the reader will assume you mean three things. "Elegant variation" is a vice in technical writing.
- **Use organic change.** Each new idea should grow out of what came before. Make changes visible when they matter, so that a change in wording or notation signals a real change in meaning.
- **Be explicit.** Say the thing. Don't leave the reader to infer a step, a definition or the relation between two claims.

## 4. Deirdre McCloskey, *Economical Writing* (3rd ed., 2019)

**The core idea: economists should write economically, and good writing is a matter of manners toward the reader.** McCloskey is the authority on economics prose specifically.

- **Know what you want to say before you write it.** Unclear writing is usually unclear thinking. Write, then find out what you think, then rewrite.
- **Don't announce; just do.** Avoid "this paper will first discuss…" roadmaps that tell the reader what they are about to read. One short roadmap sentence in a long paper is the limit.
- **Avoid boilerplate.** No "the remainder of the paper is organized as follows" paragraphs; no tables of contents for short papers.
- **Use few acronyms.** Each one is a small tax on the reader. Spell out anything used fewer than about five times.
- **Make tables and figures self-explanatory.** A reader should understand each one without the text.
- **Avoid "this" without a noun.** Write "this tax", not "this".
- **Use the first person.** "I show" is clearer and more honest than "it is shown".
- **Write for the reader, not the referee.** Be clear first, then defend.

## 5. Supporting: structure

**Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*.** Start with the answer. Then group the supporting arguments beneath it, and each argument's evidence beneath that. This is why World Bank prose puts the claim in the first sentence of each paragraph: the document, the section and the paragraph all follow the same top-down pattern.

**Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*.** Make characters the subjects of sentences and actions their verbs. Keep the topic of a run of sentences consistent. Put new and complex information last.

**George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946).** Never use a figure of speech you are used to seeing in print. Never use a long word where a short one will do. If it is possible to cut a word out, cut it out.

## How the principles combine: the house rules

1. **Answer first.** In the document, the section and the paragraph (Minto, World Bank practice).
2. **First sentence carries the claim.** The rest of the paragraph supports it.
3. **Write about the world, not about the writing** (Pinker).
4. **One word for one thing** (McIntyre).
5. **Agents as subjects, actions as verbs** (Williams, Pinker).
6. **Cut needless words, then cut again** (Strunk and White, McIntyre).
7. **Define, then exemplify, every key term** (Pinker's curse of knowledge).
8. **Every number has a unit, a year and a source.**
9. **No boilerplate and no announcing** (McCloskey).
10. **Revise until a busy outsider gets it on one read.**

## A worked example

**Before** (typical draft):

> In the context of increasing global efforts toward decarbonization, it is important to note that the implementation of carbon pricing mechanisms has been characterized by a number of challenges, particularly with regard to competitiveness concerns in trade-exposed sectors, which may potentially limit the level of carbon prices that are politically feasible.

**After:**

> Carbon prices stay low because they hurt exporters. A country that taxes its own steel makes that steel dearer abroad and at home, while imported steel pays nothing. Governments respond by keeping the price low or exempting heavy industry, which is where most of the emissions are.

The revision states the claim first, names the actors (countries, exporters, governments), uses a concrete case (steel), drops the hedges and halves the length.
