## 1. Destination versus origin basis, VAT and destination-based business taxes

**LockwoodWhalley2010.** Lockwood and Whalley, "Carbon-Motivated Border Tax Adjustments: Old Wine in Green Bottles?", *The World Economy* 33(6) (2010) (NBER WP 14025). [text: NBER WP]

- The authors revisit the 1960s debate on the destination-basis VAT. For a uniform, broad-based tax, switching between origin and destination basis has no real effects on trade, production or consumption when trade is balanced. Only price levels change, not relative prices.
- They argue the same logic applies to carbon border tax adjustments (BTAs), and that the policy debate confuses price-level effects with relative-price effects.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2 and §3. This is the theoretical basis for the claim that "border adjustments are inherent to any destination-based tax". It also sets a limit on the argument. The *value* component of VAT is trade-neutral precisely because it is uniform. Only the *carbon* component, which varies with intensity, changes relative prices. So the incentive and leakage claims rest entirely on the carbon component.

**LockwoodDeMezaMyles1994.** Lockwood, de Meza and Myles, "When Are Origin and Destination Regimes Equivalent?", *International Tax and Public Finance* 1(1) (1994), 5–24. [metadata] VERIFY content.

- This is the formal statement of the conditions under which origin- and destination-based commodity taxes are equivalent.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. Cite it for the general equivalence result.

**KeenLockwood2010.** Keen and Lockwood, "The Value Added Tax: Its Causes and Consequences", *Journal of Development Economics* 92(2) (2010), 138–151. [metadata] VERIFY content.

- The paper is a cross-country analysis of VAT adoption and its revenue effects.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. Cite it for VAT's spread and administrative maturity. Check that it supports any claim about *why* VAT spread.

**KeenLockwood2006.** Keen and Lockwood, "Is the VAT a Money Machine?", *National Tax Journal* 59(4) (2006), 905–928. [metadata] VERIFY content.

- The paper asks whether VAT adoption raised total government revenue.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §5 (fiscal design). It is relevant to whether a carbon component would grow the tax take.

**KeenSmith2006.** Keen and Smith, "VAT Fraud and Evasion: What Do We Know and What Can Be Done?", *National Tax Journal* 59(4) (2006), 861–887. [metadata: title, volume and pages confirmed in Crossref; authors VERIFY]

- The paper reviews missing-trader and carousel fraud and the remedies for them.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §9. The brief already cites it (`KeenSmith2006`). A carbon column raises the gain from misdeclaring a product's category or intensity.

**Devereux2021.** Devereux, Auerbach, Keen, Oosterhuis, Schön and Vella, "Destination-Based Cash Flow Taxation", in *Taxing Profit in a Global Economy* (Oxford University Press, 2021), 267–334. [metadata]

- The authors argue that a business tax levied where consumers are removes the incentive to shift profits or relocate production, because the base follows the immobile consumer.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. **Correction:** v0.1 cites this chapter as "Auerbach, Devereux, Keen and Vella (2021)". Crossref lists six authors with Devereux first.

**Auerbach2017.** Auerbach, Devereux, Keen and Vella, "Destination-Based Cash Flow Taxation", Oxford University Centre for Business Taxation WP 17/01 (2017). [metadata] VERIFY: not in Crossref.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. Prefer Devereux2021 as the citation.

**Shapiro2021.** Shapiro, "The Environmental Bias of Trade Policy", *QJE* 136(2) (2021). [text: NBER WP 26845]

- Tariffs and non-tariff barriers are lower on dirty industries than on clean ones, mainly because cleaner downstream industries get more protection.
- This amounts to an implicit global subsidy to CO₂ in traded goods of \$550–800 billion a year.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1 and §4. The existing destination-side tax system, tariffs, already prices embodied carbon *negatively*. The carbon component corrects that bias rather than adding a new distortion.

## 2. Consumption-based emissions accounting

**PetersHertwich2008.** Peters and Hertwich, "CO₂ Embodied in International Trade with Implications for Global Climate Policy", *Environmental Science & Technology* 42(5) (2008), 1401–1407. [metadata]

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. It is cited in v0.1 and the brief. The bibliographic details are confirmed.

**Peters2011.** Peters, Minx, Weber and Edenhofer, "Growth in Emission Transfers via International Trade from 1990 to 2008", *PNAS* 108(21) (2011), 8903–8908. [abstract]

- Emissions from producing traded goods grew from 4.3 GtCO₂ (20 percent of the global total) in 1990 to 7.8 GtCO₂ (26 percent) in 2008.
- Net transfers from developing to developed countries rose from 0.4 to 1.6 GtCO₂, which exceeds the Kyoto Protocol's emission reductions.
- Most developed countries' consumption-based emissions grew faster than their territorial emissions.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. This sizes the unpriced consumption gap. It is a better citation than Wiedmann et al. (2015), which is about *material* footprints, not carbon.

**DavisCaldeira2010.** Davis and Caldeira, "Consumption-Based Accounting of CO₂ Emissions", *PNAS* 107(12) (2010), 5687–5692. [abstract]

- In 2004, 23 percent of global CO₂ (6.2 Gt) was embodied in international trade.
- More than 30 percent of consumption-based emissions were imported in Switzerland, Sweden, Austria, the UK and France, with net imports above 4 tCO₂ per person for many Europeans.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. This is the EU-relevant number for how much of Europeans' carbon footprint is emitted abroad. It can replace v0.1's unsourced "€2–2.5 trillion of goods imported".

**Grubb2022.** Grubb, Jordan, Hertwich, Neuhoff, Das, Bandyopadhyay, van Asselt, Sato, Wang, Pizer and Oh, "Carbon Leakage, Consumption, and Trade", *Annual Review of Environment and Resources* 47 (2022). [text: Leiden repository]

- Net emission transfers from developing to developed countries peaked around 2006 and have declined since.
- There is no evidence of policy-induced leakage so far, partly because industry is shielded from carbon costs.
- The review sets out three trade-compatible price options:
  - border adjustments;
  - a carbon-footprint consumption charge;
  - a climate excise contribution combined with ETS free allocation.
- A consumption charge on *actual* footprints would give exporters an incentive to decarbonize. But tracing embodied carbon through global value chains is administratively demanding and open to fraud. Default values by product category are a feasible shortcut, provided they apply to imports and domestic goods equally.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2 and §4. **This is the key review for 1.6.** It supports the brief's switch from national scores to product-category defaults applied equally to all origins. It also names the trade-off the brief resolves through rebuttal: defaults are WTO-safe, while actual values give exporters an incentive.

**JakobSteckelEdenhofer2014.** Jakob, Steckel and Edenhofer, "Consumption- versus Production-Based Emission Policies", *Annual Review of Resource Economics* 6 (2014), 297–318. [abstract]

- One tonne of imported embodied emissions is not one extra tonne emitted globally.
- So pricing all embodied emissions in imports at the full social cost is not optimal policy.
- Trade measures should concentrate on a few highly traded, emissions-intensive industries.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. **This challenges v0.1's framing** that a consumption carbon tax is the theoretically right instrument across the whole basket. 1.6 should rest its case on administrative tractability and leakage-neutrality, not on first-best optimality.

**Steininger2014.** Steininger, Lininger, Droege, Roser, Tomlinson and Meyer, "Justice and Cost Effectiveness of Consumption-Based versus Production-Based Approaches in the Case of Unilateral Climate Policies", *Global Environmental Change* 24 (2014), 75–87. [metadata] VERIFY content.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. The brief cites it for the claim that consumption-based approaches can be fairer and more cost-effective. Read the paper before relying on that claim.

## 3. Consumption-side carbon pricing proposals (the closest prior work)

**PollittNeuhoffLin2020.** Pollitt, Neuhoff and Lin, "The Impact of Implementing a Consumption Charge on Carbon-Intensive Materials in Europe", *Climate Policy* 20(sup1) (2020), S74–S89. [text: EconStor]

- The paper simulates, with the E3ME model, a charge on consumption of basic materials at benchmark intensity, levied "at the level of the upstream exemption" (free allocation).
- At about €80/tCO₂ the charge cuts energy CO₂ by about 6 percent and process emissions by up to 40 percent by 2050. That is about 10 percent of total EU CO₂.
- GDP rises slightly and employment falls slightly. There is no leakage.
- Basic materials account for about 25 percent of global GHG emissions.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2 and §4. This is quantitative evidence that a consumption charge at default intensities works for materials. The brief cites it for input–output-based defaults. 1.6 extends the base from materials to all VAT-able goods. Say what is gained, since most consumer-goods carbon is in materials and energy.

**Ismer2016.** Ismer, Haussner, Neuhoff and Acworth, "Inclusion of Consumption into Emissions Trading Systems: Legal Design and Practical Administration", DIW Discussion Paper 1579 (2016). [metadata; Crossref lists the SSRN version, and SSRN is blocked] VERIFY content.

- This is the legal design of the materials consumption charge as an excise-type liability that applies equally to imports.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §8. It is the closest legal blueprint. An excise on materials is legally easier than a component of VAT, which is a point against the VAT vehicle that §8 should weigh.

**Neuhoff2026.** Neuhoff, Sato, Ballesteros, Böhringer and 15 others, "Industrial Decarbonization in a Fragmented World: Carbon Pricing with Border Adjustments Using Standardized Values", *Energy Policy* 216 (2026), 115405. [text: LSE repository]

- CBAM's production-specific intensities invite *resource shuffling*: foreign producers send their cleanest output to the EU.
- CBAM also leaves value-chain and export leakage unaddressed.
- The authors propose a *standardized* liability per tonne of material, independent of process or origin, applied along the value chain with export relief, alongside benchmark free allocation.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §4. This supports the brief's choice of one default per product category for all origins as the WTO-safe start. It is also a warning that rebuttal reintroduces shuffling: an exporter can rebut for the clean line it sends to the EU. Rebuttal rules should require plant- or firm-wide intensity, not consignment-level figures.

**McAuslandNajjar2015.** McAusland and Najjar, "Carbon Footprint Taxes", *Environmental and Resource Economics* 61(1) (2015), 37–70. [metadata] VERIFY content.

- This is a formal analysis of a retail tax on carbon footprints that falls on domestic and imported goods alike.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. It is cited in v0.1 and the brief. **Correction:** DOI 10.1007/s10640-013-9749-5.

**McAusland2021.** McAusland, "Carbon Taxes and Footprint Leakage: Spoilsport Effects", *Journal of Public Economics* 204 (2021), 104531. [metadata] VERIFY content.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. This is the author's follow-up on footprint leakage under carbon taxes. Read it before submission; it may qualify the leakage-proof claim.

**EichnerPethig2015.** Eichner and Pethig, "Unilateral Consumption-Based Carbon Taxes and Negative Leakage", *Resource and Energy Economics* 40 (2015), 127–142. A companion paper is "Unilateral Climate Policy with Production-Based and Consumption-Based Carbon Emission Taxes", *ERE* 61(2) (2015), 141–163. [metadata] VERIFY content.

- The papers show that a unilateral consumption-based carbon tax can produce negative leakage in general equilibrium.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. This is theoretical support for "leakage-proof".

**Holland2012.** Holland, "Emissions Taxes versus Intensity Standards: Second-Best Environmental Policies with Incomplete Regulation", *JEEM* 63(3) (2012). [text: NBER WP 15262]

- Under leakage, an intensity standard combined with a consumption tax can reach the first best.
- A consumption tax "does not violate trade law".

*Use in 1.6:* WP §2. This is the formal case for pairing the consumption charge (1.6) with an intensity-based production instrument (1.1, 1.2).

**Courchene and Allan (2009).** [not found] v0.1 cites "Carbon Taxes and the Fiscal Federalism Implications for British Columbia, SPP Research Paper 2(1)" for a "carbon-added tax". Crossref has no matching record. **VERIFY or remove.** Courchene and Allan wrote on a carbon-added tax around 2008–2009 in Canadian policy outlets, but the citation in v0.1 cannot be confirmed.

## 4. Border adjustment, trade law and trading partners

**Mehling2019.** Mehling, van Asselt, Das, Droege and Verkuijl, "Designing Border Carbon Adjustments for Enhanced Climate Action", *AJIL* 113(3) (2019). [text: Cambridge Core]

- This is a full legal analysis of border carbon adjustments (BCAs) under GATT Articles I, II:2(a), III and XX and under the SCM Agreement.
- The authors recommend narrow coverage, default values with a right of rebuttal, and crediting of foreign policies.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §4 and §8. It replaces v0.1's reliance on Schön (2016) and Grinberg (2017), which concern destination-based *income* taxes, for the claim of low WTO risk. The like-products question under Article III:2, whether carbon intensity can differentiate like products, is open. That is why defaults should be the same for every origin.

**IsmerNeuhoff2007.** Ismer and Neuhoff, "Border Tax Adjustment: A Feasible Way to Support Stringent Emission Trading", *European Journal of Law and Economics* 24(2) (2007), 137–164. [metadata] VERIFY content.

- The paper argues that border adjustment at the intensity of the best available technology is WTO-defensible.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §4. It is cited in the brief.

**KeenParryRoaf2022.** Keen, Parry and Roaf, "Border Carbon Adjustments: Rationale, Design and Impact", *Fiscal Studies* 43(3) (2022), 209–234. [text: Tokyo College WP]

- The authors recommend benchmarking initially on *domestic* intensities, moving to country-specific values later, and allowing rebuttal.
- They note the tension between destination-basis border adjustments and origin-basis accounting under the Paris Agreement.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §4. This directly supports the brief's design: generous EU-average defaults, the same for all origins, with rebuttal.

**Mattoo2009.** Mattoo, Subramanian, van der Mensbrugghe and He, "Reconciling Climate Change and Trade Policy", World Bank PRWP 5123 (2009). [text: World Bank OKR]

- A border tax on the carbon content of *imports* across all merchandise would cut China's manufacturing exports by a fifth and low- and middle-income countries' exports by 8 percent.
- Real incomes in those groups would fall by 3.7 and 2.4 percent.
- A border tax based on *domestic* carbon content does far less damage.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §4. **This is the strongest evidence against v0.1's country-differentiated national scores.** It supports the brief's switch to EU-average defaults for all origins. Rebuttal then becomes an option that exporters take up only if they gain from it.

**CBAMExposure2025.** Maliszewska, Fischer, Jung and Chepeliev, *CBAM Exposure Indices: Methodological Note*, World Bank (2025). [text: World Bank OKR]

- Macroeconomic exposure to CBAM is small for most countries but high for a few. Mozambique, through aluminium, is the leading example.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §4. The method can be reused to estimate partners' exposure to an economy-wide carbon component. That analysis would replace v0.1's China-only illustration.

**Evans2021.** Evans, Mehling, Ritz and Sammon, "Border Carbon Adjustments and Industrial Competitiveness in a European Green Deal", *Climate Policy* 21(3) (2021). [text]

- A BCA applied only to imports leaves exporters exposed.
- Export relief raises legal risk, which careful design can reduce.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §3. The VAT route zero-rates exports by construction, which is a legal advantage over a stand-alone border adjustment. Say so.

## 5. Measurement of implicit carbon prices

**Agnolucci2023.** Agnolucci, Fischer, Heine, Montes de Oca Leon, Pryor, Patroni and Hallegatte, "Measuring Total Carbon Pricing", *World Bank Research Observer* 39(2) (2024), 227–258. [text: World Bank OKR]

- The authors compute a total carbon price, combining direct and indirect pricing, for 142 countries over 1991–2021.
- Indirect pricing (fuel excises net of subsidies) is most of the global total and has stagnated.
- **General VAT is excluded.** In the authors' words, "as across-the-board consumption taxes, they do not affect relative prices". Only VAT *preferences* on fuels enter, and they enter as subsidies.

*Use in 1.6:* **Correction, WP §1 and §5.** v0.1 says the World Bank's Measuring Total Carbon Pricing "formalised implicit carbon pricing through VAT". It also says *State and Trends* "already includes VAT-equivalent carbon prices". Both are wrong: the methodology deliberately excludes uniform VAT. The brief's "€500 per tonne" arithmetic, a 15 percent VAT on a good with 0.30 kg CO₂ per euro, is correct as arithmetic. But it should not be presented as a carbon price in the World Bank's sense, because a uniform tax creates no carbon incentive. That is exactly 1.6's point: the carbon component is what turns VAT into a carbon price.

**WorldBank2025.** World Bank, *State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2025* (2025). [text: World Bank OKR]

- Direct carbon pricing covers about 28 percent of global emissions, up from 24 percent after China's ETS was extended to industry.
- The global emissions-weighted average price, counting unpriced emissions at zero, is about \$5/tCO₂e.
- More than 70 percent of emissions face no direct price.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. **Correction:** v0.1 says "28% at an average of approximately €3/tonne". The coverage figure is right. The average is about **\$5** in the 2025 edition.

**Rennert2022.** Rennert, Errickson, Prest and others, "Comprehensive Evidence Implies a Higher Social Cost of CO₂", *Nature* 610 (2022), 687–692. [text: Europe PMC]

- The preferred mean social cost of carbon is **\$185 per tonne** of CO₂ (5–95 percent range \$44–413, 2020 dollars) at a near-term discount rate of 2 percent. That is 3.6 times the US government's then-current value of \$51.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. **Correction:** v0.1 gives "€155/tonne (\$170)" and attributes it to the inclusion of "catastrophic risk". The paper's number is \$185, and the increase comes from updated socioeconomic projections, damage functions and discounting, not from catastrophic risk specifically.

## 6. Distribution and acceptability

**Sager2019.** Sager, "Income Inequality and Carbon Consumption: Evidence from Environmental Engel Curves", *Energy Economics* 84 (2019), 104507. [text: LSE manuscript]

- The paper estimates environmental Engel curves for US households (1996–2009) using input–output carbon intensities.
- Household carbon rises with income but less than proportionally: embedded CO₂ "behaves like a necessity", with income elasticity below 1.
- Progressive redistribution raises emissions by 5.1 percent at the margin.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §6. **This challenges v0.1's claim** that a consumption carbon tax's distributional profile is "more benign than production-side carbon taxes". With an elasticity below 1, a uniform per-tonne charge on consumption takes a larger share of poor households' spending before recycling. The brief's offsetting cut in value VAT is proportional to spending, so it does not fully offset this. The net effect is the difference in carbon intensity across budgets, as 1.1's Objections section says.

**LevinsonOBrien2019.** Levinson and O'Brien, "Environmental Engel Curves: Indirect Emissions of Common Air Pollutants", *REStat* 101(1) (2019), 121–133. [abstract]

- For US local air pollutants over 1984–2012, environmental Engel curves slope upward, have income elasticities below 1, and shift down over time.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §6. Same point as Sager2019. Note that it concerns local air pollutants, not CO₂.

**Feindt2021.** Feindt, Kornek, Labeaga, Sterner and Ward, "Understanding Regressivity: Challenges and Opportunities of European Carbon Pricing", *Energy Economics* 103 (2021), 105550. [text]

- The paper estimates household incidence in 23 EU countries of a comprehensive carbon price on all goods, including imported ones.
- Within each country, incidence before recycling is mostly neutral and sometimes progressive.
- Across the EU as a whole it is regressive, because low-income member states, mainly in Eastern Europe, bear high average burdens.
- Offsetting this needs relatively small transfers between member states.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §6. **This is the most directly relevant distributional study, since it models a consumption-based price.** It supports v0.1 at national level and challenges it at EU level. A uniform EU carbon component needs a cross-member-state offset, which the value-VAT cut, set nationally, does not give.

**DouenneFabre2022.** Douenne and Fabre, "Yellow Vests, Pessimistic Beliefs, and Carbon Tax Aversion", *AEJ: Economic Policy* 14(1) (2022), 81–110. [text]

- After the Yellow Vests protests, most French respondents rejected a tax-and-dividend scheme.
- They overestimated their own losses and wrongly believed the scheme regressive.
- Information treatments moved beliefs only slightly.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1 and §6. This is evidence that a *visible* new carbon tax is hard to sell. It supports the VAT-recomposition design, in which the average rate does not rise. It also warns that perceived losses can be large even when actual losses are small.

**Dechezlepretre2025.** Dechezleprêtre, Fabre, Kruse, Planterose, Sanchez Chico and Stantcheva, "Fighting Climate Change: International Attitudes toward Climate Policies", *AER* 115(4) (2025), 1258–1300. [text]

- The survey covers more than 40,000 respondents in 20 countries.
- Support depends on perceived effectiveness, on perceived personal losses and on concern for poorer households.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §6. Present the carbon component as cutting VAT on clean goods, so that the effectiveness and fairness of the change are visible.

**Carattini2018.** Carattini, Carvalho and Fankhauser, "Overcoming Public Resistance to Carbon Taxes", *WIREs Climate Change* 9(5) (2018), e531. [text]

- The review identifies five sources of resistance: personal cost, regressivity, economic damage, doubts about effectiveness, and suspicion of a revenue grab.
- Remedies include phasing the tax in, earmarking, lump-sum rebates and communication.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §6. Fiscal neutrality answers the revenue-grab concern. A phased ten-year path answers the personal-cost concern.

**Klenert2018.** Klenert, Mattauch, Combet, Edenhofer, Hepburn, Rafaty and Stern, "Making Carbon Pricing Work for Citizens", *Nature Climate Change* 8(8) (2018), 669–677. [metadata] VERIFY content.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §6–7. It is cited in the brief.

## 9. Leakage evidence relevant to "leakage-proof by construction"

**BrangerQuirion2014.** [text] A meta-analysis finds leakage of 5–25 percent without border adjustment and −5 to 15 percent with it. Some leakage survives a border adjustment in CGE models through the fossil-fuel price channel.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. "Leakage-proof" holds for the goods channel only. A consumption charge that lowers EU fossil demand still lowers world fuel prices and raises fuel use elsewhere. Qualify the claim.

**NaegeleZaklan2019** and **Dechezlepretre2022.** [text] Ex-post studies find no detectable leakage from the EU ETS up to about 2014.

*Use in 1.6:* WP §1. The case for a consumption charge is about future price levels (€100–200/t), not observed leakage.

## Synthesis: where the Carbon Consumption Tax sits, and what is new

The Carbon Consumption Tax brings together three literatures. It is closer to some of them than v0.1 acknowledged.

**First, destination-based taxation.** The equivalence of origin and destination regimes for uniform taxes is old and well understood: Lockwood, de Meza and Myles (1994), and Lockwood and Whalley (2010), who apply it to carbon. It cuts both ways for 1.6. It explains why the VAT machinery adjusts for borders automatically, which is v0.1's strongest practical argument. But it also shows that the *value* component of VAT, being uniform, carries no carbon incentive and no trade advantage. The World Bank's total carbon price methodology excludes general VAT for exactly this reason (Agnolucci et al. 2024). The claim that a 15 percent VAT "already is" a €430–500 carbon price should be dropped or reframed. All of the carbon incentive, and all of the trade effect, sits in the carbon component.

**Second, consumption-based carbon pricing.** A well-developed European proposal already exists: a consumption charge, or "climate contribution", on carbon-intensive materials at default intensities. Ismer et al. (2016) designed it legally. Pollitt, Neuhoff and Lin (2020) modelled it: at about €80/tCO₂ it gives roughly 10 percent extra CO₂ reduction with no leakage. Grubb et al. (2022) reviewed it, and Neuhoff et al. (2026) restated it for CBAM. McAusland and Najjar (2015) and Eichner and Pethig (2015) analyze carbon-footprint taxes formally. 1.6 is not the first destination-based carbon price. What it adds is the *vehicle*: running the charge through the VAT invoice chain, with credit for input carbon. Each firm then pays the price on the carbon added at its stage. That is a direct analogue of VAT's self-enforcing structure, and it extends the base from a handful of materials to every VAT-able good. The literature's main objection is the administrative and fraud cost of tracing embodied carbon (Grubb et al. 2022). The brief's product-category defaults, set at the EU average for every origin, answer that objection the way Keen, Parry and Roaf (2022) and Mattoo et al. (2009) would recommend. The legal objection is the one the brief now flags: a component of VAT is "primarily fiscal" under Article 192(2)(a), whereas an excise on materials or a compliance obligation may not be. That favors the materials-excise route for anything to be adopted by qualified majority.

**Third, credibility.** The forward obligation on energy suppliers and the Phase Two Carbon Central Bank of v0.1 sit in the time-inconsistency literature: Kydland and Prescott (1977); Laffont and Tirole (1996); Helm, Hepburn and Mash (2003), who already proposed an independent carbon agency; and Brunner, Flachsland and Marschinski (2012). The forward obligation is an application of these ideas to ETS2, not a new idea, and should be presented as such.

**The evidence on distribution qualifies v0.1.** Sager (2019) and Levinson and O'Brien (2019) find household carbon is a necessity, with income elasticity below 1. A per-tonne charge on consumption therefore takes a larger budget share from poorer households before recycling. Feindt et al. (2021), modelling exactly a consumption-based EU carbon price, find it neutral or progressive *within* most member states but regressive *across* the EU, because Eastern European households carry high burdens. The value-VAT cut, which is proportional to spending and set nationally, does not correct the cross-country pattern. WP §6 needs a cross-member-state offset.

**What is genuinely new in 1.6:**

- the VAT-chain implementation of a carbon charge, with input-carbon credit (WP §3, Propositions 1–3);
- the fiscal-neutrality rule tied to the previous year's receipts;
- product-category defaults at EU average for every origin, with plant-level rebuttal;
- the pairing with a forward-dated supplier obligation for fuels that never enter a goods chain.

Its closest relatives, to be cited in the introduction, are Pollitt, Neuhoff and Lin (2020), Ismer et al. (2016), Grubb et al. (2022) and McAusland and Najjar (2015).
