## 1. IMF practice on expenditure and public-investment efficiency (PIE-X, PIMA, C-PIMA, FM/WEO chapters, spending reviews, efficiency-adjusted capital, SDG costing)

### Takeaway
IMF practice measures public-investment efficiency as distance from an input–output frontier (PIE-X). The average gap is about 30% across 134 countries: 13% in advanced economies, 27% in emerging markets and 40% in LIDCs. The IMF addresses the gap through institutional diagnostics: PIMA (15 institutions in 3 phases, updated 2018) and C-PIMA (5 climate institutions, 2021; more than 50 countries by October 2024). On the spending side it uses spending reviews (How-To Note 2022/004) and efficiency-adjusted needs costing (SDN/19/03). Fiscal Monitor and WEO chapters show that the output payoff of public investment depends on efficiency.

### Cited Findings
**"Making Public Investment More Efficient" (IMF Policy Paper, June 2015) and PIE-X**
- The 2015 paper covered 134 countries, which it called the largest public investment efficiency analysis to date, and found an average efficiency gap of about 30%. Gaps were **LIDCs 40%, EMs 27%, AEs 13%**. — [IMF Policy Paper 2015 (PDF, infrastructuregovern.imf.org)](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/Making%20Public%20Investment%20More%20Efficient%202015.pdf); [IMF Bookstore summary](https://bookstore.imf.org/books/making-public-investment-more-efficient); [IMF Survey article (June 2015)](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2015/09/28/04/53/sonew061815a)
- PIE-X relates the public capital stock (input) to indicators of infrastructure access and quality (outputs). Countries score between 0 and 1 by distance from the efficiency frontier. — [IMF Policy Paper 2015](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/Making%20Public%20Investment%20More%20Efficient%202015.pdf)
- The 2015 paper applied the new PIMA tool to 25 countries and concluded that stronger public investment management is associated with better infrastructure outcomes. — [IMF Policy Paper 2015](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/Making%20Public%20Investment%20More%20Efficient%202015.pdf)
- Discrepancy: a Chatham House summary reports "more than 100 countries" and a 27% average loss. That figure matches the IMF's EM sub-group, so it is probably a misreading; prefer the IMF figures. — [Chatham House](https://www.chathamhouse.org/node/22883/nojs)
- A follow-on IMF paper applies the framework to the G-20: "Improving Public Investment Efficiency in the G-20" (2016). — [IMF Policy Paper PP4980](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/policy-papers/issues/2016/12/31/improving-public-investment-efficiency-in-the-g-20-pp4980)
- For comparison, an Africa-focused DEA study of 32 African countries reports an average efficiency score of 61% (working paper no. 365). — [WPS 365 (ebcam.eu-hosted PDF)](https://www.ebcam.eu/images/wps_no_365_public_investment_efficiency_economic_growth_and_debt_sustainability_in_africa_.pdf)

**PIMA (2015 framework; 2018 Review and Update)**
- "Public Investment Management Assessment—Review and Update", IMF Policy Paper, 10 May 2018. It keeps the three phases (planning, allocation, implementation) with five institutions each, for 15 institutions. Each institution has three dimensions, scored as not met, partially met or fully met. The update gives more weight to **maintenance, procurement, independent project review and the enabling environment**. — [IMF Policy Paper (May 2018)](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Policy-Papers/Issues/2018/05/10/pp042518public-investment-management-assessment-review-and-update); [IMF PFM Blog, June 2018](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2018/06/latest-update-on-public-investment-management-assessments-pimas)

**Climate-PIMA (C-PIMA)**
- C-PIMA was introduced in 2021 as an extension module of PIMA in the policy paper "Strengthening Infrastructure Governance for Climate-Responsive Public Investment" (dated 22 Dec 2021). Its aim is to identify institutional improvements for building low-carbon, climate-resilient infrastructure. — [IMF Policy Paper (Dec 2021)](https://imf.org/en/Publications/Policy-Papers/Issues/2021/12/22/Strengthening-Infrastructure-Governance-for-Climate-Responsive-Public-Investment-511258)
- The 2021 IMF PFM blog lists five pillars: climate-aware planning, coordination across government, project appraisal and selection, budgeting and portfolio management, and risk management. At launch the tool had been tested in 11 countries. — [IMF PFM Blog (Dec 2021)](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2021/12/strengthening-governance-for-climate-responsive-public-investment)
- The "C-PIMA Handbook" (IMF book, 10 Nov 2025) describes five institutions with 15 dimensions in total. It reports that C-PIMA had been conducted in more than 50 countries as of October 2024. — [IMF C-PIMA Handbook (2025)](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/books/issues/2025/11/10/c-pima-handbook-climate-public-investment-management-assessment-559283)
- Country examples include Togo (combined PIMA and C-PIMA, Dec 2024), which found "emerging good practices" but weaknesses throughout the cycle, as well as Guatemala, Cabo Verde and Maldives. — [Togo TA summary](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/high-level-summary-technical-assistance-reports/Issues/2024/12/27/Togo-Public-Investment-Management-Assessment-PIMA-and-Climate-PIMA-560259); [Cabo Verde C-PIMA TAR](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Countries/cabo-verde/Documents/CaboVerdeCPIMATAR.pdf)

**Fiscal Monitor / WEO chapters**
- **WEO October 2014, Ch. 3, "Is It Time for an Infrastructure Push? The Macroeconomic Effects of Public Investment".** It finds that more public infrastructure investment raises output in both the short and long term, especially when there is economic slack and investment efficiency is high. Debt-financed projects with clearly identified needs and efficient execution can raise output without increasing the debt-to-GDP ratio, and debt financing has larger output effects than tax- or spending-cut financing. — [WEO Oct 2014 Ch. 3 (IMF eLibrary)](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781498331555/ch003.xml); [Chapter PDF (library mirror)](https://0-www-imf-org.library.svsu.edu/external/pubs/ft/weo/2014/02/pdf/c3.pdf); [IMF press briefing transcript, 30 Sep 2014](https://www.imf.org/external/np/tr/2014/tr093014.htm); [IMF Blog, 30 Sep 2014](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2014/09/30/now-is-a-good-time-to-invest-in-infrastructure)
- **Fiscal Monitor October 2020, Ch. 2, "Public Investment for the Recovery".** Using cross-country data and a sample of 400,000 firms, it finds that public investment has strong GDP and employment effects during high uncertainty. In AEs and EMs the multiplier peaks at **over 2 after two years**. An extra 1% of GDP in public investment creates about 7 million jobs directly and 20–33 million in total. The press summary ties a 2.7% GDP effect to high-quality investment and debt burdens that do not weaken the private response. — [FM Oct 2020 Ch. 2 executive summary](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/fiscal-monitor/2020/october/english/ch2execsum.pdf.md); [IMF Blog, 5 Oct 2020](https://www.imf.org/blogs/articles/2020/10/05/blog-public-investment-for-the-recovery); [FM Oct 2020 landing page](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fm/issues/2020/09/30/october-2020-fiscal-monitor)

**Spending reviews**
- The correct title is **"How to Design and Institutionalize Spending Reviews"** (IMF FAD How-To Note No. 2022/004, September 2022, by Laura Doherty and Amanda Sayegh), not "...Design and Implement...". It sets out a four-stage process: (1) objectives and design, (2) identifying savings options through in-depth analysis, (3) deciding which measures go into the budget, and (4) implementation. Its organizational principles are strong finance-ministry oversight, a broad knowledge base, and a ministerial-level decision mechanism. — [IMF HTN 2022/004](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fiscal-affairs-department-how-to-notes/issues/2022/09/20/how-to-design-and-institutionalize-spending-reviews-523364); [RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfhtn/2022-004.html); [IMF PFM Blog, Nov 2022](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2022/11/renewed-interest-in-spending-reviews)
- There is no single "blueprint" methodology for spending reviews. — [WCPP briefing](https://wcpporguk.cf.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/WCPP-Spending-Reviews-International-Best-Practice-and-Key-Learning-Policy-Briefing.pdf)

**Efficiency-adjusted public capital**
- Gupta, Kangur, Papageorgiou and Wane, "Efficiency-Adjusted Public Capital and Growth", *World Development* 57(C): 164–178 (2014); earlier IMF WP/11/217 (2011). Author order differs between listings; the IMF listing puts Wane before Papageorgiou. The paper builds efficiency-adjusted public capital series for 52 developing countries. Public capital contributes significantly to growth. The income share of public capital is larger in middle-income countries, but the **marginal product is larger in low-income countries**. Investment quality (project selection and implementation) matters mainly in LICs. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/imfimfwpa/2011_2f217.htm); [IMF WP page](https://imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2016/12/31/Efficiency-Adjusted-Public-Capital-and-Growth-25248)
- Crivelli (IMF WP/17/168) extends this work and finds that ignoring inefficiency leads to underestimating the growth impact of public and private capital. — [IMF WP/17/168](https://imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WP/2017/wp17168.ashx)

**SDG costing (SDN/19/03)**
- Gaspar, Amaglobeli, Garcia-Escribano, Prady and Soto, "Fiscal Policy and Development: Human, Social, and Physical Investments for the SDGs", IMF SDN/19/03, January 2019. It estimates additional spending needed in 2030, relative to current spending as a share of GDP, for education, health, roads, electricity, and water and sanitation. Coverage is 155 countries (49 LIDCs, 72 EMs, 34 AEs). — [IMF Bookstore](https://bookstore.imf.org/books/fiscal-policy-and-development); [SDN full text (mirror)](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Fiscal-Policy-and-Development-Human-Social-and-Physical-Investment-for-the-SDGs.pdf); [INFF resource page](https://www.inff.org/resources/fiscal-policy-and-development-human-social-and-physical-investment-for-the-sdgs)
- Method: (1) identify the main cost drivers, (2) set reference values for those drivers consistent with high SDG performance, (3) estimate 2030 spending at the reference values. Roads and electricity use a different approach, and water and sanitation use the World Bank model. — [IMF SDG costing note](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/topics/sdg/sdg-costing-un.pdf.md)
- The five country case studies are **Rwanda, Benin, Vietnam, Indonesia and Guatemala** (Pakistan is not among them). Additional 2030 needs in percentage points of GDP: Rwanda 18.7, Benin 21.3, Vietnam 6.4, Indonesia 5.6, Guatemala 11.5. — [IMF SDG costing note](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/topics/sdg/sdg-costing-un.pdf.md); [SDN mirror](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Fiscal-Policy-and-Development-Human-Social-and-Physical-Investment-for-the-SDGs.pdf)

**Investment efficiency and the "white elephant" problem**
- The IMF PFM blog's "Trapping the White Elephants" (Nov 2015) presents the 2015 efficiency agenda as a way to avoid repeating past investment-boom failures. — [IMF PFM Blog (Nov 2015)](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/pfmblog/2015/11/trapping-the-white-elephants-can-the-latest-public-investment-boom-avoid-the-mistakes-of-the-past.html)
- Warner (IMF WP/13/58), "A Framework for Efficient Government Investment", argues that better investment efficiency could relax borrowing constraints, citing the DIG model. — [IMF WP/13/58](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2013/wp1358.pdf)

### Inferences
- PIE-X gaps can serve as a rough prior for the macro-model efficiency parameter: s ≈ 1 − gap, giving about 0.87 (AE), 0.73 (EM) and 0.60 (LIDC). They are output-frontier gaps, not shares of spending lost, so they are not the same concept as DIG's s, which is the share of spending that becomes productive capital. Treat the mapping as a bounded prior, not a calibration. DIG applications use lower values (0.35–0.6; see §2), consistent with Pritchett-style estimates of the cost-to-capital ratio.
- PIMA and C-PIMA scores are institution-level, not sector-level. A CBA layer could use the PIMA dimensions on appraisal, maintenance funding and project portfolio management as qualitative modifiers of sector efficiency and recurrent-cost coverage.
- SDN/19/03 provides a needs-costing template (cost drivers × reference unit costs). A sector CBA layer can reuse it to size investment paths, which then feed DIG-family models as exogenous public-investment shocks.

### Gaps

- **Not verified:** whether the WEO 2014 chapter's simulations used GIMF and an output elasticity of 0.17 (see §5). The commonly cited multipliers (1 pp of GDP raises output by about 0.4% in the same year and about 1.5% after four years, in AEs) and the chapter's authorship (Abiad, Furceri, Topalova et al.) are also unverified.
- **Not verified:** Fiscal Monitor April 2014 Ch. 2 ("Public Expenditure Reform: Making Difficult Choices") and IMF health and education spending-efficiency work (e.g., Garcia-Escribano, Juarros & Mogues 2022; Grigoli & Kapsoli 2013). Search budget ran out. I found no evidence of a product called an IMF "Expenditure Assessment Tool".
- **Not verified:** whether SDN/19/03 explicitly uses "efficiency-adjusted unit costs" or DIG-type modelling (the country companion papers may). ## 2. The DIG model (Buffie, Berg, Pattillo, Portillo & Zanna 2012, IMF WP/12/144)

### Takeaway
DIG is a small-open-economy model for LICs. It links public investment to growth and debt through effective public capital, an efficiency parameter s, absorptive-capacity cost overruns, recurrent costs partly covered by user fees, a gradual tax and transfer fiscal reaction, and three debt types (concessional, external commercial, domestic). Scaling up pays for itself only when effective returns (s × return) are high, user-fee recovery is adequate and fiscal adjustment is timely. Otherwise large, abrupt tax increases are needed.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Andrew Berg, Rafael A. Portillo, Edward F. Buffie, Catherine A. Pattillo and Luis-Felipe Zanna, "Public Investment, Growth, and Debt Sustainability: Putting Together the Pieces", IMF Working Paper **WP/12/144**, June 2012. The IMF page lists the authors in this order; the paper is usually cited as Buffie et al. (2012). Keywords: public investment, growth, debt sustainability, fiscal policy, infrastructure, aid. — [IMF WP/12/144 PDF](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12144.pdf); [IMF WP page](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/public-investment-growth-and-debt-sustainability-putting-together-the-pieces-25962)
- Buffie et al. (2012) emphasize how the public-investment–growth link interacts with the fiscal reaction function, absorptive capacity and other LIC features. — [IMF WP/15/272](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2015/wp15272.pdf)
- **User fees** are set as a fixed multiple of **recurrent costs**. **Absorptive capacity** is modelled as cost overruns, that is, rising "prices" of public investment rather than a separate capital loss. In the illustrative scenario, low user-fee recovery combined with an inefficient investment surge pushed tax rates sharply higher. — [IMF WP/12/144](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12144.pdf) (via search extract)
- Absorptive-capacity specification as applied in an IMF Afghanistan paper "following Buffie et al.": "cost overruns depend on the size of infrastructure investment relative to its existing level and its deviation from its steady-state level. The parameter ψ > 0 captures the severity of the absorptive capacity in public capital." — [IMF WP/13/133 (Afghanistan)](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2013/wp13133.pdf)
- **Risk premium:** "the public risk premium is constant – i.e., we set [the elasticity] = 0 in equation (22) – and calibrate it as the difference between the interest rate on public commercial debt and the risk-free foreign interest rate." Equation (22) therefore allows a debt-elastic premium that is switched off in the baseline. — [IMF WP/12/144](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12144.pdf)
- **Efficiency s** converts public investment spending into effective public capital. In the IMF's DIG application for Guinea, **user fees are set at 50% of recurrent costs**, based on sub-Saharan Africa estimates. — [IMF Country Report 16/262 (Guinea)](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/scr/2016/cr16262.pdf)
- Transition costs: when growth-driven revenue arrives too slowly, a scaling-up requires fiscal adjustment. — [IMF WP/12/144](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12144.pdf); see also [IMF WP/17/138, "Public Investment Scaling-up and Debt Sustainability: …"](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2017/wp17138.pdf)
- **Efficiency definition and calibration** (DIG authors' follow-up): efficiency is "the ratio between the actual increment to public capital and the amount spent". In standard neoclassical and endogenous growth models, more public investment in inefficient countries does **not** generally yield a smaller growth effect, because the marginal product of public capital falls as the capital/output ratio rises, so efficiency and capital scarcity are likely inversely related. The WP sets the **net return on public capital at 25% for the low-efficiency country** and uses an illustrative case with a **20% return and efficiency 0.60**. — Berg, Buffie, Pattillo, Portillo, Presbitero & Zanna, "Some Misconceptions About Public Investment Efficiency and Growth", *Economica* 86(342): 409–430 (April 2019) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ecca.12275); [IMF WP/15/272 (Dec 2015)](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2015/wp15272.pdf)
- Related DIG-family calibrations (verified via search extracts):
  - Berg, Portillo, Yang & Zanna, "Public Investment in Resource-Abundant Developing Countries", *IMF Economic Review* 61(1): 92–129 (2013), WP/12/274. Uses **efficiency 0.35–0.5**, consistent with Pritchett (2000) for sub-Saharan Africa, an **output elasticity of public capital α_G = 0.1**, and **8% annual depreciation** of public capital. — [IMF WP/12/274](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12274.pdf); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/imfer.2013.1)
  - IMF WP/15/236, "Public Investment in a Developing Country Facing Resource Depletion", starts from **efficiency 0.5**, consistent with developing countries with weak institutions. — [IMF WP/15/236](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2015/wp15236.pdf)
- **DIG-Labor (2020):** Buffie, Zanna, Adam, Balma, Tessema & Kpodar, "Debt, Investment, and Growth in Developing Countries with Segmented Labor Markets", IMF WP/20/102, June 2020. It extends "the DIG model widely used at the IMF since 2012" with segmented labour markets and an informal non-agricultural sector. In DIG-Labor, efficiency is a parameter s in (0,1), and **maintenance spending lowers the depreciation rate of infrastructure** (search extract of the PDF; a second search did not surface the maintenance passage, so treat as needing confirmation). At average estimated returns, human-capital investment outperforms infrastructure in the long run, but the payoff takes 15+ years. — [IMF WP/20/102 page](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2020/06/19/Debt-Investment-and-Growth-in-Developing-Countries-with-Segmented-Labor-Markets-49458); [WP/20/102 PDF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2020/english/wpiea2020102-print-pdf.pdf)
- Absorptive-capacity follow-up: IMF WP/20/48, "On the Capacity to Absorb Public Investment: How Much is Too Much?" — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2020-048.html)
- Recurrent-cost follow-up: IMF WP/14/73, "The Impact of Distortionary Taxation, Recurrent Costs, and …" (full title not surfaced). — [IMF WP/14/73](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2014/wp1473.pdf)

### Inferences
**Reconstructed canonical DIG equation set** (notation harmonised across the DIG family; functional forms are standard for these papers but NOT text-verified against WP/12/144):

- Sectoral production (traded x, non-traded n), with effective public capital z as an unpaid factor:
  $$Q_{j,t} = a_j\, z_{t-1}^{\psi_j}\, k_{j,t-1}^{\alpha_j}\, L_{j,t}^{1-\alpha_j},\quad j\in\{x,n\}$$
  The traded/non-traded split generates real-exchange-rate (Dutch-disease) effects when investment or aid raises non-traded demand.
- Effective public capital, with efficiency s and absorptive-capacity cost overruns (the price of a unit of public investment rises with the scale of the push):
  $$z_t = (1-\delta_z)\,z_{t-1} + s\,\frac{I^{z}_t}{P^{z}_t},\qquad P^{z}_t = 1 + \Phi\!\left(\frac{I^{z}_t}{z_{t-1}} - \frac{\bar I^{z}}{\bar z}\right),\ \Phi(0)=0,\ \Phi'>0$$
  Φ's curvature is the "severity" parameter (ψ > 0 in WP/13/133). DIG-Labor adds maintenance: δ_z = δ(M_t/z_{t−1}) with δ' < 0.
- Return on public capital (net of depreciation, as in WP/15/272):
  $$R = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial z} - \delta_z = \psi\,\frac{Y}{z} - \delta_z \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \psi = (R+\delta_z)\,\frac{z}{Y}$$
- Efficiency algebra (Berg et al. 2019): with spending-based capital K^I and z = s K^I, a 1-unit rise in spending gives dz = s and dY = (R+δ_z)·s. If ψ is held fixed across countries, (R+δ_z) = ψY/(sK^I), so dY/dI = ψY/K^I, which does not depend on s. If R is held fixed instead (e.g., from CBA), the payoff per unit of spending scales with s.
- Recurrent costs and user fees (verified convention: fees are a fixed multiple of recurrent costs):
  $$RC_t = \mu\, z_{t-1},\qquad UF_t = f\cdot RC_t\quad(\text{Guinea: } f=0.5)$$
- Government budget constraint (schematic; exchange-rate conversion omitted):
  $$\tau_t\,\text{Base}_t + UF_t + \text{Grants}_t + \Delta D^{c}_t + \Delta B^{*}_t + \Delta b_t = I^{z}_t + RC_t + \text{Tr}_t + G_t + r^{c}D^{c}_{t-1} + r^{*}_t B^{*}_{t-1} + r^{d}_t b_{t-1}$$
  Here D^c is concessional external debt (exogenous path, fixed low rate), B* is external commercial debt and b is domestic debt.
- Commercial-debt premium: r*_t = r^f + u_t, with u_t a function of the deviation of B* from its reference level (baseline elasticity = 0).
- Fiscal reaction: the tax rate and transfers move gradually toward the level that closes the fiscal gap, e.g., τ_t = τ_{t−1} + λ(τ^{target}_t − τ_{t−1}), with a tax ceiling beyond which transfers adjust and an optional delay before adjustment starts. Smaller λ or longer lags mean more borrowing (commercial or domestic) and a higher debt peak.
- Read together, the verified pieces imply that the DIG "self-financing" condition is roughly (effective return × s × tax and fee capture) ≥ (debt service + recurrent costs net of fees). This is why s, R, f and the adjustment speed are the critical parameters.

### Gaps
- Exact equation numbering and functional forms in WP/12/144 (the Φ(·) form for cost overruns, the tax instrument (consumption tax vs. VAT), transfer adjustment, whether recurrent costs scale with z or with I^z, and the household structure (savers vs. hand-to-mouth)) could not be checked because the PDF was unreachable.
- The WP/12/144 baseline calibration table was not verified: values for s, R, δ_z, ψ, f, μ, concessional and commercial interest rates, tax ceiling and λ. Typical values from related DIG work: s 0.35–0.6, R 20–25%, δ 8%, α_G/ψ 0.1, f 0.5 (Guinea), all verified as cited above. Note: depreciation rates around 5% are also used in some DIG applications (not verified).

## 3. DIGNAR (Melina, Yang & Zanna 2014/2016)

### Takeaway
DIGNAR adds a natural-resource sector, resource revenue and a **resource fund** to DIG. The fund buffers revenue volatility, and once it hits its floor, external and domestic borrowing fill the gap. With feasible fiscal adjustment, a **delinked** (sustainable-investing) public investment path combined with the fund reduces macro instability compared with **spend-as-you-go**.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Giovanni Melina, Susan S. Yang (journal: Shu-Chun S. Yang) and Luis-Felipe Zanna. IMF WP/14/50 (2014), "Debt Sustainability, Public Investment, and Natural Resources in Developing Countries: the DIGNAR Model". Published version: *Economic Modelling* 52(B): 630–649 (2016). — [IMF WP page](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2016/12/31/Debt-Sustainability-Public-Investment-and-Natural-Resources-in-Developing-Countries-the-41455); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264999315002898); [City, Univ. of London repository](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/13942/); [GOV.UK R4D listing](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/debt-sustainability-public-investment-and-natural-resources-in-developing-countries-the-dignar-model)
- Structure: a dynamic small open economy with two household types (including poor households without access to financial markets), traded and non-traded sectors, and a natural-resource sector. Public capital enters production, and public investment is subject to **inefficiencies and absorptive-capacity constraints**. Financing comes from concessional, domestic and external commercial debt plus a resource fund. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264999315002898); [City repository](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/13942/)
- Fund rule: "The resource fund can also serve as a buffer to absorb fiscal balances for given projections of resource revenues and public investment plans. When the fund is drawn down to its minimal value, a combination of external and domestic borrowing can be used to cover the fiscal gap in the short to medium run." — [IMF WP/14/50 / Econ. Modelling](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264999315002898)
- Key result: "when fiscal adjustment is implementable, a delinked public investment approach combined with the resource fund can reduce macroeconomic instability relative to a spend-as-you-go approach." — [IMF WP/14/50](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2016/12/31/Debt-Sustainability-Public-Investment-and-Natural-Resources-in-Developing-Countries-the-41455)
- Absorptive-capacity calibration anchor: Arestoff & Hurlin (2006, Mexican data) find that "the coefficient of regressing public capital produced (or effective investment in our model) on investment expenditures falls from 0.5 to 0.35 when investment expenditures exceed 1.6 times of the average level". — [DIGNAR (search extract)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264999315002898)
- Lineage: DIGNAR builds on Berg, Portillo, Yang & Zanna (2013), which combines public investment with a resource fund to address exhaustibility and volatility (the "sustainable investing approach"). — [Springer (IMF Econ Rev 2013)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/imfer.2013.1); [IMF SDN/15/18](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/sdn/2015/sdn1518.pdf)
- Extensions: DIGNAR-MTFF (*Resources Policy*) adds industrial production, intermediate-goods inflows and public-investment inefficiency for oil economies. "Optimal Public Investment in Resource-Rich Low-Income Countries" appears in *Journal of African Economies* 31(1): 75–93 (2022). A DSGE study of Iran (*Energy Policy* 146, 2020) assesses alternative investment policies. — [DIGNAR-MTFF](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0301420722005633); [JAE 2022 (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jafrec/v31y2022i1p75-93..html); [Energy Policy 2020 (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/enepol/v146y2020ics0301421520305310.html)

### Inferences
- **Reconstructed DIGNAR fiscal block** (not text-verified):
  - Resource revenue: R^N_t = τ^N p^N_t Q^N_t, with an exogenous price and output path.
  - Fund: F_t = (1+r^f)F_{t−1} + R^N_t − W_t, with F_t ≥ F_min.
  - Under "spend-as-you-go", I^z_t tracks R^N_t. Under "delinked/sustainable investing", I^z_t follows a pre-announced path and W_t absorbs the gap until F_min binds, after which commercial and domestic debt adjust.
  - A "bird-in-hand" rule spends only the return on accumulated financial assets.
  - Absorptive capacity can be expressed as an efficiency kink: s_t = 0.5 if I^z_t ≤ 1.6 Ī^z, falling toward 0.35 above that threshold (Arestoff–Hurlin).
- For a CBA layer, DIGNAR's resource-revenue path is a natural slot for **fuel-import savings or fossil-revenue losses** under climate policy (e.g., CPAT output). Its fund rules are a template for how a "climate fund" or RSF tranche could pace investment.

### Gaps
- DIGNAR calibration values (fund floor, resource-revenue share, efficiency, return, depreciation, fee share, fiscal reaction λ's) were not retrieved. No Ghana-specific DIGNAR calibration was confirmed. The exact bird-in-hand formula in DIGNAR was not verified.

## 4. DIGNAD (Marto, Papageorgiou & Klyuev 2018), the DIGNAD Toolkit, and IMF climate-resilience variants

### Takeaway
DIGNAD adds natural disasters and an **adaptation (resilient) public capital** stock, more costly but less damaged, to the DIG framework. Its Vanuatu application shows that building resilience ex ante lowers debt-distress risk at lower donor cost. The IMF now runs DIGNAD through an **Excel/Dynare toolkit** (TNM 2023/003) for climate and disaster macro-fiscal analysis in country work (e.g., IMF Country Report 2023/415). Newer IMF DSGE variants (Dominica, WP/23/138; a 2025 WP on temperature) extend it.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation (correcting the brief):** Ricardo Marto, Chris Papageorgiou and Vladimir Klyuev, "Building resilience to natural disasters: An application to small developing states", *Journal of Development Economics* **135: 574–586** (September 2018), not vol. 131. An IMF Working Paper with the same title and authors appeared in 2017. Keywords: adaptation, debt sustainability, natural disasters, resilience, small states. — [Ricardo Marto publication page](https://www.ricardomarto.com/publication/pub_vanuatu/); [Marto publications](https://www.ricardomarto.com/publication/)
- Application: Vanuatu after Cyclone Pam. To keep public debt sustainable, donors would need to provide grants equal to an extra half of pre-cyclone GDP, spread over 15 years. Helping the government build resilience lowers the risk of debt distress at lower donor cost. — [Marto publication page](https://www.ricardomarto.com/publication/pub_vanuatu/)
- **DIGNAD Toolkit:** Zamid Aligishiev, Ruane and Sultanov (first names of the co-authors not verified), "A User Manual for the DIGNAD Toolkit", IMF Technical Notes and Manuals 2023/003. DIGNAD is a dynamic GE small-open-economy model for assessing the macro-fiscal impact of natural disasters and adaptation infrastructure in LICs and EMs. The toolkit is an **Excel interface usable without MATLAB/Dynare knowledge**. — [IMF eLibrary TNM 2023/003](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/005/2023/003/article-A001-en.xml); [TNM PDF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/tnm/2023/english/tnmea2023003.pdf); [ResearchGate copy](https://researchgate.net/publication/371517646_A_User_Manual_for_the_DIGNAD_Toolkit)
- What the manual covers:
  - It separates **climate-resilient (adaptation) infrastructure** from **standard public infrastructure**. Adaptation infrastructure reduces disaster damages, and a key parameter sets how much a given capital stock mitigates them.
  - Parameters are colour-coded: **green** parameters can usually come from national accounts, **orange** should use country-specific data where available, and **yellow** are the key adaptation-related parameters.
  - Precise data on adaptation spending are often unavailable in LICs.
  - A "Disasters" sheet sets disaster magnitude, and worked scenarios cover baseline, adaptation 1 and adaptation 2. The toolkit also has a "Realism Module".
  
  — [IMF TNM 2023/003](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/005/2023/003/article-A001-en.xml); [Realism Module figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/DIGNAD-Toolkit-Realism-Module_fig12_371517646)
- DIGNAD use cases: ex-ante policies (building adaptation infrastructure, raising fiscal buffers) and debt-sustainability stress tests. — [IMF Climate Data: DIGNAD](https://climatedata.imf.org/content/310191d9da5a4e6ea5b1bcdd9123ebba); [IMF presentation, "The DIGNAD Model Applications and a New Toolkit" (MathWorks Finance Conf. 2024)](https://www.mathworks.com/content/dam/mathworks/mathworks-dot-com/company/events/conferences/mathworks-finance-conference/2024/na-emea-fin-conf-2024-imf-dignad-model.pdf)
- Country application: IMF Staff Country Report 2023/415 includes the selected-issues chapter "Building Resilience to Natural Disasters and Climate Change: A Model Application". — [IMF eLibrary CR 2023/415](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2023/415/article-A003-en.xml)
- **IMF Staff Climate Note 2022/002:** Aligishiev, Bellon & Massetti, "Macro-Fiscal Implications of Adaptation to Climate Change" (March 2022; DOI 10.5089/9798400201608.066). It sets out four building blocks for integrating adaptation into fiscal policy, starting with taking stock of climate risks. It reviews evidence on adaptation effectiveness and costs and suggests ways to integrate climate risks and adaptation costs into macro-fiscal frameworks. Companion notes are SCN 2022/001 (Bellon & Massetti, economic principles) and SCN 2022/003 (planning and mainstreaming). — [IMF SCN 2022/002](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/staff-climate-notes/issues/2022/03/16/macro-fiscal-implications-of-adaptation-to-climate-change-512769); [SCN 2022/003](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/staff-climate-notes/Issues/2022/03/16/Planning-and-Mainstreaming-Adaptation-to-Climate-Change-in-Fiscal-Policy-512776)
- **IMF WP/23/138:** Fernandez-Corugedo, Gonzalez-Gomez & Guerson, "The Macroeconomic Returns of Investment in Resilience to Natural Disasters under Climate Change: A DSGE Approach" (June 2023). This is a **Markov-switching DSGE** calibrated to **Dominica**, with incomplete markets, collateral constraints, remittances, a full menu of tax and spending instruments and an endogenous climate risk premium. Disasters cause large, persistent output and fiscal losses, while adaptation investment yields large long-run returns in private investment, employment, output and **tax revenue**, especially under climate change. — [IMF WP/23/138](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2023/06/30/The-Macroeconomic-Returns-of-Investment-in-Resilience-to-Natural-Disasters-under-Climate-534579); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2023-138.html)
- A newer IMF WP, "Building Macroeconomic Resilience to Natural Disasters and Persistent Temperature Changes" (IMF WP page dated 25 July 2025), was found. Its authors and model were not verified. — [IMF WP (2025)](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2025/07/25/building-macroeconomic-resilience-to-natural-disasters-and-persistent-temperature-changes-568891)
- Results for the DIGNAD toolkit also surfaced the 2023 RSF operational review paper and the 2024 supplement to the 2018 LIC-DSF Guidance Note, which suggests DIGNAD is referenced in RSF and LIC-DSF climate work. Their content was not verified. — [IMF RSF policy paper 2023 (ppea2023051)](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/pp/2023/english/ppea2023051.pdf); [2024 Supplement to the 2018 LIC-DSF Guidance Note](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/f5a013eba6586292355dacc48f87a831-0350012024/original/Supplement-to-2018-Guidance-Note-LIC-DSF.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Reconstructed DIGNAD capital block** (not text-verified):
  - Two public-capital stocks: standard z^S and adaptation z^A, accumulating as z^i_t = (1−δ^i) z^i_{t−1} + s^i I^i_t / P^i_t.
  - Adaptation capital costs (1+φ^A) per unit relative to standard capital.
  - A disaster of magnitude D_t destroys a fraction ε^S(D_t) of z^S, a smaller fraction ε^A(D_t) of z^A, and a fraction of private capital that falls as z^A/(z^S + z^A) rises. It also lowers TFP temporarily.
  - Production uses total effective public capital (z^S + z^A)^ψ.
  - The yellow-coded "adaptation effectiveness" parameter in the toolkit governs how far ε falls with adaptation capital.
- DIGNAD is the closest IMF analogue to a CBA "avoided-loss" calculation. A sector CBA's expected annual damage reduction per $ of resilient spending maps onto the effectiveness and cost-premium parameters.

## 5. GIMF (Kumhof, Laxton, Muir & Mursula 2010; Anderson et al. 2013) and its use for public-investment shocks

### Takeaway
GIMF is the IMF's multi-region, non-Ricardian DSGE model. Government investment builds productive infrastructure capital, while government consumption is unproductive. It underpins the IMF's structural fiscal-multiplier work, and its public-capital elasticity is the key parameter for investment shocks. IMF WP/15/95 reports a baseline core-infrastructure elasticity of 0.17, above meta-analytic averages of about 0.08–0.12.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Michael Kumhof, Douglas Laxton, Dirk Muir and Susanna Mursula, "The Global Integrated Monetary and Fiscal Model (GIMF) – Theoretical Structure", IMF Working Paper **WP/10/34**, February 2010. This is the main reference for the model. — [IMF WP/10/34](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2010/wp1034.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1555486)
- Simulation properties: "Getting to Know GIMF: The Simulation Properties of the Global Integrated Monetary and Fiscal Model" (IMF WP/13/55, 2013; series number from the brief, title verified). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256052554_Getting_to_Know_GIMF_The_Simulation_Properties_of_the_Global_Integrated_Monetary_and_Fiscal_Model)
- In GIMF fiscal-stimulus work (Freedman, Kumhof, Laxton, Muir & Mursula), **government investment augments a stock of publicly provided infrastructure capital** used by firms as an input, while government consumption is unproductive. — [Freedman et al., "Global Effects of Fiscal Stimulus During the Crisis" (draft)](http://douglaslaxton.org/sitebuildercontent/sitebuilderfiles/mk.april9.carnegie1march.pdf); see also [Coenen et al., "Effects of Fiscal Stimulus in Structural Models", IMF WP/10/73](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2010/wp1073.pdf)
- **Output elasticity of public capital:** IMF WP/15/95, "The Macroeconomic Effects of Public Investment: Evidence from Advanced Economies", calls this elasticity a critical input. It states that baseline simulations assume an output elasticity with respect to core infrastructure installed by national government of **0.17**. A survey concludes the aggregate production-function elasticity is probably **0.065–0.12**. — [IMF WP/15/95 (infrastructuregovern.imf.org)](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Knowledge-Hub/Publications/pubdocuments/The%20Macroeconomic%20Effects%20of%20Public%20Investment%20Evidence%20from%20Advanced%20Economies.pdf); [William & Mary WP 108 survey](https://economics.wm.edu/wp/cwm_wp108rev1.pdf)
- **WEO Oct 2014 Ch. 3:** higher public infrastructure investment raises output in the short and long run, more so with slack and high efficiency. Debt-financed efficient investment can leave debt/GDP unchanged. — [WEO Oct 2014 Ch. 3](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781498331555/ch003.xml); [IMF press briefing](https://www.imf.org/external/np/tr/2014/tr093014.htm)
- See also NBER w27625, "The Macroeconomic Consequences of Infrastructure [Investment]", for a critical review of infrastructure multipliers. — [NBER w27625](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27625/w27625.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Reconstructed GIMF public-capital block** (not text-verified):
  - Firms' output is Y_t = A_t (K^G_t)^{α_G} F(K_t, L_t), with K^G_t = (1−δ_G)K^G_{t−1} + I^G_t (possibly with time-to-build).
  - A fiscal rule sets the deficit ratio as a structural target plus a cyclical term.
  - Non-Ricardian features come from overlapping generations with finite planning horizons and liquidity-constrained households.
  - GIMF has no native "user fee" or "recurrent cost" block. Fees and O&M must enter as transfer or revenue offsets and government-consumption paths.
- The efficiency adjustment in GIMF-type work is done by **scaling the investment shock** (only s × I^G enters K^G) or by lowering α_G. That matches DIG's s and makes efficiency the natural common parameter for a combined tool.

- Not verified this session:
  - GIMF's exact household structure (OLG planning horizon, share of liquidity-constrained agents), tax instruments and fiscal-rule equation.
  - Whether WEO 2014 used GIMF with α_G = 0.17 for AEs, and which values it used for EMs and LIDCs.
  - The WEO 2014 empirical multipliers (often quoted as about 0.4% of GDP in year 1 and about 1.5% after four years per 1 pp of GDP).
  - The authorship of WP/13/55 and WP/15/95.
  - The authorship of NBER w27625 (believed to be Ramey 2020).

## 6. Cross-model treatment of capital vs recurrent spending, maintenance/O&M, revenue returns, PPPs, and public-capital elasticity

### Takeaway
In the DIG family, public **investment** builds effective capital (scaled by s and absorptive capacity). **Recurrent costs** are a fixed ratio to capital and are partly recovered through user fees. In DIG-Labor, **maintenance** lowers depreciation. **Revenue returns** come from user fees plus a larger tax base from higher output. GIMF treats investment as productive and consumption as unproductive. **PPPs** sit outside these models and are handled by PFRAM (2016; 2.0 in 2019/2020). Elasticity calibration rests on Bom & Ligthart (2014): 0.083 short run and 0.122 long run (central-government level), with higher values for core infrastructure.

### Cited Findings
- (i)–(iii) Capital vs recurrent spending, user fees and maintenance in DIG: user fees are a fixed multiple of recurrent costs, and absorptive capacity works through cost overruns. — [IMF WP/12/144](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12144.pdf). Guinea DIG: fees cover 50% of recurrent costs. — [IMF CR 16/262](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/scr/2016/cr16262.pdf). DIG-Labor: maintenance spending lowers infrastructure depreciation. — [IMF WP/20/102](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2020/english/wpiea2020102-print-pdf.pdf)
- The 2018 PIMA update gives more weight to **maintenance**, reflecting chronic under-funding of O&M. — [IMF PIMA Review and Update 2018](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Policy-Papers/Issues/2018/05/10/pp042518public-investment-management-assessment-review-and-update)
- Revenue returns: adaptation investment raises long-run **tax revenue** alongside output and private investment (Dominica DSGE). — [IMF WP/23/138](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2023/06/30/The-Macroeconomic-Returns-of-Investment-in-Resilience-to-Natural-Disasters-under-Climate-534579)
- (iv) **PPPs and PFRAM:** the IMF and World Bank Group developed PFRAM to estimate the fiscal costs and risks of PPP projects. It launched in **April 2016** and is used in IMF and WBG TA and by PPP units. Outputs include **DSA with and without the PPP projects**, sensitivity to macro and project parameters, and a project risk matrix. The **PFRAM 2.0 User Manual** is dated September 2019, and the WB PPP Resource Center lists 2.0 as released in 2020. — [IMF PFM Blog (Queyranne, Rial, Verdier, June 2016)](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2016/06/posted-by-maximilien-queyranne-isabel-rial-and-genevieve-verdier-1-the-pfram-dev); [PFRAM summary (library mirror)](https://0-www-imf-org.library.svsu.edu/external/np/fad/publicinvestment/pdf/PFRAM.pdf); [PFRAM 2.0 User Manual](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/PFRAM%202.0%20User%20Manual.pdf); [WB PPP Resource Center](https://ppp.worldbank.org/node/5841); [PPIAF PFRAM 2.0 Guidance Note](https://ppiaf.org/documents/5783)
- (v) **Elasticity meta-analysis:** Bom & Ligthart, "What Have We Learned From Three Decades of Research on the Productivity of Public Capital?", *Journal of Economic Surveys* 28(5): 889–916 (2014). It pools 578 estimates from 68 studies (1983–2008). Short-run output elasticity of central-government public capital is **0.083**, rising to **0.122** in the long run, and the overall average is **0.106**. Core infrastructure at regional or local level roughly doubles the estimates. Public capital appears undersupplied in OECD economies. — [Univ. of Vienna portal](https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/what-have-we-learned-from-three-decades-of-research-on-the-produc/); [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/20825-what-have-we-learned-from-three-decades-of-research-on-the-productivity-of-public-capital)
- Developing-country marginal product: the MPK of public capital is higher in LICs than in MICs. — [Gupta et al. 2014 (EconPapers)](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/imfimfwpa/2011_2f217.htm). DIG-family LIC net return calibrations are 20–25%. — [IMF WP/15/272](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2015/wp15272.pdf)

### Inferences
- Combined-tool rule: use **ψ (elasticity)** for AE/GIMF-style blocks, anchored on Bom–Ligthart and the 0.17 core-infrastructure figure. Use **R (return) with s** for LIC/DIG-style blocks. Convert between them with ψ = (R + δ)·z/Y. The two are consistent only if z/Y is measured in efficiency units.
- PPPs: the macro model needs government-pays PPP availability payments as recurrent spending (not capex). In user-pays PPPs the fee stream accrues to the private partner, so f does not accrue to the budget. Guarantees and PPA take-or-pay obligations become contingent liabilities, to be stress-tested via PFRAM-type DSA "with and without PPP".
- Maintenance: DIG-Labor's δ(M/z) and the PIMA maintenance emphasis justify passing an **O&M funding ratio** from CBA. Under-funded O&M can be modelled as higher δ.

## 7. Implications for a CBA-to-macro bridge (what sector-level information a CBA layer could pass to DIG/DIGNAR/DIGNAD/GIMF)

### Takeaway
A sector CBA layer should pass a small, model-agnostic parameter vector per sector j. The core items are efficiency s_j, gross or net return R_j (market-output-relevant only), user-fee recovery f_j, recurrent-cost ratio μ_j, depreciation and maintenance response δ_j(M), traded/non-traded and import content, absorptive-capacity thresholds, and the timing of spending. Climate-specific add-ons are the resilience premium and avoided-damage share (DIGNAD), fuel-import savings and resource-revenue paths (DIGNAR), and PPP payment and guarantee profiles (PFRAM). Aggregated to an economy-wide s, ψ and f, these can drive any of the four models.

### Cited Findings
- DIG-family models require s (spending-to-capital efficiency), a return or elasticity, user fees as a share of recurrent costs, absorptive-capacity cost overruns, debt mix (concessional, commercial, domestic) and fiscal reaction parameters. — [IMF WP/12/144](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12144.pdf); [IMF CR 16/262](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/scr/2016/cr16262.pdf); [IMF WP/13/133](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2013/wp13133.pdf)
- DIGNAD requires the split of standard vs adaptation infrastructure, adaptation effectiveness (damage mitigation per unit of capital), disaster magnitude, and country-specific national-accounts parameters. — [IMF TNM 2023/003](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/005/2023/003/article-A001-en.xml)
- DIGNAR requires resource-revenue paths, fund rules and absorptive-capacity thresholds; for example, efficiency falls from 0.5 to 0.35 above 1.6× average investment. — [DIGNAR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264999315002898)
- GIMF-type analysis requires the public-capital output elasticity (e.g., 0.17 for core infrastructure in IMF WP/15/95 baseline; meta-analytic 0.083–0.122). — [IMF WP/15/95](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Knowledge-Hub/Publications/pubdocuments/The%20Macroeconomic%20Effects%20of%20Public%20Investment%20Evidence%20from%20Advanced%20Economies.pdf); [Bom & Ligthart 2014](https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/what-have-we-learned-from-three-decades-of-research-on-the-produc/)
- Efficiency priors are available at country-group level (PIE-X gaps 13/27/40%) and at institutional level (PIMA and C-PIMA scores). — [IMF 2015](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/Making%20Public%20Investment%20More%20Efficient%202015.pdf); [C-PIMA Handbook 2025](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/books/issues/2025/11/10/c-pima-handbook-climate-public-investment-management-assessment-559283)

**Design notes for the bridge:**
- **One efficiency concept.** Pass s_j and R_j (per unit of effective capital), never "R per $ spent" as well. The DIG-family literature shows growth outcomes depend on whether ψ or R is held fixed, so declare which one the CBA anchors. The Berg et al. 2019 result shows that holding ψ fixed makes s irrelevant to dY/dI, while holding R fixed makes payoffs proportional to s.
- **Split CBA benefits into four buckets:**
  - (a) market-output (→ R/ψ);
  - (b) fiscal cash flows (→ f, μ, tax base);
  - (c) avoided damages and imports (→ DIGNAD damage parameters, DIGNAR or trade paths);
  - (d) non-market welfare (stays in CBA; reported next to macro results).
  
  Without this split, macro-fiscal benefits are double counted.
- **Aggregation.** The macro models have a single public-capital stock, or two in DIGNAD. Aggregate the sector parameters with capital-stock weights:
  - s̄ = Σ w_j s_j;
  - ψ̄ = Σ ψ_j;
  - f̄ = Σ (μ_j z_j f_j) / Σ (μ_j z_j).
  
  Assign resilient-design spending to DIGNAD's adaptation stock.
- **Fiscal space.** The macro model returns the debt path, the required tax or transfer adjustment (λ path) and the RER. CBA projects can then be re-ranked on fiscal cost per unit of net benefit under the macro-consistent shadow price of public funds.

### Gaps
- No IMF document was found that specifies a formal **CBA-to-DIG parameter interface**. The table above is a design proposal built on verified model inputs.
- Sector-specific recurrent-cost ratios, import shares and resilience premia need non-IMF sources (e.g., World Bank infrastructure O&M benchmarks), which were outside this note's scope and search budget.
