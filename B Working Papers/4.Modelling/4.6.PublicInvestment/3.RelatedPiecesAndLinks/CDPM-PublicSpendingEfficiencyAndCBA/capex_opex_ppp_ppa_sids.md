## 1. Capital vs recurrent spending: the recurrent-cost problem, maintenance returns, and how to put capex and opex on one footing

### Takeaway
There is a long, consistent literature on the recurrent-cost problem: Heller (1974) on Kenya, then donor-biased project design, and r-coefficient budgeting by the World Bank (Hood et al. 2002). Macro models and road-sector evidence show that under-funding O&M destroys capital value. Timely road maintenance costs 3–5× less than later rehabilitation (Harral & Faiz 1988), and good maintenance can cut life-cycle costs of transport and water assets by more than 50% (Rozenberg & Fay 2019). Capex and opex are made comparable by annuitising capex with a capital recovery factor (equivalent annual cost / LCOE). On a public-sector balance sheet, asset-creating capex is treated differently from consumption.

### Cited Findings
**Recurrent-cost problem**
- Peter S. Heller, "Public Investment in LDC's with Recurrent Cost Constraint: The Kenyan Case," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 88(2), 1974, pp. 251–277. This is the founding formal treatment: investment choice is constrained by the ability to fund the recurrent costs that projects generate [P] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v88y1974i2p251-277..html)
- A later IMF Working Paper (WP/14/73, on public investment, distortionary taxation, recurrent costs and incomplete appropriability; IGC version by Adam & Bevan 2014) cites Heller (1974) as an important early contribution and builds recurrent costs into a macro model of public investment [2nd] — [IDEAS IMF WP 2014/073](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2014-073.html); [IGC working paper PDF](https://www.theigc.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Adam-Bevan-2014-Working-Paper.pdf); [UK R4D record](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/public-investment-public-finance-and-growth-the-impact-of-distortionary-taxation-recurrent-costs-and-incomplete-appropriability)
- One strand explains the recurrent-cost problem as the result of donor-biased project design: donors finance capex, recipients must fund the O&M ("On the care and feeding of a gift horse: the recurrent cost problem as the result of biased project design," Boston University) [P] — [open.bu.edu](https://open.bu.edu/items/c10d953d-c00f-4ca9-9777-b0da8743a44d). Further background: Over (1981), MPRA paper 10405 on recurrent-cost problems in aid [P] — [MPRA](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/10405/1/MPRA_paper_10405.pdf); and a Pacific overview, "Recurrent cost financing: an overview" (Pacific Economic Bulletin, 1998) [P] — [Devpolicy/PEB archive](https://devpolicy.org/PEB/2019/05/23/recurrent-cost-financing-an-overview/)
- Hood, Husband & Yu (2002), "Recurrent Expenditure Requirements of Capital Projects: Estimation for Budget Purposes," World Bank Policy Research WP 2938. Its purpose is to estimate the recurrent costs that capital projects create, to avoid "chronic underfunding of maintenance and operating costs" in developing economies. It has three parts: concepts and definitions, stylised project examples, and rough empirical guidance from a sample of actual projects (i.e., r-coefficients) [P] — [IDEAS WPS 2938](https://ideas.repec.org:443/p/wbk/wbrwps/2938.html); [OKR record](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/signposting/describedby/6ed4d6ee-120e-5a21-84f2-b1356e02ea34)

**Evidence on under-maintenance and its returns**
- Rioja (2003), "Filling potholes: macroeconomic effects of maintenance versus new investments in public infrastructure," *J. Public Economics* 87(9–10): 2281–2304. Developing countries have neglected maintenance in favour of new construction. Optimal maintenance depends on new investment relative to the existing stock and on infrastructure productivity. In a calibration to Latin American countries, reallocating funds from new infrastructure to maintenance raises GDP [P] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/pubeco/v87y2003i9-10p2281-2304.html)
- Kalaitzidakis & Kalyvitis (2004), "On the macroeconomic implications of maintenance in public capital," *J. Public Economics* 88(3–4): 695–712. Durability of public capital is endogenous to usage and maintenance spending. Total spending and the maintenance share both matter for the steady state and the dynamics [P] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v88y2004i3-4p695-712.html). A companion paper on Canada (Economic Inquiry 2005) finds gains from reallocating between new public investment and maintenance [P] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/ecinqu/v43y2005i3p586-600.html)
- Devarajan, Swaroop & Zou (1996), "The composition of public expenditure and economic growth," *J. Monetary Economics* 37(2): 313–344 (43 developing countries, 20 years in the journal version; WPS 1082 used 69 countries). The capital component of spending has a **negative** relation with per-capita growth, and current spending a positive, significant one. The authors read this as "productive" spending becoming unproductive when used to excess [P] — [EconPapers WPS 1082](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/1082.htm); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3932(96)01249-4)
- Harral & Faiz (1988), *Road Deterioration in Developing Countries: Causes and Remedies* (World Bank policy study, 85 countries' main road networks). Rehabilitation backlog "more than $40 billion" (excluding bridges and minor roads). Future maintenance of **$4–5 billion per year** would stop it growing. "The cost of restoring these deteriorated roads is **three to five times greater** than the bill would have been for timely and effective maintenance" [P] — [TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/282593); [World Bank Documents](https://documents.banquemondiale.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/722851468162562433)
- Rozenberg & Fay (2019), *Beyond the Gap* (World Bank). Infrastructure funding needs of LMICs range from **2% to 8% of GDP a year to 2030**, depending on goals and policies. A policy mix can meet the key goals while keeping new-infrastructure spending at **4.5% of GDP a year**. "Ensuring a steady flow of resources for operations and maintenance is a necessary condition." Good maintenance reduces "the total life-cycle cost of transport and water and sanitation infrastructure by **more than 50 percent**" [P] — [World Bank OKR 10986/31291](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/31291)

This is an **annuity-due** factor (payments at the start of each year), and the module divides capex by it — [local: /home/user/CPAT-AI/cpat_excel/working_version/PowerModel_Method_v6_1.md, line 41](file:///home/user/CPAT-AI/cpat_excel/working_version/PowerModel_Method_v6_1.md). ### Inferences
- **Capital recovery factor (end-of-year annuity):** CRF(r,n) = r(1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1). Equivalent annual cost: EAC = CRF·K + O&M. LCOE = (CRF·K + fixed O&M)/E + variable O&M + fuel/E. CPAT's annuity-due factor equals (1+r)/CRF, so CPAT's annual capex charge equals CRF·K/(1+r). That is 8–11% lower than the end-of-year convention at r = 8–11%. The tool should choose one convention and document it.
- **Recurrent-cost coefficient approach:** recurrent cost in year t = r_s × cumulative capex in sector s still in service. The fiscal envelope check is Σ_s r_s·K_s,t ≤ the available recurrent budget. Combined with the CRF, the "true annual cost" of a project is CRF·K + r·K, and the budget must fund both lines.
- The maintenance evidence implies O&M cuts are a false saving. A 3–5× rehabilitation multiplier (roads) means that deferring $1 of maintenance converts into $3–5 of later capex. The tool can model under-maintenance as accelerated depreciation (shorter n) or as a later rehabilitation lump.
- Balance-sheet logic: debt-financed capex that creates an asset with matching value leaves net worth roughly unchanged at t0. Net worth changes only by NPV minus the over-payment, and by depreciation net of maintenance. Current spending reduces net worth one-for-one. This is the formal basis for not treating a self-financing energy investment like consumption, *provided* the asset is valued realistically and maintained.

## 2. PPPs: fiscal economics, accounting, and when they add value

### Takeaway
Engel, Fischer & Galetovic show that PPPs do not "free up" public funds under the intertemporal budget constraint. Any case for them rests on efficiency (bundling, incentives, risk allocation). The "fiscal illusion" arises when PPP liabilities are left out of debt and deficit statistics. Eurostat/ESA 2010 keeps a PPP off the government balance sheet only if the partner bears construction risk plus availability or demand risk. IPSAS 32 brings the asset and a matching liability onto the grantor's balance sheet when the grantor controls the asset. Private finance costs more than sovereign borrowing (UK: a premium of about 2–3.75 pp). Efficiency gains must exceed this premium.

### Cited Findings
- Engel, Fischer & Galetovic (2013), "The Basic Public Finance of Public–Private Partnerships," *JEEA* 11(1): 83–111 (NBER WP 13284, 2007). The paper rejects the idea that PPPs are justified because they free public funds. Where PPPs are justified on efficiency grounds, the optimal contract has a minimum revenue guarantee and a revenue cap, can be run through a competitive auction, and in risk allocation looks more like public provision than privatisation [P] — [IDEAS JEEA](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jeurec/v11y2013i1p83-111.html); [NBER w13284](https://nber.org/papers/w13284)
- Engel, Fischer & Galetovic (2014), *The Economics of Public-Private Partnerships: A Basic Guide* (Cambridge UP, 176 pp.). PPPs have a fiscal footprint like conventional provision and are not a source of extra public money. "Pervasive renegotiations, faulty fiscal accounting, and poor governance" threaten the model. The justification is efficiency and service gains, which "often prove elusive" [P] — [World Bank PPP library](https://ppp.worldbank.org/index.php/library/economics-public-private-partnerships-basic-guide); [Lavoisier listing](https://www.lavoisier.fr/livre/autre/the-economics-of-public-private-partnerships/engel/descriptif_3081386)
- IMF/World Bank **PFRAM** (PPP Fiscal Risk Assessment Model; v2.0 2019) is a spreadsheet tool. It estimates a PPP's impact on the deficit, debt and stock of contingent liabilities, with sensitivity analysis. It covers debt guarantees, minimum revenue guarantees, termination clauses and PPAs [P] — [PPIAF PFRAM 2.0](https://ppiaf.org/documents/5782); [IMF CD page](https://www.imf.org/external/np/ins/English/capacity_countries_pf_global.htm); [IMF PFM blog 2016](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2016/06/posted-by-maximilien-queyranne-isabel-rial-and-genevieve-verdier-1-the-pfram-dev); [PFRAM 2.0 user manual](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/PFRAM%202.0%20User%20Manual.pdf); see also [World Bank "Fiscal risk in PPPs" blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/ppps/fiscal-risk-ppps-whats-problem-what-do) and [Quick Read: Fiscal Risk in PPPs](https://ppp.worldbank.org/public-private-partnership/sites/default/files/2022-04/Quick-Read-InfraFinance_Fiscal_Risk_in_PPPs.pdf)
- **Eurostat/EPEC (Sept 2016), *A Guide to the Statistical Treatment of PPPs*** (complements ESA 2010 and SNA 2008). It identifies construction, availability and demand risk. To be off the government balance sheet, the contract must transfer construction risk **and** either availability or demand risk ("most of the risk"). Reward factors also count. An Irish parliamentary critique argued the guide makes "any part of the profit" accruing to government sufficient for on-balance-sheet treatment (contested) [P/2nd] — [Eurostat/EPEC guide PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/1015035/7204121/epec-eurostat-statistical-guide-en.pdf); [World Bank PPP library entry](https://ppp.worldbank.org/public-private-partnership/library/guide-statistical-treatment-ppps-epec-2016); [Oireachtas debate 2016](https://www.oireachtas.ie/en/debates/question/2016-06-21/323/)
- **IPSAS 32** (Service Concession Arrangements: Grantor) has two models. In the *financial liability model* the grantor pays cash for control of the asset. In the *grant of a right to the operator model*, users pay. "Where the grantor recognises a service concession asset, the grantor also recognises a liability." The liability is initially measured at the asset's value, adjusted for other consideration. This mirrors IFRIC 12 [P/2nd] — [BDO PBE IPSAS 32 summary](https://www.bdo.nz/getmedia/feb0a910-1f53-4a93-ba7f-4fe1067124a9/BDO-SOAP-PS-PBE-IPSAS-32.pdf); [IFAC IPSAS 32 Q&A](https://www.ifac.org/system/files/publications/files/IPSASB-32-Q-A-Document.pdf); [IMF GFSAC presentation (Carruthers 2017)](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/gfs/gfsac/meetings/2017/pdf/Carruthers4.2.pdf)
- **UK PFI evidence (NAO, Jan 2018, "PFI and PF2")**: 716 operational deals, an annual charge of £10.3 bn in 2016/17, capital value about £60 bn, and **£199 bn** of future payments even with no new deals. Little evidence that PFI gives more certainty on construction outturn cost. The cost of finance is **2–3.75% higher** than government borrowing. Coverage differs on whether this range is the NAO's own estimate or the NAO quoting HM Treasury's 2010 National Infrastructure Plan. A DfE analysis cited by the NAO found PF2 schools cost **~40% more** than if financed by government borrowing [2nd] — [Construction Enquirer](https://www.constructionenquirer.com/2018/01/18/nao-challenges-value-of-pfi-contracts/); [ITV](https://itv.com/news/2018-01-18/taxpayers-will-need-to-pay-200bn-pfi-bill-says-watchdog); [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/spotlight/2018/01/national-audit-office-puts-uk-s-pfi-bill-nearly-200bn); [TES (PF2 schools 40%)](https://tes.com/news/schools-cost-40-cent-more-build-under-pfi-watchdog-reveals)
- Blanc-Brude, Goldsmith & Välilä (2006), EIB Economic and Financial Report 2006/01, "Ex Ante Construction Costs in the European Road Sector: A Comparison of PPPs and Traditional Public Procurement". Theory predicts higher PPP construction costs because bundling gives incentives to invest up front to lower later O&M (the whole-life-cost argument) [P, abstract only] — [EIB EFR 2006/01](https://www.eib.org/files/efs/efr_2006_v01_en.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The fiscal-neutrality condition:** a PPP availability or PPA payment stream P_t replaces public capex K plus O&M. Under the intertemporal budget constraint, PV(P_t at r_g) ≥ K + PV(O&M) unless the private party is more efficient or bears risks the government would otherwise bear. The financing premium, PV(P at r_g) − (K + PV(O&M)), is the price of risk transfer plus any efficiency gain. If the gains are smaller than the premium, the PPP destroys value even though it "saves" t0 debt.
- PPPs genuinely add value when: (i) bundling build-operate lowers whole-life cost (the EIB abstract's mechanism); (ii) construction and performance risk are really transferred (payment only for delivered availability or energy); (iii) competitive tendering reveals cost (Scaling Solar, ASPIRE; see section 3); (iv) private O&M is more reliable than a liquidity-constrained public utility (links to the recurrent-cost problem, since a PPA "ring-fences" O&M funding).
- Accounting: an IPP selling energy under a long-term PPA to a state utility is economically a service concession. Whether it goes on balance sheet depends on control and residual interest (IPSAS 32) or on risk transfer (Eurostat). For DSAs, the LIC-DSF treats it through the PPP contingent-liability shock (section 3).

### Gaps
- The often-cited Blanc-Brude et al. headline (PPP roads ex ante construction costs **~24% higher** than traditional procurement, similar to average traditional cost overruns) was **not verified** (full text blocked). Candidate value; verify.
- World Bank *PPP Reference Guide v3* (2017) and the World Bank **PFCL** tool / "Implementing a Framework for Managing Fiscal Commitments from PPPs" were **not reached**. IMF GFSM 2014 "economic ownership" criteria were not checked.
- Systematic evidence on PPP cost-of-capital premia in EMDEs (as opposed to the UK) was not found.

## 3. PPAs and IPPs in power: take-or-pay, guarantees, DSA treatment, and cautionary cases

### Takeaway
Take-or-pay PPAs convert generation capex into long-term fixed payments by a (usually state-owned, often weak) off-taker. Competitive auctions with guarantees (Scaling Solar, Maldives ASPIRE) have delivered low, fixed tariffs. Badly sized, capacity-heavy PPAs, as in Ghana, Kenya and Pakistan, have become large hidden fiscal liabilities. The LIC-DSF captures PPPs and PPAs through a standard contingent-liability shock of **35% of the PPP capital stock**, triggered when the stock exceeds 3% of GDP, plus a 2%-of-GDP SOE shock. Utility quasi-fiscal deficits in SSA average about 1.5% of GDP.

**How DSAs treat PPPs, PPAs and SOEs**
- LIC-DSF (2018 framework; Guidance Note Dec 2017). The default contingent-liability stress test adds **35% of the PPP capital stock** to PPG debt, triggered when the PPP capital stock exceeds **3% of GDP**. The SOE debt shock is **2% of GDP**, reducible to 0 if SOE debt is already in coverage and residual risk is negligible. Other components include a 5%-of-GDP financial-market shock and natural disasters [P via DSAs] — [LIC-DSF Guidance Note (WB)](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/513741518471205237/pdf/LIC-DSF-SGN-2017-Clean-Feb0718-02082018.pdf); [IMF Guidance Note pp122617](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/pp/2017/pp122617guidance-note-on-lic-dsf.pdf.md); [IMF LIC-DSF review pp082217](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/pp/2017/pp082217lic-dsf.pdf.md)
  - Examples: Ghana DSA, PPP shock = 35% of PPP stock = **1.44% of GDP**, total contingent-liability shock 9.4% of GDP [P] — [Ghana DSA](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/973971632340489819/pdf/Ghana-Joint-World-Bank-IMF-Debt-Sustainability-Analysis.pdf). ### Inferences
- **What separates the good from the bad PPA cases** is not "PPA vs public" but (a) competitive price discovery, (b) sizing to demand (Ghana and Kenya over-contracted dispatchable capacity), (c) payment structure, and (d) credible off-taker payment security. On (c), solar PPAs typically pay per kWh delivered plus "deemed energy" for curtailment or grid outages, whereas thermal PPAs carry large fixed capacity charges. Solar or wind "take-and-pay" structures carry much less unconditional liability than thermal take-or-pay. (General industry structure, not verified in this session.)
- A World Bank/IDA or MIGA guarantee reduces the IPP's WACC and so the tariff. It also creates a contingent claim of the sovereign on itself (an IDA payment guarantee is ultimately backed by a sovereign indemnity), so it does not remove the fiscal exposure. It prices and shares it.
- **DSA treatment is coarse.** The 35%-of-PPP-capital-stock shock is a blunt stress, not a measure of the PV of committed payments. A SIDS with a $120 m solar-plus-storage IPP (about 6% of GDP in the worked example) would trigger the shock: 0.35 × 6% ≈ **2.1% of GDP** added to PPG debt in the stress scenario.

## 5. The portfolio/hedging argument

### Takeaway
Awerbuch and Berger apply mean-variance portfolio theory to generation mixes. Fixed-cost, fuel-free renewables lower portfolio cost risk even when their stand-alone cost is higher. For an oil-importing SIDS, each $10/bbl swing moves the diesel fuel bill by roughly 7–8% (worked example). A fixed-price PPA or a debt-financed solar asset swaps that volatility for a fixed obligation.

### Cited Findings
- Awerbuch & Berger (2003), "Applying Portfolio Theory to EU Electricity Planning and Policy-Making," IEA/EET Working Paper EET/2003/03 (IEA/OECD, Paris). Berger's TU Wien dissertation (2003), "Portfolio analysis of EU electricity generating mixes and its implications for renewables," was written at the IEA. In the method, the inverse of unit cost (kWh/€) is the "return", and fuel-driven cost fluctuation is the "risk" [P/2nd] — [TU Wien repositum](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/186168); [IAEE proceedings abstract](https://www.iaee.org/en/publications/proceedingsabstractpdf.aspx?id=229); [Austrian ministry project report](https://nachhaltigwirtschaften.at/resources/nw_pdf/0302_versorgungssicherheit.pdf)
- Awerbuch & Yang (2007), EIB Papers 12(2), applied the approach to the projected 2020 EU business-as-usual mix. They found portfolios that cut **cost, market risk and CO2** relative to BAU [P] — [EIB Papers 2007 v12 n2](https://www02.eib.org/attachments/efs/eibpapers/eibpapers_2007_v12_n02_en.pdf)
- Applied example: a portfolio-theory assessment of the South African electricity mix [P, title] — [J. Energy in Southern Africa](https://journals.assaf.org.za/jesa/article/view/1545)

### Inferences
- **Quantified for the worked example:** with the derived fuel-cost formula, dSRMC/dBrent ≈ $0.0016/kWh per $1/bbl. For 300 GWh of diesel generation that is about **$0.48 m per $1/bbl**, or **$4.8 m per $10/bbl** (about 0.24% of an assumed $2 bn GDP). Displacing 90 GWh removes about 30% of that exposure.
- For the tool, the hedge value can be included as (a) a risk premium on the diesel counterfactual (certainty-equivalent fuel price above the mean forecast), or (b) the expected cost of fuel-price-shock-induced fiscal adjustment, using the variance of Brent and the fuel-cost pass-through to budget or utility. Either way, the fixed-cost asset's value rises with fuel-price volatility and with the share of fuel costs that falls on the budget.

## 6. The "trap": self-financing but blocked investments, and how DSAs treat them

### Takeaway
In the worked example, a solar+storage project financed by 100% debt with an annuity loan matched to asset life is **cash-flow positive from year 1** (fuel savings > debt service + O&M). The binding constraints are the debt **stock** (DSA thresholds, ratings, market access) and the **cost** of capital, not liquidity. A PPA removes the t0 debt jump but creates a PV obligation about 1.3–1.5× the capex at the sovereign rate. Concessional finance (grant element about 40% at a 5% discount rate) dominates both. DSAs give little explicit credit to an investment's own fiscal returns. The 2026 LIC-DSF review is the venue where that could change (roll-out 2027).

- **Reading the trap.** Route A is cash-flow positive from year 1, so the binding constraint is a debt-stock rule or DSA threshold (a 6%-of-GDP jump), a rating or market-access problem, or an 8%+ borrowing cost. ## 7. Revenue returns from public assets: fiscal NPV vs economic NPV, and quasi-fiscal channels

### Takeaway
Economic NPV counts resource savings (fuel, O&M, avoided externalities) at shadow prices, whoever captures them. Fiscal NPV counts only flows through the public sector's accounts: budget, plus SOE utility if consolidated. They diverge through tariff pass-through, fuel taxes and subsidies, and utility losses. In SSA, utility quasi-fiscal deficits average 1.5% of GDP, so utility finances are a major fiscal channel.

### Cited Findings
- Kojima & Trimble (2016): of 39 SSA power sectors, only 2 were financially viable and 19 covered opex. QFDs average **1.5% of GDP** and exceed **5%** in several countries. Network and collection losses outweigh under-pricing as hidden costs [P] — [World Bank](https://documents.banquemondiale.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/293531475067040608); [WPS 7788](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/pt/182071470748085038/pdf/WPS7788.pdf)
- The IMF *Fiscal Monitor* (Oct 2018) public-sector balance sheet includes public corporations' assets (**120% of GDP** in the sample). This supports consolidating SOE utilities when assessing the fiscal effects of energy investments [P/2nd] — [IMF FM Oct 2018](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/FM/Issues/2018/10/04/fiscal-monitor-october-2018)
- PPA capacity charges are borne first by the utility and then by the budget via arrears, bailouts or circular debt (Ghana, Kenya, Pakistan; section 3) [2nd] — [Ghana PPA study](https://rise.esmap.org/data/files/library/ghana/Renewable%20Energy/Ghana_PPA%20study_2021.pdf); [Nukta on Pakistan](https://nukta.com/imf-approves-pakistan-circular-debt-plan)

### Inferences
- **Fiscal NPV** = Σ_t [θ_t·(fuel savings_t) + Δtax_t − debt service_t (or PPA payments × utility-to-budget pass-through) − O&M_t − contingent-liability calls_t] / (1+r_g)^t. Here θ is the share of fuel costs borne by the public sector (budget subsidy plus SOE losses), and Δtax is the change in fuel-excise or import-duty revenue, often negative when diesel imports fall unless power-sector fuel is exempt.
- **Economic NPV** = Σ_t [fuel resource savings at border prices + O&M savings + avoided CO2/local pollution + hedge value − capex − O&M] / (1+SDR)^t. Financing flows are excluded, except that a foreign-exchange shadow price may apply in import-dependent SIDS.
- A project can have a high economic NPV but a low fiscal NPV, for example under cost-reflective tariffs, where consumers gain. The reverse occurs when fuel subsidies are large. The tool should show both, plus a third view: the **utility/SOE NPV**.

### Gaps
- No SIDS-specific QFD estimates were retrieved (e.g., for Pacific utilities such as PPL, EPC and TPL, or Caribbean ones such as LUCELEC, BL&P, JPS).
- The treatment of foreign-exchange savings (shadow exchange rate) in SIDS appraisal was not researched.

## 8. Implications for the tool (formulas, fiscal-flow timing, liability recognition, default parameters)

### Takeaway
Implement a three-lens appraisal (economic NPV, fiscal NPV, debt and liability profile) with explicit annuitisation (CRF), an r-coefficient O&M module, and a financing-route switch (public commercial / concessional / IPP-PPA ± guarantee). The route switch should produce (i) annual net fiscal flows, (ii) headline debt, and (iii) "debt-like" PPA liabilities, valued as the PV of committed payments at the sovereign rate, plus the LIC-DSF 35% stress. Default SIDS parameters are below, with sources in sections 1–7.

### Cited Findings
- The current CPAT power model annuitises capex with an annuity-due "discounted lifetime" factor and treats PPAs only as must-run thermal generation — [local PowerModel_Method_v6_1.md](file:///home/user/CPAT-AI/cpat_excel/working_version/PowerModel_Method_v6_1.md)
- The LIC-DSF PPP shock is 35% of the PPP capital stock when the stock exceeds 3% of GDP, and the SOE shock is 2% of GDP — [LIC-DSF Guidance Note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/513741518471205237/pdf/LIC-DSF-SGN-2017-Clean-Feb0718-02082018.pdf)
- IPSAS 32 recognises the asset and a matching liability (at the asset's value) when the grantor controls the asset. Eurostat's on/off test is construction risk plus availability or demand risk — [BDO IPSAS 32](https://www.bdo.nz/getmedia/feb0a910-1f53-4a93-ba7f-4fe1067124a9/BDO-SOAP-PS-PBE-IPSAS-32.pdf); [Eurostat/EPEC guide](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/1015035/7204121/epec-eurostat-statistical-guide-en.pdf)
- PFRAM is the reference IMF/WB tool for PPP fiscal flows, debt and contingent liabilities — [PPIAF PFRAM 2.0](https://ppiaf.org/documents/5782)

### Inferences: formulas

1. **Annuitising capex**
   - CRF(r,n) = r(1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1). Annual capital charge A = CRF(r,n)·K, with end-of-year payments.
   - Annuity-due (CPAT convention): A_due = K·CRF(r,n)/(1+r). Pick one convention; recommend the end-of-year CRF for fiscal flows, since debt service is paid in arrears.
   - Components with different lives: A = Σ_j CRF(r,n_j)·K_j (e.g., PV n=25, BESS n=15, inverters n=12–15).
   - Equivalent annual cost: EAC = A + O&M_fixed + O&M_var·E + fuel·E. LCOE = EAC/E.
   - Reference CRFs: CRF(1.5%,25)≈0.048; CRF(4%,25)=0.064; CRF(8%,25)=0.094; CRF(8%,20)=0.102; CRF(11%,20)=0.126; CRF(13%,20)=0.142; CRF(8%,15)=0.117.

2. **Recurrent cost (r-coefficient) module**
   - O&M_t = Σ_s r_s·K_s (in service at t). Recurrent budget check: O&M_t ≤ recurrent envelope_t. Under-funding by u_t (share unfunded) shortens life: n' = n·(1 − λ·u) or adds a rehabilitation lump of m·u·(deferred O&M), with m ≈ 3–5 (Harral & Faiz road evidence).
   - Defaults: r_PV ≈ 1–2% of capex a year; r_BESS ≈ 2–3%; diesel non-fuel O&M ≈ $0.02/kWh (assumptions; see Gaps).

3. **PPA conversion (capex → opex)**
   - Levelised PPA tariff: τ = [CRF(WACC_IPP, T_PPA)·(K + PV_WACC(replacements)) + O&M] / E_contracted.
   - Guarantee effect: WACC_IPP,g = WACC_IPP − Δ_g (default Δ_g = 2 pp; assumption).
   - Annual payment P_t = τ·E_t (as-available "take-and-pay") or τ·max(E_t, E_min) (take-or-pay). Add deemed-energy payments for curtailment and grid unavailability, and capacity charges for firm-capacity PPAs.

4. **Fiscal-flow timing by route** (per year t; θ = public share of fuel cost; π = pass-through of PPA cost to the budget)
   - Public, borrowed: F_t = θ·S_t − DS_t(K, r_loan, maturity, grace) − O&M_t − Repl_t; ΔDebt_0 = +K.
   - Concessional: same, with concessional DS_t. Report the grant element GE = 1 − PV_{r_ref}(DS)/K (reference r_ref = 5%; to be verified, see Gaps).
   - IPP-PPA: F_t = θ·S_t − π·P_t; ΔDebt_0 = 0 on the headline measure; contingent and committed liabilities below.
   - Here S_t = E_t·(c_fuel(Brent_t) + c_vom,diesel) is the avoided diesel cost.

5. **Liability recognition (debt-like PPA)**
   - Committed-payment liability: L_PPA,t = Σ_{k=t+1}^{T} P_k^{committed}/(1+r_g)^{k−t}. P^committed = capacity or fixed charges plus minimum take, or expected deemed payments for as-available solar.
   - IPSAS-32-style on-balance-sheet: if the grantor controls the asset (utility off-taker, asset transfers at end, regulated tariff), recognise asset = liability = K at t0 and amortise. The "service" component of the payment is excluded.
   - LIC-DSF stress: if PPP capital stock > 3% GDP, ΔPPG_stress = 0.35·PPP capital stock (year 2 of the stress scenario).
   - Report the **financing premium** = L_PPA,0 − K (worked example: +$37–55 m on $120 m).
   - Termination liability (PFRAM): PV of outstanding project debt plus equity compensation on government-default termination. Approximate it as the outstanding IPP debt balance.

6. **Hedge value (optional):** HV = E·(CE fuel cost − mean fuel cost), with the certainty-equivalent from Brent volatility and a public risk-aversion parameter. Alternatively, report the fiscal sensitivity per $10/bbl.
