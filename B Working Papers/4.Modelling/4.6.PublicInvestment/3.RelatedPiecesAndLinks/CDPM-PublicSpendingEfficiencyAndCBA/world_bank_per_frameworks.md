## 1. What a PER / PFR covers today: the three-level framework and the PER→PFR shift (official guidance, sector PER guides, climate PER tools)

### Takeaway
The three-level framework still organizes WB thinking: aggregate fiscal discipline, allocative efficiency, and technical/operational efficiency. It comes from Schick 1998 and Campos & Pradhan, and the Bank's first PER guidance was Pradhan 1996.

Since 2024 the Bank has replaced the PER with the **Public Finance Review (PFR)**. The PFR adds a revenue chapter, is now a *core mandatory diagnostic*, and is backed by a 2024 "Guidance for Public Finance Reviews" plus a growing series of **"PFR Fundamentals"** how-to notes (2024–2026) produced by the Fiscal Policy unit. The notes cover:
- spending efficiency (frontier tool)
- budget rigidity
- wage bill
- potential fiscal savings
- fiscal multipliers
- tax potential
- tax expenditures
- cyclicality
- policy recommendations

Sector PER guides exist for education (2017), health (2025) and agriculture (2011). Climate expenditure work runs through CPEIR, climate budget tagging, PEFA Climate (2024) and the CCIA.

### Inferences
- In WB practice, "allocative efficiency" is mostly assessed *within* sectors (for example primary vs tertiary education, curative vs preventive health, subsidies vs public goods in agriculture) or descriptively, by comparing sector shares with peers. The Malawi and Gambia PERs explicitly frame it "within sectors". Quantified *cross-sector* reallocation (equalizing marginal social returns across ministries) does not show up as a standard PFR module.
- The PFR Fundamentals notes are the closest thing to an official "PER/PFR toolkit" today. A tool that reproduces their outputs (frontier scores, structural-benchmark savings, wage-bill benchmarks, multipliers) would be "consistent with how the WB already thinks".
- Climate expenditure assessment (CBT, CPEIR, PEFA Climate) concentrates on tracking and alignment, not efficiency or returns. This leaves room for a tool that attaches economic returns (including the shadow price of carbon, SPC) to tagged climate lines.

## 2. Santiago Herrera's work on spending efficiency (methods, findings, tools)

### Takeaway
"Santiago Harrer" is almost certainly **Santiago Herrera**, a WB Lead Economist. His affiliation is listed as Macroeconomics, Trade & Investment GP / Fiscal Policy unit, and he is a former Deputy Minister of Finance and Budget Director of Colombia.

He is the main WB author of cross-country **efficiency-frontier benchmarking** of public spending:
- PRWP 3645 (2005, with Pang)
- PRWP 8586 (2018, with Ouedraogo)
- *Journal of Applied Economics* 2025 (with Isaka & Ouedraogo)

That research has been turned into the official **PFR Fundamentals spending-efficiency tool**. It covers education, health and infrastructure, using FDH, DEA, bootstrapped and conditional variants, and SFA.

He also co-authored the **structural-benchmark "potential fiscal savings"** method (with Isaka; building on Shelton 2007, Herrera & Velasco, and Herrera & Olaberria) and the budget-rigidity and procyclicality work.

What he has published is mainly *technical-efficiency* and *spending-level* benchmarking. None of it found here quantifies cross-sector allocative efficiency via marginal returns.

**Herrera & Pang (2005), WB PRWP 3645**, "Efficiency of Public Spending in Developing Countries: An Efficiency Frontier Approach". Staff research.
- Estimates FDH and DEA frontiers for health and education outputs.
- Scores input-inefficiency ("excess input consumption to achieve a level of output") and output-inefficiency ("output shortfall for a given level of inputs").
- Covers **140 countries, 1996–2002**.
- In the multiple input–output framework, developing countries score on average **0.85–0.95 in output efficiency**.
- Motivating statement: developing-country governments spend 15–30% of GDP, so small efficiency changes matter.
- A search summary also reports that a larger wage-bill share of the budget is associated with lower efficiency [SNIPPET].
- One catalog record shows a 2012 date, which is a re-catalog date. — [WB PDF wps3645](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/262621468135939068/pdf/wps3645.pdf); [SSRN 757387](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=757387); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/3645.html)

**Herrera & Pang (2006)**, "How Efficient is Public Spending in Education?", *Ensayos sobre Política Económica* 24(51): 136–201, with commentary by Alejandro Gaviria. Journal version of the education strand. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bdr/ensayo/v24y2006i51p136-201.html)

**Herrera & Ouedraogo (Sept 2018), WB PRWP 8586**, "Efficiency of Public Spending in Education, Health, and Infrastructure: An International Benchmarking Exercise". Staff research.
- Efficiency is the distance from an FDH/DEA frontier. Input- and output-inefficiency are scored for **175 countries, 2006–16**.
- Findings:
  - efficiency is negatively associated with spending levels;
  - it is negatively associated with the ratio of public to private financing of service provision;
  - capital-spending efficiency correlates with governance: regulatory quality (+) and perceived corruption (−).
- The second stage is reported as a **Tobit** model [SNIPPET, secondary summary].
- Authors' caveat: "no causality may be inferred". — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/8586.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/8586.htm); [OKR PDF](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/2298313c-4e72-538b-841b-c71ea01890e1/download); [SSRN 3249289](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3249289)

**Herrera, Isaka & Ouedraogo (2025)**, "Efficiency of public spending in education, health, and infrastructure: an international benchmarking exercise", *Journal of Applied Economics* 28(1), article 2480985, DOI 10.1080/15140326.2025.2480985.
- Uses non-parametric methods on **195 countries, 2010–2020**, with **four estimators: FDH, DEA, FDH-bootstrap (FDH-B), DEA-bootstrap (DEA-B)**.
- Findings:
  - developing countries are least efficient in **infrastructure**, moderately efficient in **education**, and more efficient in **health**;
  - efficiency of human-capital spending has improved relative to earlier studies;
  - efficiency is negatively associated with spending levels and positively with governance.
- It is cited by the IMF Oct-2025 Fiscal Monitor.
- A CEPR reference gives the article number as 2480995; the DOI and RePEc give 2480985. — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15140326.2025.2480985); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/recsxx/v28y2025i1p2480985.html)

**Official note: "PFR Fundamentals: Estimating Potential Fiscal Savings"**, prepared by **Herrera and Hironobu Isaka** (Fiscal Policy unit).
- Estimates potential savings in **five areas: wage bill, pensions, capital spending, goods & services, social protection**.
- Compares actual spending with a **structural benchmark**: the level "expected from long-term factors that policymakers don't directly control". The benchmark model builds on **Shelton (2007)**, as adapted by **Herrera & Velasco (2019)** and **Herrera & Olaberria (2019/2020)**.
- The wage-bill companion note uses a **fixed-effects** model that splits spending into a long-run structural part and a policy-driven/cyclical part; the gap is a proxy for potential savings. Example: Montenegro has ~**2.8% of GDP** of its wage bill attributed to non-structural factors. — [Fiscal savings note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099021126140533503/pdf/P506801-b4e18138-d5b4-4d77-ae0f-5fb5e9b0b8b5.pdf); [Wage bill note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099062325190034662/pdf/P506801-a3647740-b169-443a-b44a-a3eeccd12013.pdf)

**Other Herrera fiscal work**
- *Budget Rigidity in Latin America and the Caribbean: Causes, Consequences, and Policy Implications* (Herrera & Olaberria, WB International Development in Focus, 2020). — [WB PDF](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/181171586472396016/pdf/Budget-Rigidity-in-Latin-America-and-the-Caribbean-Causes-Consequences-and-Policy-Implications.pdf); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/b/wbk/wbpubs/33571.html)

- "Public Expenditure and Growth" (Herrera; SSRN 1020840; series and number not confirmed). — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1020840)

### Inferences
Methods a tool can reproduce (standard formulations, consistent with the papers above; formulas [TK]):
- **FDH input efficiency** for country *i* with a single input *x* (spending, % of GDP or per capita PPP) and outputs **y**:
  - θᵢ = min over j with **y**ⱼ ≥ **y**ᵢ of (xⱼ / xᵢ), with θᵢ ∈ (0,1].
  - Potential input saving = (1 − θᵢ)·xᵢ. For example, spending 5% of GDP at θ = 0.8 implies ~1% of GDP could be saved at the same output.
- **FDH output efficiency:** φᵢ = max over j with xⱼ ≤ xᵢ of minₘ(yⱼₘ / yᵢₘ) ≥ 1. The score is reported as 1/φᵢ, and the output gap is (φᵢ − 1)·yᵢ.
- **DEA (VRS, input-oriented)** solves min θ subject to:
  - Σⱼ λⱼ xⱼ ≤ θ xᵢ
  - Σⱼ λⱼ **y**ⱼ ≥ **y**ᵢ
  - Σ λⱼ = 1
  - λ ≥ 0

  This is a small LP per country, solvable in Python with `scipy.optimize.linprog`. The output-oriented version is symmetric.
- **Bootstrap:** the Simar–Wilson smoothed bootstrap for bias-corrected scores and confidence intervals. **Conditional efficiency:** Daraio–Simar, conditioning on environmental variables such as GDP per capita and governance. These are the standard references behind "DEA-B/FDH-B" and "conditional DEA/FDH" [TK; not verified this session].
- **Second stage:** Tobit (or Simar–Wilson truncated regression) of scores on governance (WGI regulatory quality, control of corruption), spending level, public/private financing share, and urbanization. "Localized" frontier: restrict the peer set to GDP-per-capita neighbours.
- **Structural-benchmark savings:** panel fixed-effects regression sₖ,ᵢₜ = αᵢ + β′Zᵢₜ + εᵢₜ.
  - sₖ is spending category *k* as % of GDP.
  - Z are slow-moving structural determinants: income, demographics/age-dependency, urbanization, population size/openness (Shelton-type) [TK for the exact variable list].
  - Savings proxy = max(0, sₖ,ᵢₜ − ŝₖ,ᵢₜ).
- Herrera's line of work measures **technical efficiency** (frontier) and **excess levels** (structural benchmark). Neither gives the marginal *social return* by sector needed for cross-sector allocative efficiency. The negative efficiency–spending association suggests diminishing returns, which could be used to approximate marginal returns from frontier slopes.

## 3. Fiscal Policy unit and related WB tools: MFMod / CC-MFMod, multipliers, LIC-DSF, PIM diagnostic and Reference Guide, PEFA

### Takeaway
The WB macro-fiscal toolset is MFMod (structural macro-econometric, country-specific) plus MANAGE (CGE). Climate-extended variants (CC-MFMod, with damage functions and an adaptation-returns module) are used in more than 80% of CCDRs since FY22. MFMod is also now proposed for **country-specific fiscal multipliers in PFRs**.

On institutional quality, the WB's 2010 PIM diagnostic ("eight must-haves") fed PEFA 2016 PI-11. The 2020 PIM Reference Guide compiles good practice. PEFA PI-1/PI-2 give standardized execution-deviation metrics that can be computed directly from budget-vs-outturn data such as BOOST.

### Cited Findings
**Macro models**
- MFMod is a structural econometric model covering national accounts, balance of payments, labor and fiscal sectors, with country-specific parameters estimated from historical data. MANAGE-WB (and ENVISAGE) are dynamic-recursive CGE models.
  - Since FY22, "over eighty percent" of CCDRs have used at least one of these models.
  - The CGE outputs include public and private investment. — [WB Macro-modeling & climate](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/economicpolicy-macro-modeling/macro-modeling-climate-change); [MFMod page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/economicpolicy-macro-modeling/mfmod); [WBG Macro Models for Climate Policy Analysis](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/490571642086593026/pdf/World-Bank-Group-Macroeconomic-Models-for-Climate-Policy-Analysis.pdf)
- CC-MFMod extends MFMod with emissions, pollution, and temperature- and flood-related damage functions, plus an **adaptation module that evaluates economic returns to resilience investment**. It was first built for Pakistan and Jamaica (per the Liberia CCDR annex). The Türkiye CCDR combined MANAGE and MFMod with sector models. An IMF review notes CCDRs use Solow, CC-MFMod, MANAGE and ENVISAGE, with varying macro coverage. — [Liberia CCDR annexes](https://docshare.wps.com/document/liberia-country-climate-development-report-annexes/109128/_payload.json); [Türkiye CCDR background note 6](https://docshare.wps.com/document/turkiye-ccdr-background-note-6-macroeconomic-modelling/108118/_payload.json); [IMF CMAP review](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/pp/2023/english/ppea2023025.pdf.md)

**Fiscal multipliers**
- Adarov, Clements & Jalles, WB PRWP 10954 (2024). Staff research.
  - Local projections, 129 countries, 1980–2019.
  - +1% of GDP public investment raises output ~**1.1% after five years** on average, up to ~**1.6%** when spending is efficient and fiscal space ample.
  - Multipliers are larger in recessions and capital-scarce economies. — [PRWP 10954 PDF](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099719510222432022/pdf/IDU1fd4bd390139da14d001b6c910c4f56d49874.pdf)
- PRWP 9796 literature review on infrastructure investment as fiscal stimulus: public-investment multipliers are "typically below one in the short run", can exceed one over multi-year horizons, and are rarely estimated for developing countries or infrastructure subsectors. — [PRWP 9796](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/fr/178841633526651703/pdf/What-Have-We-Learned-about-the-Effectiveness-of-Infrastructure-Investment-as-a-Fiscal-Stimulus-A-Literature-Review.pdf)

**LIC-DSF (joint WB–IMF; official)**
- Introduced 2005, revised 2017, operational 2018.
- Classifies debt-carrying capacity using a **composite indicator**. Per a secondary source, it combines CPIA, real GDP growth, remittances, reserves and world growth.
- Mechanical risk signals from debt-burden thresholds are combined with judgment, over a 10-year projection horizon. — [IMF factsheet](https://www.imf.org/en/About/Factsheets/Sheets/2023/IMF-World-Bank-debt-sustainability-framework-for-low-income-countries); [IEG on LIC-DSF](https://ieg.worldbankgroup.org/node/55624); [WB LIC-DSF page](https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/LIC%20DSF/Site%20File/station1.html)

**Public Investment Management**
- Rajaram, Le, Biletska & Brumby (2010), WB PRWP 5397, "A Diagnostic Framework for Assessing Public Investment Management". Staff research that became de facto guidance. It defines eight "must-haves":
  1. investment guidance, project development and preliminary screening;
  2. formal project appraisal;
  3. independent review of appraisal;
  4. project selection and budgeting;
  5. project implementation;
  6. project adjustment;
  7. facility operation;
  8. project evaluation.

  These are framed as a minimum, not best practice, to encourage periodic self-assessment. Per PEFA's stocktaking, the diagnostic has 19 questions across the eight features, and they were incorporated "as much as possible" into **PEFA 2016 PI-11 (public investment management)**. — [RePEc PRWP 5397](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/5397.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/5397.htm); [PEFA 2022 Stocktaking B13](https://www.PEFA.org/sites/pefa/files/PEFA%202022%20Stocktaking%20-%20B13.pdf)
- Kim, Fallov & Groom (Feb 2020), *Public Investment Management Reference Guide* (WB Governance GP; OKR handle 10986/33368). Compiles country experience and good practice from WB diagnostics and TA. Explicitly "not a rulebook". — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/b/wbk/wbpubs/33368.html); [OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/99c14858-9bf8-53e3-8fa3-67bd9b46610f); [PEFA resource page](https://pefa.org/resources/public-investment-management-reference-guide-wbg-publication)

**PEFA 2016 (official multi-donor framework; WB hosts the Secretariat)**
- PI-1 measures how far aggregate expenditure outturn reflects the originally approved amount.
- PI-2 measures expenditure composition outturn, with three dimensions: 2.1 by function, 2.2 by economic type, 2.3 expenditure from contingency reserves. — [PEFA 2016 Framework PDF](https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/PEFA_2016_Framework_Final_WEB_0.pdf); [PEFA Handbook Vol 2](https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/2020002215CHNchn001_PEFA%20Handbook%20Vol%202-new-logo.pdf)
- Scoring thresholds [TK; not confirmed from the 2016 text this session]:
  - **PI-1:** A if outturn is within 95–105% of approved in at least 2 of the last 3 years; B 90–110%; C 85–115%; D otherwise.
  - **PI-2.1/2.2:** composition variance below 5% / 10% / 15% for A / B / C in at least 2 of 3 years.
  - **PI-2.3:** contingency spending on average below 3% / 6% / 10% of the original budget for A / B / C.
  - The 2012 clarifications confirm the 5%/10% logic for the older PI-2. — [PEFA 2012 clarifications](https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/Clarifications-revised-2012-En.pdf)

### Inferences
- PEFA PI-1/PI-2 are directly computable from BOOST (approved vs executed by function and economic type) [TK formulas]:
  - Aggregate deviation = |Σ actual / Σ approved − 1|.
  - Composition variance = Σₖ |actualₖ − approvedₖ·(Σ actual / Σ approved)| ÷ Σₖ approvedₖ·(Σ actual / Σ approved).
- PI-11 scores, or WB PIM-diagnostic ratings, can serve as an **efficiency haircut** on capital spending. The GEP 2024 / PRWP 10954 finding (1.1 average vs 1.6 with efficient spending and ample space) gives a WB-consistent range for scaling multipliers by PIM quality.
- MFMod is not public as a general-purpose library. A lightweight tool would more realistically take MFMod- or PFR-derived multipliers as **parameters** than embed the model.

### Gaps
- No primary documentation of the "Fiscal Space" toolkit was found (for example the WB Prospects Group fiscal space database). [TK] Kose, Kurlat, Ohnsorge & Sugawara (2017/2022), *A Cross-Country Database of Fiscal Space*; not verified.

## 4. BOOST: what it is, coverage, how PERs use it, and current tooling

### Takeaway
BOOST (launched 2010) is a Bank-wide initiative that pulls **transaction-level budget data from treasury/FMIS systems**. It holds approved, revised and executed amounts by administrative, economic, functional (and, where available, programme, geographic and funding-source) classification. It is used in PERs/PFRs for:
- capital/current split
- execution rates
- wage bill
- O&M
- sector shares
- sub-national allocation

Coverage estimates range from more than 80 countries (2018 manual) to "over 90 developing countries" engaged (current WB page), with roughly 40+ datasets published openly. The data are **country-specific, not harmonized cross-country**, and licences vary. No evidence was found of a "Mega-BOOST" or a Databricks-based harmonized BOOST lake, although the WB does run an enterprise Databricks platform.

### Inferences
Technically feasible BOOST-derived indicators, by country-year and computable in Python or Excel from a BOOST extract:
1. Execution rate = executed/approved, overall and by economic and functional category. Capital execution is typically the binding one.
2. PEFA-style PI-1 and PI-2 deviations.
3. Capital share of primary spending, and capital/current ratio by function.
4. Wage bill as % of spending, revenue and GDP, matching the PFR wage-bill benchmarks.
5. Non-wage recurrent (goods & services / O&M) per unit of capital spending, or per unit of an asset proxy. This is a maintenance-adequacy indicator.
6. Functional (COFOG-mapped) shares versus peers.
7. Per-capita allocation by region/district, an equity indicator. Requires BOOST geographic codes.
8. Climate-tagged shares, if a CBT tag is mapped onto BOOST lines.
9. The input (x) for frontier analysis: sector spending.

- Because BOOST is not harmonized, a tool needs a **mapping layer** (country chart of accounts → COFOG/GFS economic codes) and should flag the unmappable remainder.

## 5. WB project-level economic analysis and prioritization: OPCS guidance, discounting, shadow price of carbon, Infrastructure Prioritization Framework, Beyond the Gap, CCDR investment needs

### Takeaway
Official IPF economic analysis rests on **three questions**:
1. development impact;
2. **rationale for public-sector provision/financing**, considering alternative delivery modes;
3. **World Bank value added**.

These appear in the 2013 OPSPQ guidance note and are carried into the current Bank Directive. Supporting notes set:
- **discounting**: 2016; the rate lies between y% and 2y%, where y is expected per-capita growth, so 3% growth gives 6%;
- the **shadow price of carbon**: 2017, updated **14 March 2024**. It uses High-Level Commission on Carbon Prices values in 2017 USD, extrapolated beyond 2030, low end for LICs and high end for MICs. It is mandatory for GHG-accounted IPF but "not an automatic rule".

For portfolio-level prioritization when full CBA is infeasible, the **Infrastructure Prioritization Framework** (PRWP 7674, 2016) builds two PCA-weighted indices (social-environmental and financial-economic) under a budget line. *Beyond the Gap* (2019) and the CCDRs frame investment needs as **ranges dependent on policy and O&M**.

### Cited Findings
**Economic analysis guidance**
- OPSPQ "Investment Project Financing Economic Analysis Guidance Note" (9 April 2013). "The revised approach posits three questions that should be addressed in all projects", including "Is public sector provision or financing the appropriate vehicle?", which "should explicitly consider alternative modes of provision". — [2013 guidance note (mirror)](http://www.ampres.com.mx/assets/guidance_note_economic_analysis.pdf)
- The Bank Directive on IPF (2023 version on thedocs) states the three key questions the economic analysis addresses:
  - "(a) the Project's expected contribution to the country's socioeconomic development;
  - (b) the rationale for the public sector provision; and
  - (c) the value added of the Bank's support". — [Bank Directive IPF](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/a63d7e40de1c13802a096eddf6d105bb-0290012023/original/Bank-Directive-Investment-project-financing.pdf); [Bank Policy IPF](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/6da2e812c0b8e84a1a614bb3c0507e5a-0290012023/original/Bank-Policy-Investment-project-financing.pdf)

**Shadow price of carbon**

- Marcelo, Mandri-Perrott, House & Schwartz (May 2016), WB PRWP 7674, "Prioritizing Infrastructure Investment: A Framework for Government Decision Making". Staff research (WB PPP group).

**Beyond the Gap (Rozenberg & Fay, eds., WB 2019)**. Flagship.
- Shifts the debate "from spending more to spending better on the right objectives".
- Costs both capital and O&M across water and sanitation, transport, electricity, irrigation and flood protection.
- Investment needs for LMICs range from **2% to 8% of GDP per year to 2030** across thousands of scenarios. A policy mix achieves the infrastructure SDGs and a path toward full decarbonization while limiting new-infrastructure spending to **4.5% of GDP per year**.
- A steady O&M flow is a "necessary condition". Good maintenance reduces total life-cycle cost of transport and WSS infrastructure by **more than 50%**. — [WB doc page](https://documents.banquemondiale.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/682091553093275315); [OKR metadata](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/signposting/describedby/95801508-1130-5ed0-843a-113b50285006); [IISD summary](https://infrastructure.iisd.org/index.php/insights/beyond-gap-how-countries-can-afford-infrastructure-they-need-while-protecting-planet)

**CCDR investment needs**
- CCDR adaptation costing counts only the **incremental** cost of resilient infrastructure, not the full asset cost. Results are cross-checked against top-down studies (IEA, ETC).
- Extrapolated LMIC needs (excluding China):
  - Mar 2023: $783bn per year to 2030;
  - May 2024: revised to $574bn per year (**2.8% of GDP**).
- CCDRs covered 42 economies as of May 2024. — [WB feature May 2024](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2024/05/01/what-you-need-to-know-about-ccdr-investment-estimates-and-the-role-of-the-private-sector); [WB feature Mar 2023](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2023/03/13/what-you-need-to-know-about-how-ccdrs-estimate-climate-finance-needs)

### Inferences
- WB project appraisal and WB budget analysis run on **separate tracks**. OPCS guidance (discount rate, SPC, rationale for public provision) governs Bank-financed projects. PFRs benchmark aggregate and sector spending. The IPF-tool is the main WB bridge between project-level returns and a budget envelope.
- A tool "consistent with WB thinking" would:
  - discount at r ∈ [g, 2g] using country per-capita growth;
  - value ΔGHG with the 2024 SPC path, low or high by income group;
  - report ENPV/ERR alongside the IPF-tool's two indices;
  - check affordability against a budget line.
- *Beyond the Gap* supports building **O&M-inclusive life-cycle costing** and treating investment needs as **policy-contingent ranges**.

- The 2024 SPC year-by-year table (2025–2050 values) was not retrieved.
- No evaluation of IPF-tool pilot outcomes was found.

## 6. Sector allocative-efficiency tools used by the WB (health, education, social protection, infrastructure, agriculture)

### Takeaway
Sector tools are where WB practice comes closest to **quantified allocative efficiency**, but mostly *within* sectors:
- **Health:** Optima (HIV/TB/nutrition) and the DALY-maximizing benefits-package tool (HIPtool, with DCP3 cost-effectiveness data).
- **Education:** LAYS and GEEAP "Smart Buys" cost-effectiveness tiers; Education PER guidelines with benefit incidence analysis.
- **Social protection:** ASPIRE coverage, incidence and adequacy indicators.
- **Roads:** HDM-4 and RONET for maintenance-versus-new trade-offs.
- **Agriculture:** evidence that shifting spending from private-good subsidies to public goods (R&D, extension, infrastructure) raises returns (López & Galinato 2007; Goyal & Nash 2017).

## 7. Recent WB flagship and related work on spending efficiency, public investment and fiscal space (2023–2026)

### Takeaway
The WB's most recent flagship statement on public spending quality is **GEP June 2024, Chapter 3, "Harnessing the Benefits of Public Investment"**. Its findings:
- EMDE public-investment growth halved;
- a 1% of GDP rise in public investment raises output by up to 1.6% where fiscal space is ample and spending efficient;
- private investment rises by up to 2.2% and TFP by up to 0.8%;
- policy package: expand fiscal space, improve public-investment efficiency, global support.

The closest recent cross-sector "spending smarter" quantification is the **IMF's** Oct-2025 Fiscal Monitor, which cites Herrera et al. It is not a WB product but is the reference point WB teams will encounter.

## 8. Implications for tool design and incremental upgrades to WB practice

### Takeaway
A Python/Excel extension can be "WB-consistent" by reproducing four official PFR Fundamentals computations:
- frontier efficiency;
- structural-benchmark savings;
- wage-bill/rigidity benchmarks;
- multipliers.

It should use BOOST, PEFA and PIM inputs, and apply OPCS appraisal conventions: discount rate in [g, 2g], 2024 SPC, and the three questions.

The incremental upgrades fill three verified gaps in current practice:
1. **cross-sector allocative efficiency** (marginal social return per $ by sector), which PFR guidance asks about but its tools do not compute;
2. **capital–recurrent integration** (life-cycle O&M versus BOOST allocations);
3. **economic versus fiscal returns** (ENPV versus net budget impact feeding debt sustainability).

### Cited Findings (anchors for the design; all cited above)
- The PFR guidance asks whether spending "can be made more efficient" and distinguishes allocative from technical efficiency. — [PFR Guidance](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099053124144119039/pdf/P175240-590f330c-30d7-4fe7-bac5-9c0b0d6fcdb8.pdf)
- The official efficiency tool covers education, health and infrastructure, using FDH/DEA (bootstrapped and conditional) and SFA, plus a composite score. — [PFR Fundamentals: Spending Efficiency](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099053124144068167/pdf/P175240-9b995be0-4268-4da9-95c9-5fd84dd3c38e.pdf)
- Potential savings come from a structural benchmark across the wage bill, pensions, capital, goods & services and social protection. — [Fiscal savings note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099021126140533503/pdf/P506801-b4e18138-d5b4-4d77-ae0f-5fb5e9b0b8b5.pdf)
- Multipliers are 1.1 on average, up to 1.6 with efficient spending and fiscal space. — [PRWP 10954](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099719510222432022/pdf/IDU1fd4bd390139da14d001b6c910c4f56d49874.pdf); [GEP Jun-2024 Ch.3](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/41681424-4459-4959-84d5-63709dee74dc/content)
- Maintenance cuts life-cycle cost by more than 50%; needs are 2–8% of GDP. — [Beyond the Gap](https://documents.banquemondiale.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/682091553093275315)
- BOOST provides approved, revised and actual spending by admin/economic/functional classification, but it is country-specific. — [Armenia BOOST](https://datacatalog.worldbank.org/search/dataset/0040229/armenia-boost-public-expenditure-database); [Moldova manual](https://www.mf.gov.md/sites/default/files/Moldova%20BOOST%20User%20Manual_September%202018%20%28en%29.docx)
- PIM must-haves include facility operation and evaluation, and are embedded in PEFA PI-11. — [PEFA Stocktaking B13](https://www.PEFA.org/sites/pefa/files/PEFA%202022%20Stocktaking%20-%20B13.pdf)
- The OECD water note says allocative efficiency receives "more limited" attention than technical efficiency. — [OECD 2026](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/events/2026/09/14th-roundtable-on-financing-water/background-note-public-expenditure-efficiency.pdf)

### Inferences (design proposals; these are the researcher's synthesis, not WB statements)

**A. Data → parameter map**

| Source | Feeds | Computable in |
|---|---|---|
| **BOOST** (country extract; approved/revised/executed × admin/econ/func/[prog/geo]) | sector spending (frontier input x); execution rates; PEFA PI-1/PI-2 deviations; capital share; wage share; G&S/O&M share; O&M-to-capex ratio; per-capita regional allocation; climate-tag shares | Python (pandas) / Excel (pivot) |
| **PEFA 2016** scores (PI-1, PI-2, PI-11, PI-8 service delivery performance [TK], PI-10 fiscal risk) | execution-reliability factor; PIM-quality haircut on capital efficiency; risk flags | lookup table |
| **PEFA Climate** (CRPFM-1…14) | climate alignment and tracking flags; climate outturn reliability (CRPFM-14) | lookup table |
| **IMF PIMA / C-PIMA** (covered by another researcher; IMF, not WB) | public-investment efficiency gap → capital efficiency parameter | lookup |
| **WGI / CPIA** | second-stage covariates (regulatory quality, corruption) as in Herrera & Ouedraogo | regression |
| **WDI / UIS / WHO / HCI / LAYS** | frontier outputs y; education outcome monetization | Python |
| **ASPIRE** | SP coverage, incidence, adequacy, benefit–cost | lookup |
| **CCDR / Beyond the Gap** | investment-needs ranges (% of GDP); adaptation incremental costs | parameters |
| **OPCS notes** | discount rate r ∈ [g, 2g]; SPC path by income group (2017 USD) | parameters |
| **GEP 2024 / PRWP 10954 / PFR multipliers note (MFMod)** | public-investment multipliers conditioned on efficiency and fiscal space | parameters |

**B. Modules that reproduce current WB practice (low risk)**
1. **Technical-efficiency benchmarking.** FDH and DEA-VRS, input- and output-oriented, with Simar–Wilson bootstrap CIs and an optional GDP-per-capita-localized peer set. Outputs: θ, φ, implied savings (1−θ)·x in % of GDP, and output gap. Second-stage Tobit on governance.
   - Python: `scipy.optimize.linprog` for DEA; numpy dominance tests for FDH.
   - Excel: FDH is feasible with MINIFS/array formulas; DEA needs Solver per country, so it is impractical beyond about 30 countries.
2. **Structural-benchmark savings.** Panel fixed-effects regression of category spending/GDP on structural Z. Savings = positive residual. Needs a cross-country panel (IMF GFS/WEO, WWBI), not BOOST alone. Use BOOST only for the country's own actuals.
3. **Execution and composition diagnostics.** PEFA PI-1/PI-2 metrics and execution rates by category from BOOST. Flag chronic capital under-execution.
4. **Wage-bill and rigidity benchmarks.** Wage bill as % of revenue and expenditure versus peers. Rigid share = wages + pensions + interest, following Herrera–Olaberria, as applied in the Belize PER.

**C. Incremental upgrades (where WB practice has gaps)**
5. **Cross-sector allocative-efficiency module.** Estimate a marginal social return per $ (MSRₛ) for each sector s, by triangulating:
   - (i) the frontier slope, i.e. marginal output per unit input on the efficient facet near the country's peers, times a monetized outcome value (LAYS → earnings; DALY → value per DALY; infrastructure → ERR);
   - (ii) the distribution of ERRs from appraised projects in that sector (PAD economic analyses / IPF-tool financial-economic index);
   - (iii) macro multipliers by spending type (GEP/PRWP 10954; IMF FM 2025 reallocation elasticities, labelled IMF).

   Then solve max Σₛ Bₛ(xₛ) subject to Σₛ xₛ ≤ X and rigidity floors (wages/pensions/interest). Report the welfare gain from moving ΔX toward sectors where MSRₛ is above the mean. Under diminishing returns (consistent with Herrera's negative efficiency–spending link) this gives an interior reallocation, not a corner solution.
6. **Capital–recurrent integration.**
   - For each capital program, attach a recurrent-cost coefficient (O&M per year as % of capex, by asset class). Compute the required O&M path.
   - Compare it with BOOST G&S/maintenance lines to get a maintenance gap (% of GDP).
   - Value the gap using *Beyond the Gap*'s more-than-50% life-cycle cost evidence, or HDM-4/RONET outputs for roads.
   - This implements PIM must-have #7 (facility operation) quantitatively.
7. **Economic versus fiscal return dashboard.** For each project or program, report:
   - ENPV/ERR: economic prices, SPC (2024 note) and r ∈ [g, 2g];
   - fiscal NPV: capex + O&M − user charges − induced tax revenue (tax ratio × ΔGDP from multipliers), discounted at the government borrowing rate;
   - the debt-path effect: feed net fiscal flows into a LIC-DSF-style debt-dynamics block.

   Then classify into four quadrants, in the spirit of the IPF-tool's two-index plane. This operationalizes the IPF "rationale for public provision" test and the GEP's fiscal-space conditionality.
8. **PIM-quality-adjusted capital.** Effective investment = PIM efficiency (from PEFA PI-11 / PIMA) × nominal investment, so returns and multipliers fall when PIM is weak (GEP 2024: 1.1 → 1.6 range).
9. **Climate overlay.** Map CBT tags onto BOOST lines, attach SPC-valued emissions changes and CC-MFMod-style adaptation returns, and report climate-aligned share versus CCDR needs (for example 2.8% of GDP, LMIC average).

**D. Where WB practice appears weakest (inferred from the evidence above)**
- **Cross-sector allocative efficiency is rarely quantified.** PFR guidance poses the question; the official tools measure technical efficiency (frontier) and excess levels (structural benchmark). Sector tools (Optima, HIPtool, GEEAP, HDM-4) optimize *within* sectors. The OECD (2026) notes the same imbalance for WASH.
- **Frontier scores are fragile.** They use outcome indicators driven by non-fiscal factors, are sensitive to outliers, and are weakest for infrastructure, where the 2025 paper finds the lowest efficiency. For SIDS, small populations and diseconomies of scale make GDP-localized peer sets thin. A small-states peer group and conditional (environment-adjusted) scores are needed.
- **Capital and recurrent spending are analyzed separately.** PERs report capital shares and execution, but rarely link them to asset stocks and life-cycle O&M needs, despite *Beyond the Gap* and PIM must-have #7.
- **Fiscal and economic returns are not jointly reported.** OPCS appraisal targets economic returns for Bank projects. A quantified life-cycle fiscal impact and debt link was not found as a requirement (unverified). PFR multipliers address the macro level but are not linked to project pipelines.
- **The data layer is not harmonized.** No evidence was found of a harmonized cross-country BOOST, so peer benchmarking still relies on IMF GFS/WEO and WDI. BOOST serves country actuals and within-country analysis.

### Gaps
- The exact variable definitions and peer-selection rules of the PFR spending-efficiency tool, and its software form, are unverified. Obtaining the PDF or code from the WB Fiscal Policy unit would let a tool replicate its scores exactly.
- I could not verify whether WB has an internal AI/Databricks BOOST pipeline. Any "BOOST impacts" feature should assume **country-level BOOST CSV/Excel extracts** plus a user-built classification crosswalk.
- Recurrent-cost coefficients by asset class, and default values per DALY or LAYS, were not researched here; they need separate sourcing.
- Verification was limited to search metadata and snippets, since all document hosts were blocked for direct fetch. Items marked [SNIPPET] or [TK] should be confirmed against the PDFs before the tool cites them.
