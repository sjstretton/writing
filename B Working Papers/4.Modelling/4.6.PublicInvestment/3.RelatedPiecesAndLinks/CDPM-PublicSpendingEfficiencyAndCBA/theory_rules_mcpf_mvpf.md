## Q1. Definitions and the standard decision rule (allocative vs technical efficiency; efficiency vs effectiveness; equalising marginal social benefit per dollar; Samuelson)

### Takeaway
"Allocative efficiency" of public spending means spending on the right things in the right amounts. At the margin, the social value of each use per net dollar of public cost is equal across uses, and no lower than the cost of raising that dollar (λ, the marginal cost of public funds). "Technical (productive) efficiency" means delivering a given output at least cost. Modern practice turns the allocative rule into a comparison of the "marginal value of public funds" (MVPF = willingness to pay ÷ net government cost) across policies and against the cost of the marginal financing source.

### Cited Findings
- Hendren & Sprung-Keyser divide each policy's benefit to recipients (willingness to pay, WTP) by its net cost to government, long-run budget effects included. They state that "comparing MVPFs across policies provides a unified method of assessing their impact on social welfare." This is the operational form of "equalise marginal benefit per dollar" — [Hendren & Sprung-Keyser 2020, QJE 135(3):1209–1318, author page](https://hendren.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/unified-welfare-analysis-government-policies); [NBER w26144](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26144).
- Finkelstein & Hendren define the MVPF as "the ratio of beneficiaries' willingness to pay for the policy to the net cost to the government," where net cost "includes long-run effect of the policy on the government's budget (i.e., fiscal externalities)." They apply it to canonical cases in public finance, labour, **development**, trade and industrial organisation — [Finkelstein & Hendren 2020, "Welfare Analysis Meets Causal Inference," JEP 34(4):146ff, DOI 10.1257/jep.34.4.146](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.34.4.146); [NBER w27640](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27640/w27640.pdf).
- The marginal cost of public funds (MCPF/MCF) is the efficiency loss from raising additional tax revenue. Dahlby's standard text applies it to sales and excise taxes, labour-income taxes, taxes on capital income, public borrowing and intergovernmental grants — [Dahlby 2008, *The Marginal Cost of Public Funds: Theory and Applications*, MIT Press, ISBN 9780262042505](https://www.porchlightbooks.com/products/marginal-cost-of-public-funds-bev-dahlby-9780262042505); [library record](https://library.usi.edu/record/389669); [Wikipedia overview (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_cost_of_public_funds).
- Composition (allocative) choices matter in their own right. Devarajan, Swaroop & Zou derive conditions under which shifting spending toward one component raises steady-state growth. These "depend not just on the physical productivity of the different components of public expenditure but also on the initial shares" — [Devarajan, Swaroop & Zou 1996, JME 37(2):313–344, DOI 10.1016/0304-3932(96)01249-4](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/281841); [WB WPS 1082 version](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/1082.htm).
- Technical-efficiency losses are large. The IMF's 2015 cross-country study finds an average public-investment efficiency gap of about 30%: 40% in low-income developing countries (LIDCs), 27% in emerging markets and 13% in advanced economies — [IMF 2015, "Making Public Investment More Efficient," policy paper](https://www.imf.org/external/pp/longres.aspx?id=4959); [PDF mirror](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/Making%20Public%20Investment%20More%20Efficient%202015.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Terminology trap.** Farrell's (1957) "allocative (price) efficiency" is about the *input mix* at given input prices; cost efficiency = technical efficiency × allocative (input-mix) efficiency. Public-finance "allocative efficiency" is about the *output mix across uses* (what to spend on). A methodology review should say which one it means. The IMF "efficiency gap" is a Farrell-type technical/frontier measure. MVPF and CBA rules are about the output mix.
- **Efficiency vs effectiveness** (standard usage): *efficiency* = outputs per unit of input (cost per km of road, per classroom). *Effectiveness* = outcomes achieved per output or input (travel-time savings, learning, welfare). CBA and the MVPF work at the outcome/welfare level, so they capture both, provided benefits are measured as outcomes valued by WTP.
- **Musgrave's three functions** (allocation, distribution, stabilisation): allocative efficiency belongs to the allocation branch. CBA with distributional weights (Q7) deliberately merges allocation and distribution in one welfare metric.
- **Key formulas** (standard; my notation):
  - Samuelson condition for a pure public good with lump-sum finance: `Σ_i MRS_i = MRT`.
  - With distortionary finance (the "modified Samuelson rule"): `Σ_i MRS_i = λ · MRT`, with λ = MCPF ≥ 1 in the simple case.
  - Allocation across uses j: maximise `W` subject to `Σ_j G_j = budget`. The first-order condition is `MSB_j / MC_j = μ` for every funded j: equalise marginal social benefit per dollar, where μ is the shadow price of the budget. If the budget is set optimally by the tax decision, μ = λ.
  - Project test with distortionary finance: `NPV_λ = Σ_t [B_t − C^res_t − (λ − 1)·G^net_t] / (1 + r)^t ≥ 0`. Here B = WTP-valued benefits, C^res = resource costs at shadow prices, and G^net = net budget outlay (outlays − revenues − fiscal externalities).
  - Ratio form: `MVPF_j = WTP_j / G^net_j`. Fund j (financed by instrument τ) if `MVPF_j ≥ MVPF_τ`. With welfare weights ψ, the test is `ψ_j·MVPF_j ≥ ψ_τ·MVPF_τ`.

## Q2. Rationale for public spending: market failure vs equity; additionality/counterfactual; the World Bank "rationale for public sector provision" test

### Takeaway
The social value of a public intervention is the *difference* between outcomes with it and without it, net of what private actors (and other policies) would have done anyway. The case for public provision rests on a market failure (public goods, externalities, natural monopoly, missing markets, information, coordination) or on equity. Devarajan, Squire & Suthiwart-Narueput (1997) moved World Bank appraisal from precise rate-of-return calculation toward three questions: the rationale for public intervention, fiscal impact, and the fungibility of aid.

### Cited Findings
- Devarajan, Squire & Suthiwart-Narueput argue for moving appraisal away from precise rate-of-return calculation toward broader sectoral analysis and public expenditure reviews. They name three areas: (i) the rationale for public intervention, (ii) the project's fiscal impact, and (iii) the fungibility of external assistance — [DSS 1997, "Beyond Rate of Return: Reorienting Project Appraisal," *World Bank Research Observer* 12(1): 35–46](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/oup/wbrobs/v12y1997i1p35-46.html); [journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/4352285).
- Harberger's published comment argues their steps "fit quite easily within the inherited corpus of applied welfare economics." In other words, the counterfactual/fiscal critique refines CBA and does not replace it — [Harberger 1997, comment, WBRO 12(1): 73–79](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/wbrobs/v12y1997i1p73-79.html).
- The companion paper "Reviving Project Appraisal at the World Bank" (same authors) states: "Since World Bank projects are public sector projects, the relevant counterfactual involves assessing what the private sector would have otherwise provided." It argues for shifting emphasis "to a broader examination of the rationale for public provision" — [library record](https://library.au.int/node/322460). **Date conflict:** catalogue records give 1999, and one library record gives 1995 ([IPB record](https://readily.ipb.ac.id/opac/detail-buku?id=14282)).
- World Bank policy OP/BP 10.04 held that "no investment projects are exempt from a net-benefit test." Benefits and costs are measured against the without-project situation and compared with alternatives — [IEG approach paper on CBA at the World Bank](https://ieg.worldbankgroup.org/node/3638).
- The Bank's *Handbook on Economic Analysis of Investment Operations* integrates financial, economic and fiscal analysis so that a project can be viewed from several stakeholders' perspectives — [Handbook record](https://preventionweb.net/quick/38589).
- A World Bank transport note (Mackie, Nellthorp & Laird, 2005) states that public contributions to PPPs "should be justified on the basis of external benefits from the project, compared with the scenario where no public contribution is made." This is an explicit additionality test — reported in search retrieval; the likely document is [WB Transport Note (link from search; note number not confirmed)](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/234331468329400081/pdf/339270rev.pdf).
- Subsidies and in-kind transfers create (negative) fiscal externalities. Luttmer notes that internalising them through taxation would undo the subsidy that caused them — [Luttmer 2022, "Can Fiscal Externalities Be Internalized?", NBER w30213](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30213/w30213.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Counterfactual value of a public dollar** (my formalisation): `ΔW = W(with intervention, incl. private responses) − W(without intervention, private baseline)`. Operationally, `B_net = B_gross·(1 − d)`, where d is the deadweight share (benefits that would have occurred anyway, including private provision crowded out). Private resources freed by crowding-out are a benefit, not a loss, and should not be double counted.
- **The "Pigouvian gap" as the decision quantity.** For a good with marginal external benefit e and subsidy s (quantity q, slope q' = dq/ds), welfare is `W = CS(s) + e·q − λ·s·q`. Using dCS/ds = q, the first-order condition gives `s* = [e − (λ − 1)·q/q'] / λ` (my derivation). With λ = 1 the optimum is the Pigouvian subsidy s* = e. With λ > 1 the optimal subsidy is *below* e. The shortfall is larger when inframarginal (non-additional) units q are large relative to induced units q'. This single formula ties together the Pigouvian wedge, additionality/deadweight, and the MCPF. Value comes from the *induced change* times the wedge, while every *inframarginal* dollar costs λ − 1.
- **Fungibility** (DSS): when aid or a project finances something the government would have done anyway, the true marginal effect is whatever the freed money funds (the marginal public expenditure). The counterfactual applies to the budget as well as to the private sector.
- **Equity rationale**: if spending is justified on distribution grounds, the relevant comparison is with the cheapest alternative way of redistributing (e.g., a cash transfer). This is the logic of comparing MVPFs to that of a transfer (Q4, Q9).

## Q3. Cost of public funds: MCPF theory, estimates (especially developing countries), the "premium on public income" in classic appraisal, and values governments use

### Takeaway
Raising $1 of revenue through distortionary taxes costs taxpayers about $λ, with λ > 1. The best developing-country evidence, for 38 African countries, puts the average MCF at about 1.2 for a mix of taxes and about 1.1 when using the cheapest instrument (usually VAT). Higher informality goes with higher MCF. In high-tax welfare states, participation-margin estimates can approach 2. Norway has used MCF = 1.2 as its CBA convention since 1997.

### Cited Findings
- **Auriol & Warlters (2012)**, "The marginal cost of public funds and tax reform in Africa," *Journal of Development Economics* 97: 58–72, covers 38 African countries. Base case: average MCF ≈ **1.2** for marginal increases in all five tax instruments. Using the lowest-cost instrument in each country (usually VAT): ≈ **1.1**. The size of the informal sector is strongly related to the MCF, while MCF differences are *not* strongly related to national wealth. An earlier working-paper version reported "a reasonable estimate of the average MCF in Africa is 1.17" — [TSE/UT-Capitole PDF](https://publications.ut-capitole.fr/id/eprint/975/1/auriol_975.pdf); [abstract record](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/321678); [CEPR DP6007, "The Marginal Cost of Public Funds in Developing Countries: An Application to 38 African Countries"](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/DP6007.html); [World Bank working-paper version, "The marginal cost of public funds in Africa" (Warlters & Auriol)](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/358481468003006205).
- **Kleven & Kreiner (2006)**, "The marginal cost of public funds: Hours of work versus labor force participation," *JPubE* 90(10–11): 1955–1973, DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2006.03.006. Fixed costs of work create discrete participation responses with first-order revenue effects. For 15 European countries, MCF estimates "depend crucially on the country … and on the properties of the tax reform," and are high, "centring around 2," in Scandinavian and Central/Northern Continental countries for proportional tax changes — [author PDF](https://www.henrikkleven.com/research/published/kleven-kreiner_jpube2006.pdf); [LSE record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/5388); [earlier CESifo WP 935](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo_wp935.pdf).
- **Government convention, Norway:** NOU 1997:27 recommended setting MCF = **1.2** in cost–benefit analyses, and this has remained recommended practice. A 2015 Statistics Norway review concluded "we do not have firm ground to give a point estimate of MCF" — [SSB Reports 2015/13](https://www.ssb.no/en/offentlig-sektor/artikler-og-publikasjoner/the-size-of-the-marginal-cost-of-public-funds). A Norwegian study argues that the MCF in Norway should lie in the interval **1.06–1.16** when social transfers alleviate the participation distortions — [SSB Discussion Paper 925, "The marginal opportunity cost of public funds"](https://www.ssb.no/en/forskning/discussion-papers/the-marginal-opportunity-cost-of-public-funds); [SwoPEc record](https://swopec.hhs.se/dispap/abs/dispap925.htm) (mapping of the 1.06–1.16 figure to this DP is from search retrieval).
- Dahlby's book provides the general MCF framework, including the MCF of public borrowing and of intergovernmental grants — [Dahlby 2008, MIT Press](https://www.porchlightbooks.com/products/marginal-cost-of-public-funds-bev-dahlby-9780262042505).
- Informality also weakens fiscal policy transmission: fiscal multipliers in high-informality countries are "significantly lower" than in low-informality ones — [IMF WP/22/82, "Fiscal Multipliers and Informality" (2022)](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2022/english/wpiea2022082-print-pdf.pdf).

### Inferences
- **MCF formula (Dahlby-type, proportional tax t on a base with elasticity ε with respect to the net-of-tax rate):** `MCF = (1 − t) / (1 − t − t·ε) = 1 / (1 − t·ε/(1 − t))`. Illustrative values (my calculation): t = 0.30, ε = 0.2 → 1.09; t = 0.30, ε = 0.5 → 1.27; t = 0.20, ε = 1.0 → 1.33; t = 0.40, ε = 0.5 → 1.50. The MCF rises without bound as the rate approaches the revenue-maximising (Laffer) rate.
- **Why low-capacity countries can have high λ despite low tax rates.** A large informal sector raises the effective elasticity of the narrow formal tax base, and enforcement is weak. This is consistent with Auriol & Warlters' finding that informality, not income, predicts the MCF. It implies λ should be set by country (by informality and tax mix), not by income group.
- **Classic project-appraisal analogue.** Little–Mirrlees, Squire–van der Tak and the UNIDO guidelines value *uncommitted public income* at a premium (v > 1) over private consumption at the average income level. The reasons are a scarcity of savings and investment and distortionary taxation. Drèze & Stern (1987) generalise this as shadow prices derived from the government's marginal reform options. Functionally, v plays the same role as λ: each $ of net public cost is penalised and each $ of revenue return is rewarded (see Q5).
- **Theoretical caveat.** How large the "λ − 1" penalty should be depends on how the marginal dollar is financed and on distributional judgements. Under the Kaplow (1996) / Jacobs (2018) view (UNVERIFIED, see Gaps), much of the measured "MCF > 1" reflects redistribution, and at an optimal redistributive tax system the distribution-corrected MCF is about 1. The MVPF approach (Q4) sidesteps this by comparing a policy's MVPF with the MVPF of the actual financing instrument.

## Q4. Marginal Value of Public Funds (MVPF): definition, treatment of revenue returns, "self-financing" policies, LMIC extensions, and the link to MCPF and BCR

### Takeaway
MVPF = beneficiaries' WTP ÷ net government cost, and the net cost subtracts fiscal externalities (future tax gains and benefit savings). Revenue returns therefore enter the *denominator*, and a policy whose fiscal externalities exceed its upfront cost "pays for itself" (MVPF = ∞). In U.S. data, child health and education policies often reach very high or infinite MVPFs, while adult-targeted policies mostly fall between 0.5 and 2. Applications in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are still few (Mauritius UI ≈ 1.23 as reported; Mexico property tax; Kenya deworming's fiscal-return claim).

### Cited Findings
- Hendren & Sprung-Keyser (2020, QJE 135(3): 1209–1318) study 133 U.S. policy changes (social insurance, education and job training, taxes and cash transfers, in-kind transfers). For each, benefit (WTP) is divided by net cost "including long-term effects on the government's budget." Several policies "pay for themselves." MVPFs are large for child-focused health and education spending. Adult-targeted policies "generally have smaller MVPFs, between 0.5 and 2," unless they produce large spillovers on children — [author page](https://hendren.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/unified-welfare-analysis-government-policies); [NBER w26144](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26144); [Opportunity Insights summary](https://opportunityinsights.org/?p=2646).
- Definition with fiscal externalities: "ratio of beneficiaries' willingness to pay for the policy to the net cost to the government," where net cost "includes long-run effect of the policy on the government's budget (i.e., fiscal externalities)" — [Finkelstein & Hendren 2020, JEP](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.34.4.146); [NBER w27640](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27640/w27640.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Formula:** `MVPF = WTP / NetCost`, with `NetCost = C_upfront − FE` and `FE = PV(Δ tax revenue) + PV(Δ transfer/benefit savings) + PV(other budget savings)`. If `NetCost ≤ 0` and `WTP > 0`, the policy "pays for itself" and is coded MVPF = ∞. If both are negative (a revenue-raising policy), the ratio is read as the cost to beneficiaries per $ raised.
- **Relation to MCPF (my derivation, consistent with Hendren's framework).** For a small tax cut, the envelope theorem gives WTP = the mechanical revenue cost M. Net cost = M − behavioural revenue recovery FE. So `MVPF_taxcut = M/(M − FE) = 1/(1 − FE/M)`, which is the (Dahlby) MCF of that tax. A spending policy j financed by raising tax τ therefore improves (unweighted) welfare iff `MVPF_j > MCPF_τ`. This is the MVPF restatement of the modified Samuelson / λ rule.
- **Relation to the λ-adjusted BCR.** With net fiscal cost G^net in the denominator, `BCR_λ = B / (λ·G^net) ≥ 1 ⇔ MVPF ≥ λ`. A conventional BCR that uses *gross* cost and counts revenue returns as benefits is not invariant to that classification choice. The MVPF fixes the convention: *all* budget effects go in the denominator and all beneficiary effects go in the numerator.
- **Does this change in LMICs?** (1) Informality and low tax-to-GDP mean a smaller share of induced income returns as tax, so fiscal externalities are smaller and fewer programmes self-finance. (2) A higher λ makes every revenue return more valuable. (3) Higher discount rates shrink long-run fiscal externalities in present value. (4) Welfare weights matter more because the WTP of the poor is low in dollar terms (Q7). (5) Implementation risk lowers expected WTP (Q8). The deworming case shows that self-financing is possible in LMICs, though the estimate is contested.

## Q5. Treatment of revenue returns in CBA: transfers vs benefits, the λ − 1 premium, fiscal vs economic analysis, self-financing projects, and appropriability

### Takeaway
In *economic* CBA, user fees and taxes are transfers. The user's WTP is the benefit and the fee simply moves money between user and government, so it is not an additional benefit. With λ > 1, however, every $1 of net revenue returned to the budget is worth an extra λ − 1 (it avoids $1 of distortionary taxation). Equivalently, revenue returns reduce the net-fiscal-cost denominator (MVPF / BCR on net public cost). A separate *fiscal* analysis is still needed, because low "appropriability" (the government captures only a small share of the output it creates) plus recurrent O&M costs can make a high-return project fiscally unsustainable in low-income countries.

### Cited Findings
- **Adam & Bevan (2014)**, "Public Investment, Public Finance, and Growth: The Impact of Distortionary Taxation, Recurrent Costs, and Incomplete Appropriability," IMF Working Paper WP/14/73 (April 2014). Effective public investment requires solving the "recurrent cost problem," so that O&M spending is large enough to keep public capital productive. The paper extends Buffie et al. (2012) by treating taxation as distortionary, and simulates replacing a distortionary output tax with a consumption tax and restoring O&M to efficient levels. These are stylised experiments showing that tax and spending structures matter for growth and debt sustainability in LICs — [IMF WP page](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/public-investment-public-finance-and-growth-the-impact-of-distortionary-taxation-recurrent-41518); [IGC PDF](https://www.theigc.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Adam-Bevan-2014-Working-Paper.pdf); [UK R4D record](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/public-investment-public-finance-and-growth-the-impact-of-distortionary-taxation-recurrent-costs-and-incomplete-appropriability).
- Applied to Uganda, the Adam–Bevan model suggested that an overly steep investment surge would hurt long-run results and recommended a more gradual expansion — [IGC Uganda project memo](https://www.theigc.org/publications/public-investment-and-growth-implications-recurrent-costs-uganda-project-memo).
- Fiscal impact is one of the three pillars of the DSS reorientation of Bank appraisal — [DSS 1997, WBRO](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/oup/wbrobs/v12y1997i1p35-46.html). The Bank Handbook integrates financial, economic and fiscal perspectives — [Handbook record](https://preventionweb.net/quick/38589).
- The MVPF puts long-run budget effects (fiscal externalities) in the net-cost denominator — [Hendren & Sprung-Keyser 2020](https://hendren.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/unified-welfare-analysis-government-policies); [Finkelstein & Hendren 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.34.4.146).
- Subsidies and in-kind transfers generate negative fiscal externalities, and internalising them via taxation would undo the transfer — [Luttmer 2022, NBER w30213](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30213/w30213.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Accounting identity for a tool (my formulation):**
  - Economic NPV with costly public funds: `ENPV_λ = PV(B − C^res) − (λ − 1)·PV(G^net)`, where `G^net = capex + O&M + subsidies paid − user fees − induced tax revenue − avoided public spending`.
  - Fees and taxes are excluded from B and C^res, because they are transfers. They enter only through G^net. A $1 fee therefore raises ENPV_λ by exactly λ − 1 (e.g., +$0.20 at λ = 1.2).
  - Do **not** also add fee revenue as a benefit. That double counts, because users' WTP already includes what they pay.
- **When revenue returns should reduce the denominator versus be counted as a benefit:**
  - (a) For ratio rankings under a budget constraint (MVPF, BCR on net public cost, "profitability index"), *always* net revenue returns out of the denominator. The binding constraint is the budget, and revenue returns relax it.
  - (b) In an NPV, revenue returns appear only through the (λ − 1) term.
  - (c) Revenue returns to a *different* budget (another level of government, a state-owned enterprise, a donor) should be valued at that budget's shadow price, which may differ from λ.
  - (d) Revenue that merely shifts the timing of the government's own cash (e.g., cost recovery via user charges that would otherwise be paid by other taxes) still counts, because it reduces future distortionary taxation.
- **Appropriability and self-financing:** let α be the share of incremental output the government captures (fees plus the effective tax rate on induced income). The fiscal return is α·ΔY. Approximately, the project is *fiscally self-financing* if `PV(α·ΔY) ≥ PV(capex + O&M + debt service)`. A simple debt-dynamics check is `Δd_t ≈ (i − g)·d_{t−1} + (I_t + OM_t − α·ΔY_t)/Y_t`. In low-tax, high-informality LICs α is small, so even high-ENPV projects can raise debt ratios. This is why the economic test (ENPV_λ) and a fiscal test (debt and O&M coverage) are both needed.
- **Grant or concessional financing does not make the public cost zero.** Through fungibility (DSS), the opportunity cost of a dollar of aid is the marginal public project it could otherwise fund. Value tied aid at the shadow price of the budget (μ), not at zero. Value only the concessionality (grant element) as a genuine resource gain to the country.

## Q6. Social discount rate: Ramsey, declining rates, World Bank and ADB practice, Arrow–Lind, and the role of high sovereign borrowing costs

### Takeaway
The World Bank's 2016 guidance links the social discount rate (SDR) to growth via the Ramsey rule: an SDR of 1–2 times expected long-run per-capita growth, which gives about 2–10% and a typical 6%, with sensitivity analysis required. ADB's 2017 guidelines use 9% as the minimum economic IRR (down from 12%), and 6% for certain environmental-benefit projects. High sovereign borrowing costs belong in the *fiscal* analysis and the cost of public funds, not in the SDR, except where the government is genuinely capital-rationed.

### Cited Findings
- **World Bank (OPSPQ) guidance note, "Discounting Costs and Benefits in Economic Analysis of World Bank Projects" (2016).** It uses the Ramsey formula and recommends an SDR between 1 and 2 times the country's long-term per-capita growth rate. Its example: 3% per-capita growth → 6% SDR; per-capita growth of 1–5% → SDRs of 2–10%. The elasticity of marginal utility is assumed to be 1–2, and the OPSPQ calibration used 1996–2016 growth for client countries with elasticity 2. "A lower value could make sense in a situation in which long-term prospects for growth are limited." Sensitivity analysis is recommended whatever SDR is chosen. Before this guidance, the Bank and other IFIs used 10–12% as a default — [WB guidance note PDF](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099610503022315638/pdf/IDU02e978a2a023f704c140bb1b082595e67e593.pdf); summarised in [WB OKR document](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/cf399a26-0e18-4ff4-8602-3f7582265277/download) and [WB, "Setting the Discount Rate for Public Investment Projects in Serbia"](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/466001625159430426/pdf/Setting-the-Discount-Rate-for-Public-Investment-Projects-in-Serbia.pdf). **Conflict:** one secondary source describes 6% as the Bank's *minimum* recommended rate, while others describe a 2–10% range averaging 6%.
- Older practice: a 2016 UNDP training deck lists WB 10%, ADB 12%, IDB 12% and EBRD 10%, stating that all projects "must use the same discount rate" — [UNDP CBA training slides](https://adaptation-undp.org/sites/default/files/uploaded-images/session_6_discounting_cba_step_5.pdf) (secondary and partly superseded).
- **ADB (2017), *Guidelines for the Economic Analysis of Projects*.** The minimum required EIRR is the discount rate, and a project must show positive ENPV at that rate. The 2017 revision lowered the rate from **12% to 9%**. For projects with benefits "such as pollution control, protection of the ecosystem, flood control, and control of deforestation," the minimum EIRR can be lowered to **6%** — [ADB guidelines page](https://www.adb.org/documents/guidelines-economic-analysis-projects); [ADB TA 8507 completion report noting 12%→9%](https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/project-documents/47330/47330-001-tcr-en.pdf); [ADB project economic analysis citing 9% standard SDR](https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/linked-documents/57267-001-ea.pdf); [development.asia summary](https://development.asia/summary/guidelines-economic-analysis-projects). Background: [ADB, "Theory and Practice in the Choice of Social Discount Rate for Cost-Benefit Analysis: A Survey"](https://www.adb.org/publications/theory-and-practice-choice-social-discount-rate-cost-benefit-analysis-survey).
- Global health practice: about 85% of studies in the Global Health CEA Registry with horizons of 3+ years use 3%. A growth-based global rate would be about 5% (population-weighted per-capita growth implies about 6%, unweighted about 4%). CGD recommends country-specific calibration — [CGD on discount rates in global health](https://www.cgdev.org/node/3128144).
- Country SDR estimation precedent: [WB Policy Research WP 4639, "The Social Discount Rate: …"](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/135541468266716605/pdf/wps4639.pdf) (title truncated in retrieval).

### Inferences
- **Ramsey rule:** `r = δ + η·g`, where δ is pure time preference (plus catastrophe risk), η is the elasticity of marginal utility and g is per-capita consumption growth. The World Bank rule is the special case δ ≈ 0, η ∈ [1, 2].
- **Declining discount rates (DDRs):** under uncertainty about g (persistent shocks) or about r itself (Weitzman's gamma discounting), the certainty-equivalent rate falls with the horizon. This matters for long-lived assets such as climate adaptation, coastal protection and education (see Gaps for the UK schedule).
- **Arrow–Lind:** if project returns are independent of national income and spread across n taxpayers, the risk premium per taxpayer falls roughly as 1/n² and the total as 1/n, so it vanishes for large n. Public projects can then be discounted at a risk-free social rate. The theorem *fails* where (i) the project is large relative to the economy (SIDS), (ii) returns are correlated with aggregate shocks (hurricanes and tourism collapses hit both project benefits and GDP), or (iii) costs fall on a concentrated group. The better fix is to adjust *expected benefits to certainty equivalents* (or add a consumption-beta premium only for correlated risk), rather than raising r across the board.
- **High sovereign borrowing costs in LICs and SIDS.** The Ramsey SDR measures how society trades consumption over time. A high market borrowing rate i is a *financing cost*. Its welfare cost is captured by bringing debt service into net fiscal cost and pricing it at λ. Illustrative multiplier (my calculation): the PV, at SDR r, of debt service on $1 borrowed for n years at rate i is `[i/(1−(1+i)^−n)]·[(1−(1+r)^−n)/r]`.
  - i = 8%, r = 6%, n = 30 → **1.22**. Each $1 of commercially borrowed spending costs $1.22 of future taxes in PV, or about $1.47 at λ = 1.2.
  - i = 10%, r = 6%, n = 20 → **1.35**.
  - i = 1.25%, r = 6%, n = 30 (concessional) → **0.55**, i.e., a grant element of about 45%.

  So: keep the SDR growth-based; put borrowing costs in the fiscal layer; and use a *higher hurdle* (an opportunity-cost-of-capital approach, ADB-style 9%) only if the country is capital-rationed and the true constraint is displaced public investment.
- WB (growth-based, about 6%) and ADB (9% hurdle) embody different philosophies: social rate of time preference versus a quasi opportunity cost of capital. A tool should label which one it uses.

## Q8. Low-capacity countries: "cumulated effort is not capital", efficiency gaps, absorptive capacity, recurrent costs, optimism bias, and implications for decision rules

### Takeaway
In low-capacity settings, money spent does not equal capital created. Pritchett's CUDIE argument and IMF evidence of about 40% efficiency gaps in LIDCs point the same way, as do recurrent-cost under-funding (Adam & Bevan) and systematic cost and time overruns (HM Treasury optimism-bias uplifts of up to 66% capex for non-standard civil engineering and 200% for equipment/development). Devarajan, Swaroop & Zou found that developing countries over-allocated to capital relative to current spending. The implied rule changes are:
- haircut expected benefits for efficiency and completion risk;
- uplift costs and delays;
- require funded O&M;
- prefer simpler, rules-based instruments where capability is weak;
- scale up gradually.

### Cited Findings
- **Pritchett (2000)**, "The Tyranny of Concepts: CUDIE (Cumulated, Depreciated, Investment Effort) Is Not Capital," *Journal of Economic Growth* 5(4): 361–384. Core point: "the cost of public investment is not the value of public capital" — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org:443/r/kap/jecgro/v5y2000i4p361-84.html); [World Bank OKR record](https://seal.matrade.gov.my/neuaxis-e/Record/okr-10986-18846).
- **IMF (2015):** average public-investment efficiency gap ≈ 30% (LIDCs 40%, EMs 27%, AEs 13%). The most efficient public investors get twice the growth "bang" per "buck" of the least efficient. Strengthening public-investment-management institutions can close up to two-thirds of the gap. The PIMA framework assesses 15 key institutions (initial sample of 25 countries) — [IMF policy paper](https://www.imf.org/external/pp/longres.aspx?id=4959); [PDF](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/dam/PIMA/Forum/IGEUR/Resources/Making%20Public%20Investment%20More%20Efficient%202015.pdf); [IMF PFM blog, "Trapping the White Elephants"](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/pfmblog/2015/11/trapping-the-white-elephants-can-the-latest-public-investment-boom-avoid-the-mistakes-of-the-past.html); [IMF PFM blog on PIMA launch](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/en/pfmblog/2015/07/imf-launches-new-public-investment-management-assessment). **Date conflict:** the IMF catalogue gives May 2015; the PDF and conference presentations cite June 2015.

"Seemingly productive expenditures, when used in excess, could become unproductive," implying that developing-country governments over-allocated to capital — [abstract record](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/281841); [WB WPS 1082 ("What do governments buy?")](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/1082.htm). **Sample conflict:** the WP version abstract reports 69 countries.
- **Recurrent costs and pacing:** O&M must be sufficient for capital to yield services (Adam & Bevan 2014), and a gradual scale-up was recommended for Uganda — [IMF WP/14/73](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/public-investment-public-finance-and-growth-the-impact-of-distortionary-taxation-recurrent-41518); [IGC Uganda memo](https://www.theigc.org/publications/public-investment-and-growth-implications-recurrent-costs-uganda-project-memo); see also [Adam & Bevan, state capabilities and public investment (IGC 2013)](https://www.theigc.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Chris-Adam---David-Bevan-StateCapabilities-AGF2013.pdf).

  |---|---|---|
  | Standard buildings | 24% / 2% | 4% / 1% |
  | Non-standard buildings | 51% / 4% | 39% / 2% |
  | Non-standard civil engineering | 66% upper | n/a |
  | Equipment/development | 200% / 10% | 54% / 10% |

  A 44% upper bound is cited in a local-authority road-scheme note, consistent with the standard civil engineering category. Worked example: a 28-month non-standard building becomes about 38.9 months at the 39% uplift — [HMT Optimism Bias guidance PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a74dae740f0b65f61322c72/Optimism_bias.pdf); [gov.uk page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-optimism-bias); [Edinburgh Tram Inquiry copy](https://www.edinburghtraminquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/CEC02084818.pdf); [Flyvbjerg, report for the Edinburgh Tram Inquiry](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.12106); [Staffordshire CC note (44%)](https://www.staffordshire.gov.uk/Highways-1/roadworks/stafford/westernaccess/Documents/App-42-Optimism-Bias-note.pdf); [Braintree submission (66%)](https://www.braintree.gov.uk/downloads/file/896/cause-response-to-exd083). **Date conflict:** the guidance originated around 2003, and the gov.uk page is dated April 2013 (republication; figures appear unchanged).

### Inferences
- **Effective capital and expected benefits (my formulation):** `K_eff,t = (1 − δ(m))·K_eff,t−1 + e·I_t`, where e = efficiency/conversion factor (≈ 1 − efficiency gap) and δ(m) is a depreciation rate that rises as the O&M funding ratio m falls below 1. Expected benefits are `E[B_t] = p·e·a·B_t`, with p = completion probability and a = additionality. Expected costs are `E[C] = (1 + OB_capex)·C`, and benefits start after `T·(1 + OB_dur)`.
- **Illustration** (made-up parameters): e = 0.6 (LIDC-type gap), p = 0.85, a = 0.7 turns 100 of headline benefit into about 36 of expected benefit. Combined with a +44% to +66% capex uplift, many headline BCRs of 2–3 fall below 1. *Switching values* (the minimum e, p or a at which ENPV = 0) are therefore more informative than point BCRs in low-capacity settings.
- **Rule changes for low-capacity countries:**
  1. Haircut benefits (e, p) and uplift costs and durations instead of arbitrarily raising the discount rate.
  2. Require funded O&M (a recurrent-cost coverage test) and a maintenance-first priority. Rehabilitating and maintaining existing assets often has higher returns than new build, consistent with Devarajan, Swaroop & Zou's over-allocation-to-capital result and with Adam & Bevan.
  3. Use a country-specific, likely higher λ where informality is high.
  4. Prefer simpler, rules-based instruments (e.g., transfers with low implementation complexity) when capability is weak. A complex project must beat a cash benchmark on expected MVPF after haircuts.
  5. Scale up gradually (absorptive capacity).
  6. Use simpler appraisal (fewer, robust parameters, switching values, reference-class uplifts) rather than spuriously precise IRRs. This echoes DSS (1997).

### Gaps
- UNVERIFIED this session (standard details): World Bank (1997) *World Development Report 1997: The State in a Changing World*. Its two-part strategy is (1) matching the state's role to its capability and (2) raising capability by reinvigorating public institutions. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013) "Escaping Capability Traps through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA)," *World Development* 51: 234–244. Pritchett, Woolcock & Andrews (2013) "Looking Like a State: Techniques of Persistent Failure in State Capability for Implementation," *Journal of Development Studies* 49(1): 1–18. ## Q9. "Efficiency of public spending per se" vs the allocative/Pigouvian wedge: which matters more for welfare?

### Takeaway
Welfare per public dollar is roughly the *product* of (i) the allocative value of the use, meaning the wedge between social and private value net of the counterfactual, and (ii) technical efficiency, divided by the cost of funds. The allocative term varies across uses by multiples (MVPFs range from below 1 to infinite) and can be zero or negative. Technical efficiency typically scales value by about 0.6–0.9. So "doing the right things" usually dominates "doing things cheaply." In low-capacity states, however, implementation failure can drive the technical term toward zero, which makes "can the state actually do this?" a first-order allocative question.

### Cited Findings
- MVPF dispersion across policies is large. Many child health and education policies have very large or infinite MVPFs, while adult-targeted policies mostly sit between 0.5 and 2 — [Hendren & Sprung-Keyser 2020](https://hendren.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/unified-welfare-analysis-government-policies); [Opportunity Insights](https://opportunityinsights.org/?p=2646).
- Technical-efficiency gaps average about 30% (40% in LIDCs), and the most efficient investors get about 2× the growth return of the least efficient — [IMF 2015](https://www.imf.org/external/pp/longres.aspx?id=4959); [IMF PFM blog](https://blog-pfm.imf.org/pfmblog/2015/11/trapping-the-white-elephants-can-the-latest-public-investment-boom-avoid-the-mistakes-of-the-past.html).
- Composition (allocation between current and capital spending) affects growth, and developing countries over-allocated to capital — [Devarajan, Swaroop & Zou 1996](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/281841).
- DSS (1997) argue that appraisal effort should shift from precise rate-of-return calculation to the rationale for public intervention, fiscal impact and fungibility. In other words, allocative and counterfactual questions come before technical precision — [DSS 1997](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/oup/wbrobs/v12y1997i1p35-46.html).
- Cost is not value: spending effort does not equal capital in low-capacity states — [Pritchett 2000](https://ideas.repec.org:443/r/kap/jecgro/v5y2000i4p361-84.html).

### Inferences
- **Decomposition (my formulation):** `Welfare per $ ≈ [ψ·(MSB − counterfactual private/other-policy value)·e·p] / (λ·net fiscal cost per $)`. The Pigouvian/market-failure wedge determines *whether* public money should go to a use at all, and at what scale (Q2's `s*` formula). Technical efficiency e determines how much of that value survives delivery.
- **Answer to the review's framing.** The theoretically correct decision quantity is neither "efficiency of public spending per se" nor the raw Pigouvian gap. It is the **marginal social value per net public dollar relative to the counterfactual**, i.e., the MVPF compared with λ. That quantity combines:
  - the wedge (via WTP net of what would happen anyway),
  - fiscal externalities and revenue returns (via the denominator),
  - technical efficiency and implementation risk (via e and p in expected WTP and in cost uplifts).
- **Which matters more.**
  - Across-programme dispersion in allocative value (orders of magnitude, including infinite) exceeds plausible dispersion in technical efficiency (a factor of about 2). The literature reviewed here (DSS 1997; Hendren & Sprung-Keyser) therefore implies that allocation is usually the bigger lever.
  - Technical inefficiency is not second-order where it is extreme (the CUDIE logic). In low-capacity states the binding question becomes which interventions retain value given weak implementation, which is the WDR 1997 "match role to capability" logic (UNVERIFIED citation).
  - The literature's practical landing is to do both: screen on rationale and counterfactual first, then haircut for implementation, then rank by value per net public dollar.

### Gaps
- No single empirical study was found that formally decomposes welfare losses from public spending into "allocative" and "technical" components across countries. The "which matters more" conclusion is an inference from the separate magnitudes above.

## Q10. Implications for a practical tool (spreadsheet/Python CBA layer)

### Takeaway
Each concept maps onto an explicit, auditable parameter or rule:
- λ (MCPF), applied to net fiscal cost;
- an MVPF output with an infinity flag;
- an additionality share a, efficiency factor e, completion probability p and optimism-bias uplifts;
- an O&M coverage test;
- SDR choice (growth-based WB rule, ADB 9%/6%, or Green Book 3.5% with declining rates) with sensitivity;
- a borrowing-cost multiplier in the fiscal layer;
- distribution weights (η);
- an SPC path.

Decision outputs should be ENPV_λ ≥ 0, MVPF ≥ λ, a budget-constrained ranking by MVPF, switching values, and a fiscal-sustainability check.

### Cited Findings
- λ defaults: Africa average MCF ≈ 1.2 (all instruments) and ≈ 1.1 (cheapest instrument, usually VAT); informality is the key driver — [Auriol & Warlters 2012](https://publications.ut-capitole.fr/id/eprint/975/1/auriol_975.pdf). Norway's convention is 1.2 — [SSB 2015](https://www.ssb.no/en/offentlig-sektor/artikler-og-publikasjoner/the-size-of-the-marginal-cost-of-public-funds). High-tax welfare states reach about 2 on the participation margin — [Kleven & Kreiner 2006](https://www.henrikkleven.com/research/published/kleven-kreiner_jpube2006.pdf).
- MVPF definition, with net cost including fiscal externalities — [Finkelstein & Hendren 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.34.4.146); benchmark ranges — [Hendren & Sprung-Keyser 2020](https://hendren.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/unified-welfare-analysis-government-policies).
- SDR: WB rule of 1–2× per-capita growth (typically 6%, range 2–10%) with mandatory sensitivity analysis — [WB 2016 note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099610503022315638/pdf/IDU02e978a2a023f704c140bb1b082595e67e593.pdf). ADB uses 9% (6% for environmental-benefit projects) — [ADB](https://www.adb.org/documents/guidelines-economic-analysis-projects); [ADB TCR](https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/project-documents/47330/47330-001-tcr-en.pdf).
- Efficiency factor proxies from IMF gaps: LIDC 40%, EM 27%, AE 13% — [IMF 2015](https://www.imf.org/external/pp/longres.aspx?id=4959).
- Optimism-bias uplift table — [HMT optimism-bias guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a74dae740f0b65f61322c72/Optimism_bias.pdf).
- Distribution weights: η = 1.3 (sensitivity 0.8, 2.0), median reference — [CRAN greenbook](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/greenbook/greenbook.pdf).
- SPC: US$40/80 (2020) → 50/100 (2030) → 78/156 (2050), 2017 US$, 2.25%/yr — [WB 2024 SPC note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099553203142424068/pdf/IDU1c94753bb1819e14c781831215580060675b1.pdf).
- Recurrent-cost and appropriability concerns justify a separate fiscal layer — [Adam & Bevan 2014](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/public-investment-public-finance-and-growth-the-impact-of-distortionary-taxation-recurrent-41518).

**Core equations for the CBA layer** (per project j; PV at r):
- Expected economic benefits: `B*_t = w·a·e·p·B_t` (benefits start after `T·(1 + OB_d)`; environmental benefits use SPC_t·ΔCO2e_t).
- Resource costs: `C*_t = (1 + OB_c)·C^res_t` (at shadow prices; fees, taxes and subsidies excluded as transfers).
- Net fiscal cost: `G^net_t = κ-adjusted outlays (capex + O&M + subsidies) − fees_t − α·ΔY_t − avoided spending_t`.
- `ENPV_λ = PV(B*) − PV(C*) − (λ − 1)·PV(G^net)`; decision: ENPV_λ ≥ 0.
- `MVPF = PV(B* − beneficiaries' own costs incl. fees) / PV(G^net)`. If PV(G^net) ≤ 0 and the numerator is > 0, flag as "self-financing (MVPF = ∞)". Decision: MVPF ≥ λ (equivalently ENPV_λ ≥ 0 when C* is entirely publicly financed).
- Budget-constrained portfolio: rank by MVPF (or B*/G^net) and fund down the list until the envelope is exhausted. The cut-off MVPF is the shadow price of the budget μ: μ > λ signals that the envelope is too small, and μ < λ that it is too large.
- Switching values: solve for e, p, a, OB_c or λ such that ENPV_λ = 0, and report them alongside the point estimates.
- Fiscal-sustainability screen: `PV(α·ΔY + fees) / PV(capex + O&M + debt service)` and the debt-ratio path `Δd ≈ (i − g)d + (I + OM − α·ΔY − fees)/Y`.
- **Cash benchmark** for low-capacity contexts: a complex project's expected MVPF (after e, p, OB) should exceed that of a well-targeted transfer. Adult transfer MVPFs are typically ≈ 0.5–2 in Hendren & Sprung-Keyser's U.S. evidence; LMIC values are scarce.

**Guardrails:**
- Never count fees or taxes both as benefits and as cost reductions.
- Keep the SDR separate from the financing cost.
- Value aid at the budget shadow price (fungibility), not at zero.
- Apply the SPC only in the economic, not the financial, analysis.
- Show results with and without λ, weights and haircuts so reviewers can see what drives the ranking.

### Gaps
- The default values for p and for a (beyond pure public goods) are assumptions, not sourced estimates. Calibrating them would need reference-class data (e.g., World Bank or IEG project completion and outcome ratings, which were not retrieved).
- Mapping IMF efficiency gaps one-for-one into project-level haircuts is an approximation (see Q1 Gaps).
- The WB SPC income-group differentiation, the ADB 6% eligibility list, the Green Book SDR schedule and quintile weights, and the WB IPF "three questions" wording all need checking against primary texts before being hard-coded (see the respective Gaps).
