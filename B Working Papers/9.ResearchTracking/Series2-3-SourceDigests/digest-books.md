## 0. Headline findings (read first)

1. **The book's core architecture is already Series 2 plus Series 3.** Chapter 14 (ll. 1070–1094) states it as "one structure, several views". There is one pool of loss-absorbing equity. It (a) co-invests in construction and sells out on completion, (b) stands as second loss behind a sovereign first loss and ahead of a AAA guarantor, and (c) shares profits with early movers. Chapters 10, 11, 18, 19 and 20 each present a "view" of that pool. The seven-layer stack in ch 14 is the canonical waterfall for **[S2.1]**.
2. **"Guarantee the offtaker, not the loan"** (ch 8, ll. 610–620) is the book's own formulation of 2.1's "guarantee the contract not the debt". **CONFLICT (internal):** chs 18–19 and footnote 14 revert to *loan* guarantees: "MIGA-style guarantees … supporting low-cost loans", the CIIF "as insurer against non-performing loans", and a "Multi-Tiered … Loan Guarantee System". Series 2 needs to pick one formulation and note the other.
3. **The waterfall order and labels differ across chapters.** Ch 8 calls the equity fund the "first-loss fund" beneath the bonds, with a developed-market sovereign or reinsurer (Swiss Re / Munich Re) above. Ch 10 has a temporary lending facility that pays the IPP, with the buffer fund as *final* guarantor and the SPV also guaranteed by a AAA entity. Ch 14 and ch 19 have developer equity, then the sovereign (first loss, as a ledger entry), then the buffer (second loss), then AAA (final). Use ch 14 and ch 19 for the drafts.
4. **The book retracted its own fiscal-space claim.** Ch 14 (l. 1058) "retires" the ch 10 claim that the structure "adds nothing to developing country government liabilities". What replaces it is a *conditional, self-controlled contingent liability*. The 2.1 SIDS energy-as-a-service section must not repeat the older claim that the debt counts "neither in substantive nor measured terms". That claim appears in the WP's SPIFF text (WP l. 2283) and implicitly in ch 18 ("an energy bill, not a debt").
5. **The book's position on currency changed between chapters.** Ch 8 says currency risk "cannot be guaranteed away". Ch 14 calls that "too pessimistic": the risk can be *hedged* (TCX), *priced*, *shared* (partial indexation, declining tariff) or *avoided* (local-currency savings vehicles). 2.1's "currency choice" should take the ch 14 position.
6. **The CIT numbers in the draft match ch 20 exactly** ($5bn = $1bn donor + $4bn country; $95bn equity; $400bn debt; seven regional child funds; about $2tn per year). Ch 20 has internal arithmetic inconsistencies, listed in §5.20, that the 3.2 draft should resolve rather than inherit.
7. **There are three names for the trust family.** They are the Climate Incentives Trust (ch 20 title), the Climate Investment Trust (WP3, WP l. 2285; ch 14 fn 14) and the Climate Investment and Insurance Fund (ch 19; once mistyped "Climate Investment and Infrastructure Fund", WP l. 1471). Paper 3.2's "Investment/Incentives" naming reflects a real ambiguity in the sources.
8. **The forest-value argument "developed at length in the companion volume"** (T&C l. 1740) is in fact about two pages: RPWT ch 6, "The Price Set by a Technology Competition" (RPWT ll. 313–377). At the price of air capture (about $200/t), one ton offset buys about 5 t of avoided deforestation. Mitigation is allocated non-uniformly (Amazon tipping: losing the next 2% would cause loss of 75% of the remainder). The concept of financial net zero sits underneath. "At length" overstates it. See §7.
9. **A source exists for 2.1 that the drafts may not have used.** WP Appendix 3 (WP ll. 2269–2283) contains "Paper WP2: SPIFF (Solar Payments Intermediary and Financing Facility)". It is in effect a three-component prototype of 2.1: an RE contract intermediary managed by MIGA, two-stage project finance, and pay-as-you-go energy contracts for SIDS and countries in debt distress. "Paper WP3: Climate Investment Trust" follows it.

## 1. Preface and "A Note on Terms" (ll. 87–173)

**Thesis (l. 89).** The binding constraint on the transition in most of the world is neither technology nor the total quantity of capital. It is the *price* of capital, which is set by risk, and institutions can change risk.

**Numbers.**
- The same panel produces electricity at "around 80 percent more" cost when the borrowing rate is 12% rather than 4%, and at more than twice the cost at 15% (calculated in ch 14).
- Debt-to-GDP rises by about **70 pp** under a green big push at current bond rates, against about **10 pp** at World Bank rates. The source is an "illustrative simulation by the Independent High-Level Expert Group on Climate Finance", and it is explicitly glossed as a demonstration of scale rather than a forecast. Ch 19 attributes the same figure to "a recent LSE study" (fn 19: Songwe, Stern, Bhattacharya 2022, *Finance for climate action*). **DOUBT:** PR (Decisions, item 1) records that the underlying assumptions could not be verified from the IHLEG report.

**Timing argument.**
- Learning curves, *financial learning* ("lenders who have watched five PPAs perform will price the sixth differently") and political-economy lock-in all mean the cost of capital falls *as a consequence of* deployment.
- These dynamics have thresholds. A transition financed at 12% "stays a subsidy programme rather than becoming an industry".
- Hence dynamic incentives that reward acting *now*. **Cross-reference error:** the text cites "Chapters 20 and 22" for this; the material is in chs 18 and 20.
- Hence also recycling structures: "a fund that doubles every three years is a mechanism for compounding and a grant is not".

**The "chance" claim.** A large part of the premium is "the price of an absent track record", which can be removed "without anyone bearing a loss". PR (gap 7) holds that the wording is "a large part", not "most".

**Scope.** The book covers mitigation only and excludes adaptation, because adaptation assets rarely have a contracted revenue stream (l. 127). A companion volume carries the feebate and carbon-market material, green growth, the literature notes and the institutional briefs.

**"Borrowing from the Future" (ll. 129–137)** **[S3-ov]**
- The intergenerational Pareto improvement: if the damage prevented exceeds the debt service, no generation loses. "The beneficiaries cannot yet sign the contract."
- A sketched mechanism: countries borrow in proportion to historical responsibility, starting at **USD 1 per historical ton**. The US is the largest historical emitter at **400 billion tonnes**. The maximum liability is **USD 100/t** for a country joining now, with only USD 1 payable at present. Countries would issue **SDRs against historical emissions** rather than bonds.
- RPWT ch 7 repeats this as "sovereign co-guaranteed long-term borrowing — hundred-year maturities — repaid in proportion to historical emissions".

**Defined terms (ll. 141–173), for consistency across series.**
- *Cost of capital*: the required return; the largest component of solar LCOE.
- *PPA*: 20–25 years, usually with a utility.
- *Offtaker*: the PPA buyer, usually a state utility. Whether it pays is "the largest single risk in an operating solar project".
- *Reverse auction*: the government fixes quantity; the lowest bid wins a PPA.
- *CfD*: a top-up below the strike price and a payback above it.
- *Guarantee*: "do not eliminate risk; they move it to a party better able to bear it".
- *MDB*: IBRD lends to governments, IFC to private projects, MIGA insures political risk.
- *Preferred creditor status*: "the observed fact that countries repay development banks even when they default on other creditors".
- *SPV*, *tranche*, *first loss / second loss*.
- *Concessional finance*: "much of it can be manufactured instead".
- *DPF*: budget lending conditioned on reforms.
- *PforR*: disbursement against verified results.
- *FIF*: a pooled fund hosted by the WB with its own governance.
- *LCOE*.

## 2. Part I: The Price of Money

### Ch 1. Why Capital Costs Too Much (ll. 177–295)

**Core argument.**
- The gap between needed and available green finance is close to an order of magnitude, and closing it means building new channels.
- Barriers: transaction costs (which fall hardest on small projects), the complexity of climate funds (a "capability tax"), and innovation risk (no local track record).
- Global resources are limited, so the aim is to "buy virtuous circles rather than megawatts".
- The most important structural idea in the book is to separate the *incentive* to transform from the *cost* of transforming. It appears three times: the feebate (companion volume), derisking (Part III) and concessionality (Part IV).

**Numbers.**
- IEA green financing need of about **$3tn/yr**, split roughly equally between generation, end-use and infrastructure (IEA 2021a, 2021b).
- EMDE excluding China: about **$600bn/yr** of capital spending by 2030.
- **$138.9bn** total 2000–2017, cumulative (IRENA 2020). **DOUBT:** the bibliography source is IRENA (2020) "Finance for Renewables in Developing Countries Is on the Rise". That figure is very likely *international public* finance flows to developing countries, not total investment, so the "order of magnitude" inference is shaky.
- Africa could generate 8% of its electricity from hydro but generates 2.7%. **DOUBT:** this looks garbled. Hydro supplies a far larger share of African electricity than 2.7%.
- LAC microfinance: 4% of budgets went to clean energy in 2012 (Forcella et al. 2017).
- Transition delay of up to 40 years without collective implementation (Van der Ploeg and Rezai 2016).

**Mechanisms.**
- *Division of labour.* Governments set policy. Governments and MDBs together derisk: "the government holds the policy levers, the MDB holds the credit rating". MDBs and DFIs structure instruments. *Risk-absorbing equity funds take the first loss none of the others can afford.*
- *Developing-country paradox* (l. 247). Government guarantees are indispensable, but government balance sheets are the binding constraint. The resolution is "dedicated risk-absorbing funds that enhance the derisking capability of governments and MDBs — extending what those institutions can guarantee without extending what they must ultimately pay". This is the seed of **[S2.2]** and **[S3.2]**.
- *Investor mismatch* (l. 287). Commercial investors accept risk but want short tenor. Institutional investors accept long tenor but want low risk. Green infrastructure is long and risky, so Part III splits projects into a long low-risk tranche and a short high-risk one. This is the rationale for two-stage finance in **[S2.2]**.
- A derisking fund is preferable to a direct subsidy of the same size because it "can be leveraged many times over".

**Tipping typology** (Van der Ploeg 2021): policy, technology cost, social, political-economy, technological business model, *financial business model* (a standardised financing structure, "the explicit objective of the schemes in Part III"), and international tipping.

**Citations.** IEA (2021a, b); IRENA (2020); Altamirano (2021); Van der Ploeg (2021); Collier and Venables (2012, 2014); Hafner et al. (2020); GGGI (2016); Forcella et al. (2017); Van der Ploeg and Rezai (2016); Besley and Persson (2019); Van der Ploeg (2016).

### Ch 2. Financial Constraints as a Barrier (ll. 297–341)

**Core argument.**
- Lower risk does two separate things: it lowers the *cost* of finance (price), and it raises the *quantity* available (whether a project can be financed at all).
- Solar is the right test case for financial rather than technical reasons. Once operational, O&M is minimal, there is no fuel, insolation is predictable, and the power can be sold at an auctioned price. **"A solar park looks less like an industrial asset and more like a bond"** (l. 317), so it should be financeable at bond-like rates. The gap between actual and deserved financing cost "is the value that derisking structures exist to capture".
- Paris-aligned portfolio shifts can cause *outflows* from non-aligned EMDEs. Footnote 1: NZAM covers $57tn AUM; NZBA covers more than 40% of global banking assets.
- The perceived/real risk distinction (Battiston et al. 2021). Real risk must be reduced or transferred. Perceived risk is addressed by track record, standardisation and "a credible institution alongside".
- *Single-buyer model.* The creditworthiness of the whole programme depends on one state counterparty (fns 3–4, Foster and Rana 2020).
- Auctioned PPAs are "the vital policy component that makes financial risk reduction possible at all".
- The objective is to "reduce financial risk by *more* than the public financial input" (l. 333).

### Ch 3. Why Risk Matters: A Primer (ll. 343–395) **[S2.1, S2.2]**

**Core argument.**
- Risk causes direct harm, but more importantly it raises borrowing costs. "Reducing risk is … the cheapest available method of reducing the price of clean electricity."
- Risk can be *reduced* or *transferred*. Transfer "works normally almost all of the time and then fail[s] together". A structure built only on transfer "has hidden its risk". This objection is answered in ch 14.

**Risk by party.**
- Investor: market and FX, construction, political.
- Government: energy price, and fiscal risk from climate (physical and transition).
- MDB: borrower credit risk.

**Allocation rule.** Construction risk goes to the developer, policy risk to the government, and currency risk "ideally, nowhere".

**Four strategies.**
1. *Diversification*: idiosyncratic risk shrinks with pooling, systemic risk does not. "The single most powerful and most underused property in climate finance". This is why the equity fund can cross-guarantee projects it does not own.
2. *Hedging*: options (insurance) and forwards. A CfD is "a hedge against the government's own policy, sold by the government."
3. *Security and relationship*: the MDB's key asset is that countries value the relationship.
4. *Seniority / tranching*: "the sum of the parts is worth more than the whole".

**Defined term: *Green superseniority*** (ll. 391–395). Green lending is privileged as a claim ranking above other claims on a sovereign or utility. It is the cheapest claim, so the cost of capital falls most where fiscal space is tightest. The cost falls on other creditors, so it must be established *before* other debt is issued or negotiated in a restructuring. It "requires no new money at all". The book promises that "Part IV returns to it", but Part IV only mentions it in one line of the ch 15 toolkit (l. 1219). That is a gap Series 2 or 3 could fill.

## 3. Part II: Contracts

### Ch 4. Why Contracts Beat Prices (ll. 399–437) **[S3-ov]**

**Core argument.**
- A carbon price tells an investor today's cost of emitting, not the cost in 2045. An ETS is worse than a tax because the price absorbs all adjustment. A tax sits in the budget and can change annually. "A price signal that nobody will finance is not a price signal; it is an announcement."
- A reverse auction fixes quantity and so coordinates intertemporally.
- Claims 3 and 4 of the "four claims about environmental taxation" are: (3) procurement auctions coordinate intertemporally better than given prices; (4) **the financing cost of renewables is lower under procurement auctions** ("the hinge of this whole book").
- The auction is not a second-best to a carbon price. It does what a carbon price cannot: "creating a contract. And a contract is a financeable asset."
- The bond-like quality comes from the *contract*, not the sunshine. A merchant solar park "is a commodity producer with a fixed cost base".

**Olson's logic of collective action (Olson 1965).** A policy is feasible when benefits are concentrated and costs diffuse. Cost-of-capital policy concentrates benefits on developers, lenders, finance ministries and consumers, and puts its costs on a guarantor's balance sheet "in states of the world that mostly do not occur". It is "a policy shaped to pass". The **[S3-ov]** framing of guarantees as an incentive route rests here.

### Ch 5. Commitment Devices: CfDs and Price Floors (ll. 439–467) **[S3.3]**

- Credibility has two uses: international and investment.
- **Implication for conditionality (l. 451):** "a prior action consisting of a long-term commitment should count for more than one that does not. A carbon tax legislated for this year is a gesture. A carbon tax with a statutory floor trajectory and contractual counterparties is a commitment." This is directly usable for codifying sustainability-linked sovereign lending (**[S3.3]**).
- A two-sided CfD "is not a subsidy". It swaps price risk for quantity certainty, and the government takes the risk because it controls the policy. CfDs are proven in electricity and emerging hydrogen markets.
- Floors are blunter but force coal out of the merit order. Floors and CfDs are complements: the floor operates at system level, the CfD converts it into project-level collateral.

### Ch 6. The Power Sector and Carbon Pricing (ll. 469–493)

- Power matters twice: it is a large emitter itself and the carrier for electrification.
- A carbon price acts through three timescales: generation (hours), retirement (months and years), investment (decades).
- Power is merit-order "economic", but in EMDEs it is state-owned. A vertically integrated state utility under a political tariff "may not respond at all".
- Pass-through problems:
  - Regressivity, solved by targeted transfers.
  - It works *against electrification*, which argues for **output-based rebating**: charge on fuel, refund per kWh.
  - Credibility: a pass-through "that cannot survive a bad winter is not a signal investors will finance against".
- **Cross-reference error:** the chapter says "Chapter 2 returns to this", which points backwards.

## 4. Part III: The Instruments

### Ch 7. Forms of Guarantees (ll. 497–541) **[S2.1]**

**Core argument.**
- Do not guarantee all risks. Target the risks created by low confidence in state institutions: policy and payment risk from government or state entities, and political risk. "A guarantee against everything is an equity investment with extra steps." Targeting buys "a large reduction in perceived risk for a small assumption of real risk."
- A *combination* of forms is worth more than the sum of its parts.

**Forms.**
- **Sovereign guarantee**, either of the SOE contract or of the IPP borrowing (fn 6: IRENA, "Sovereign Guarantees – RE Finance Brief 01", 2020). It is a contingent liability, and that is the binding constraint: guarantee capacity "runs out fastest" where it is most needed. There are two routes to reduce the contingent liability: *risk transfer* (MIGA reinsurance) and *risk reduction* (IBRD relationships) (fn 7: MIGA 2013).
- **MIGA**: Political Risk Insurance (for example against nationalisation) and Credit Enhancement. MIGA guarantees are typically *reinsured*, which "recruits the capacity of the global insurance market".
- **IBRD guarantee with sovereign indemnity.** The IBRD guarantees an SOE and the state indemnifies the IBRD. "The IBRD acts as a commitment device, using its strong credit rating to allow a government to guarantee its own state-owned enterprise at a *higher* rating than it could achieve directly." The key line (l. 525): **"The concessionality is generated not by spending money but by borrowing credibility."**
- **Intermediate contracting (SECI).** IPPs contract with the Solar Energy Corporation of India, not the Discoms, and Discoms pay SECI the same fee. The federal government indemnifies SECI, and states guarantee their Discoms. This is "the IBRD principle applied domestically" (fn 8: Gadre et al. 2020, BNEF, "India's Clean Power Revolution").
- **Securitisation of PPA proceeds.** An SPV receives the PPA payments and lends to the IPP. With guaranteed payments plus a highly rated guarantor, it issues low-coupon green bonds to the pool of investors committed to the Paris goals. The SPV can be housed in a global investment bank or an MDB. The order of payments is guaranteed payments to the SPV, then bondholders, then equity. "The bondholder is therefore exposed to the guarantor rather than to the developer."

### Ch 8. Synthetic Sovereign Bonds for Green Energy Investment (ll. 543–676): the flagship for **[S2.1]**

**Summary.** These are bonds "partially co-guaranteed by a consortium of developed-country governments", arranged by MDBs without using EMDE fiscal space (fn 9: "relates to the idea of a 'climate club'"). The underlying assets are derisked *at the PPA* through national or regional SECI-like intermediaries.

**Scale (IEA 2021a, b).** About $3tn/yr of clean energy investment by 2030, about a third ($1tn/yr) for supply. Split roughly equally between China, the developed world and the developing world. The chapter restricts itself to *supply* in EMDEs (fn 10: electrified end-use is "the more general T2B report"; T2B is Trillions to Billions).

**Scaling problem.** Some constraints are *endogenous* and relax only through expansion. Physical constraints include grid, supply chains, minerals (fn 11: IEA critical minerals), land and labour.

**PPAs and fiscal risk (ll. 581–595).**
1. *PPAs are a macro risk.* Worked example: a contract at 5c/kWh flat real 2023–2030 while new solar falls to 2.5c/kWh creates a loss of 2.5c on every kWh (as with Europe's feed-in tariffs). Remedy: a **declining tariff**, for example **6c now falling to 3c by 2030**.
2. *Who pays for the decline* (added after peer review, PR gap 8). It moves cost-curve risk to the developer, who bids a higher start price. The design is still right because the offtaker is least able to bear an open-ended real commitment, but the saving "is smaller than the headline decline suggests". The schedule should be set against a **conservative** cost path.
3. *Curtailment.* **Take-or-pay** puts curtailment on the offtaker and the cash flow is bond-like. **Take-and-pay** puts it on the developer, who prices it. In a weak grid the difference can be "several percentage points on the cost of capital".
4. PPAs also *reduce* macro risk elsewhere, because certainty lowers the tariff.
5. PPAs can be made more certain: "the efficient thing to guarantee is the *payor* in the reverse auction".

**Definition (ll. 597–608).** *Green synthetic-sovereign-rated bonds* are rated like the top developed-country sovereigns and backed by real assets *and* by payment guarantees from an AAA entity. The goals are to cut the cost of capital, reach institutional investors and domestic savings at scale, invest in poorer countries, and scale under constrained fiscal space.

**Guarantee the offtaker, not the loan (ll. 610–620).** Three compounding reasons:
1. *Efficiency*: it isolates payment risk. Guaranteeing the loan bundles policy, payment, construction and FX risk.
2. *Recourse*: if the borrower is guaranteed, the guarantor pays and the SOE faces no consequence. If the payment is guaranteed, the offtaker "can still repay the guarantor over the long term. The obligation converts into a credit relationship rather than vanishing".
3. *Incentive*: arrears become a debt owed to a repeat counterparty.

**SECI model (ll. 622–630).** SECI holds credit lines with the RBI and long-term indemnities from state governments, and pays generators if the Discoms are late. "Nobody has fixed the Discoms. What has been fixed is the *sequencing* of who waits for whom … the cost of that rearrangement is a credit line rather than a subsidy."

**Scaling the model (ll. 632–638).**
- National intermediaries for large countries (Nigeria, Brazil). For small countries, **regional clubs sharing one SECI-like institution**, which diversifies across sovereigns.
- Best suited to bonds over *operational* portfolios.
- "The World Bank could help create SECI-like structures without taking the risk onto its own balance sheet."
- **Developed-country guarantors need a carbon value**, meaning certified avoided emissions. The legal foothold is **paragraph 108 of the Paris decision** (the social value of mitigation activities). "The guarantee is the vehicle, and the global social cost of carbon is what determines how much of it a developed country should be willing to write." This bridges **[S2.1]** and **[S3.1]**.

**Currency (ll. 640–650).**
- Hard-currency PPAs behave like external debt.
- Mitigation 1: the declining PPA.
- Mitigation 2: local currency, but then design the bonds explicitly for *domestic savings*.
- "No clean solution here within this instrument." This is superseded in ch 14 (**CONFLICT, internal**).
- **Cross-reference error:** "Chapter 12 raised" the green numéraire, but ch 12 comes *after* ch 8.

**Trade balance.** Imported panels are a hard-currency import bill, so industrial policy is part of the financing answer.

**Mechanics of issuance (ll. 658–668).**
- **Bond priority to cash flows**: securitised PPA cash flows go *directly* to bondholders. "The bond becomes, in substance, a claim on a highly rated institution."
- **Construction risk**: a bank finances construction, and the bond is a *refinancing* on completion. "The single most important structural feature … the cheap long-term money never bears construction risk, and the expensive short-term money is only outstanding for eighteen months."
- **Secondary guarantees**: a *dual-use equity fund* co-invests and, for a fee, cross-guarantees *other* projects' bonds. Above this "first-loss fund" sits a final guarantor: a developed-market sovereign or a reinsurer (Swiss Re, Munich Re).
- **Stack** (l. 668): contracted revenue; guarantee at the offtaker by a SECI-like intermediary indemnified by the state; securitisation into an SPV that pays bondholders first; first-loss equity buffer beneath; sovereign or reinsurance guarantee above.
- **CONFLICT (labels):** here the equity fund is "first loss". In chs 14 and 19 it is *second* loss behind the sovereign.

**Conclusion.** The key innovation is a central counterparty for PPAs. It can underpin **AAA bonds or bank-deposit money-market funds**, and it creates a *safe asset*: "a country that can manufacture investment-grade instruments out of its own sunshine". This is relevant to **[S2.2]** (asset classes).

### Ch 9. What the Record Shows (ll. 678–730) **[S2.1 evidence base]**

**Five lessons, each with evidence.**

1. **Cost of capital drives everything** (fn 12: Komendantova et al. 2019; Steckel and Jakob 2018).
   - IEA Cost of Capital Observatory, launched 2022 with WEF, ETH Zurich and Imperial: for utility-scale solar in EMDEs in 2021, financing was about **half of LCOE**, against **25–30%** in advanced economies and China.
   - Median WACC just under **10%** in India and Mexico and about **13%** in Brazil, which is 2–3 times the OECD level.
   - The 2023 update (adding Kenya, Peru, Senegal, Vietnam) found the EM cost of capital still more than twice the AE level. A 1 pp narrowing would save about **$150bn/yr**.
   - Merchant projects carry higher WACC. About **90%** of surveyed projects had a PPA, FiT or CfD.
2. **Solar plus auctioned PPAs is the right foundation.**
   - South Africa's REIPPPP (2011): 20-year PPAs with Eskom, backed by a National Treasury guarantee. Tariffs fell about **70%** from bid window 1 to 3. In BW6, solar cleared at **R0.62–0.75/kWh** against a retail tariff of **R1.85**. More than **R256bn** invested, more than **7,300 MW**, about **95 projects** (Eberhard and Naude 2017).
   - IFC Scaling Solar: templated documents, a pre-negotiated financing package, and a WBG guarantee on the offtaker. Zambia 2016: **6.02 US c/kWh** for 25 years, against about **12c** unsolicited a year earlier. Senegal 2018: **3.80 and 3.98 euro cents**. Uzbekistan: **2.7 US c**.
   - **Cautions:** only **three countries** completed projects. Emery (2023, Energy for Growth Hub) found the WBG understated the concessional elements (partial risk guarantees, concessional loans, an interest-rate swap). A neighbouring finance minister cited Zambia's price to cancel **14 projects at 11.5c**. Moral: derisking should be "visible, priced, and replicated".
3. **Policy and payment risk can be almost eliminated, not quite (SECI honest account).**
   - SECI was set up in 2011. It signs a 25-year PPA with the generator and a back-to-back PSA with the Discom.
   - **Payment Security Fund** (2016, central money, replenished from Discom payments and developer penalties).
   - **2017 tripartite agreement** (RBI, centre and states): the centre can withhold transfers from a state whose Discom defaults.
   - **2019** letter-of-credit requirement.
   - Tariffs fell to about **Rs 2.44/kWh** by 2017.
   - But Discom dues were **Rs 1.32tn** in mid-2022 and **Rs 779bn** in March 2024, of which about **Rs 273bn** was overdue. Central liquidity injections of **Rs 90,000 crore** (2020), later raised to **135,000 crore**. In the change-in-law episode, SECI reimbursed developers first, and lenders then questioned whether SECI's balance sheet could bear the timing gap.
   - "The risk was moved … It was not eliminated, and the party it was moved to has needed periodic support from the party behind it." **DOUBT (flagged by the author):** PR says the SECI arrears figures come from press and portal reporting and are "indicative" (PRAAPTI).
4. **Splitting debt and equity wins.** Insurers and pension funds must hold low-risk paper and central banks buy bonds, so IG debt is cheap. SA real equity returns fell from **17% (BW1) to about 9.5% (BW4)**. That is track record appearing in the data.
5. **Long-term relationships and credit ledgers.** "A failure to pay need not be a default. It can be a ledger entry, repaid over time." Examples: SECI, the RBI tripartite mechanism, the SA Treasury guarantee. Caveat: "nursery-slope arrangements" for domestic firms.

**Decomposing the premium (ll. 724–730).** The IEA Kenya and Senegal analysis attributes **60–90%** of the African solar cost of capital to the base (sovereign) rate, against **35%** in China and about **10%** in AEs. The sector premium is driven by regulation, offtaker reliability and transmission. Conclusion: "between a third and a half of the premium in a typical emerging market, and a smaller share in the poorest" is dissolvable by track record and guarantees. The rest is sovereign risk, "and it yields only to the safe-intermediary structures of Part IV". This split is important for positioning 2.1 (sector premium) against 2.2 and Series 3 (sovereign base rate).

### Ch 10. Goals, Criteria, and a Worked Derisking Scheme (ll. 732–812) **[S2.1, S2.2]**

**Goals.**
1. Reduce investor risk using MDB due diligence. The *signalling* effect is "remarkably capital-efficient".
2. Let IPPs tap international capital markets. Move guarantees *off* MDB balance sheets, and make derisking profitable because "a for-profit equity fund can presumably attract considerably more capital than a facility whose purpose is to absorb losses".
3. Local financial products through international intermediation (PAYG micro-solar on mobile rails: "the binding constraint is sometimes the payment rail").

**Criteria.**
- *Cost-risk effectiveness*: includes synergy, for example insuring policy risk also acts as a commitment device and reduces the risk it insures. Crossing a ratings threshold matters most.
- *Capital efficiency*: "Capital efficiency achieved by obscuring where the risk has gone is not capital efficiency; it is the 2008 financial crisis."
- *System-dynamic potential*: self-financing, a virtuous circle, something resembling a global commons fund.

**Worked scheme.**
- *Foundations*:
  - Approved developers with land and grid set aside. **Retired coal grid connections** are the most valuable asset. Community solar is the other model.
  - Open, best-practice auctions in increasing *number*.
  - Tariff design: part of the tariff in international currency **in exchange for** a declining tariff or a **sunset clause** with later payments indexed to the international cost of new solar.
  - PPAs guaranteed "ideally not directly but through a long-term international lending facility that lends temporarily to IPPs to cover any default by the SOE … then recovers the money from the SOE or the government later". The **Global Climate Equity Derisking Buffer Fund** is the final guarantor, "at risk only if neither the SOE nor the government ever paid". This is a *contracted* waterfall with a liquidity line, a direct antecedent of 2.1.
- *SPV*:
  - The portion of PPA revenue covering debt service goes *directly* to the SPV. The SPV issues "green derisked bonds".
  - Several SPVs may exist globally, managed by a global investment bank and ringfenced.
  - Losses are covered ultimately by the buffer fund. AAA sovereigns or MDBs also guarantee the SPV.
  - The target is Germany, US or Swiss sovereign rates.
  - **Bond currency mix matches the underlying PPAs**, so the SPV takes no currency position.
- ***Global Climate Risk Equity Fund***:
  - Investors are governments and private individuals with a cap of **$100,000** each.
  - Profit sharing of about **25%** to the populations of participating countries. The remainder is reinvested, "a sort of supplementary pension fund for the population".
- *Fit between SPV and fund*: the fund takes a stake or, better, an **option** to buy equity at planned investment cost, so the fund bears no construction risk.
- **Worked example.**
  - A $1m PV project, 80% SPV debt and 20% ($200k) equity from the developer during construction.
  - The developer grants the fund an option to buy **$150k** of shares at cost on completion.
  - If the cost of capital moves from **12% to 8%**, electricity cost falls by about a fifth (ch 14 table: 6.5c to 5.0c).
  - Gain split in the first deal: about **¾ of the equity uplift to the developer and ¼ to the public fund**. The consumer's share comes via the *next auction* (SA tariffs down 70%, returns 17% to 9.5%). This paragraph was added after PR gap 10.
- *Claimed advantages*:
  1. The SPV can cut debt costs toward developed-sovereign levels.
  2. A hybrid profit-recycling public fund is a genuine innovation.
  3. "Does not weigh down anyone's balance sheet". The developing-country exposure is a contingent liability that crystallises only if the country reverses its own policy. This claim is **revised by ch 14**.
- *Further work*: derisk the debt enough for **central bank repo or asset-purchase eligibility**. Standardisation "turns a series of bespoke deals into an asset class" (**[S2.2]** headline).
- **Naming inconsistency within the chapter:** "Global Climate Equity Derisking Buffer Fund", "Global Climate Risk Equity Fund" and "Global Climate Equity Fund" are all used.

### Ch 11. Procurement Auctions and a Clean Energy Equity Fund (ll. 814–850)

The chapter is headed "Drafted as a blog pitch with Anders".

- The procurement framing: the transition can cut fiscal costs and risks and create jobs, but needs forward planning and investor risk management. Auctions and guaranteed revenues enable "cheap financing off the government's balance sheet".
- IEA about **$4tn/yr** globally, **$1–2tn/yr** in EMDEs. **DOUBT:** inconsistent with the **$3tn** used in chs 1, 8 and 15.
- Finance must be at "rates comparable to those of the World Bank itself".
- **Additionality** (ll. 830–836): climate finance must be additional to development finance, because (a) needs are additional and (b) "if a government undertakes a difficult reform and receives climate finance that is offset by a reduction in other concessional finance, it has been paid nothing … it destroys the credibility of the incentive for everyone watching" (**[S3.3]**).
- **Risk absorption is the constraint.** The three current sources (WB shareholder equity, project equity, guarantees) are each limited. "There is a great deal of money in the world that would fund clean energy at 4 percent. There is very little that will take first loss."
- The fund co-invests in construction and cross-guarantees. Regional funds sit with regional development banks. It supports local-currency and dollar debt. Investors are governments and small savers. "A rich country investing on behalf of a poorer one makes a burden-sharing transfer without a grant". Open to outside investors, it has "no real limit on its capitalization", unlike the WB's replenishment model.

### Ch 12. Green Policy as Growth Policy (ll. 852–878) **[S2.2]**

- Financial systems split into a no-risk part (banks, backed by the CB and the state) and a risky part. Monetary easing transmits via safe assets with factor monopoly or market power (land, incumbent equity, resource extractors). That is unstable and unequal; post-2008 is the illustration.
- **Green safe asset.** If green assets are safe, easing builds things. Possible **interest-rate separation** through CB eligibility or regulatory preference.
- For weak-currency countries, a green asset could serve as an **alternative numéraire** for long-term contracts such as PPAs, instead of dollarisation, and "could plausibly function as an international reserve asset". It resolves the dilemma that dollar debt is cheap but risky while local debt is safe but dear.

### Ch 13. The Sollar (ll. 880–952): brief

- One *Sollar* is an index claim on installed utility-scale panels across participating parks. The panel quantity per Sollar grows as profits are reinvested.
- After **5 years**, it is convertible each **1 January** into a bond over a fixed panel quantity paying dollar dividends.
- Illustration: one Sollar is enough panels for **1 W average output, which is 8.8 kWh/yr** (strictly 8.76), or **5–10 Wp**.
- The operator's fee is about **10%**, reduced for underperformance.
- Developers *borrow Sollars* from international institutions for construction at near-zero or negative Sollar interest and repay by issuing Sollars once built. "A developer pays their suppliers in the thing they are about to manufacture."
- Administered by the WBG (IBRD or IFC; analogy to IFC's Indian local green bond).
- Guarantees: the operator guarantees output; the purchaser guarantees price, easiest when paired with **feebates** (combinations of carbon taxes and feed-in tariffs). Diversification across about 30 jurisdictions removes single-government risk.
- Tension between domestic retention and foreign ownership.
- Conclusion: "Fix the numéraire and much of the rest of this book's machinery becomes unnecessary."
- Relevance to Series 2 is marginal; it at most belongs in a footnote to the currency section of 2.1.

**Case 1: what solar risk is.**
- About half of required new generation investment is solar.
- *Construction*: about **2 years** (REIPPPP requires commercial operation within 24 months of commercial close; Scaling Solar reached financial close in about one year). It is genuinely project-specific and deserves an equity-like return, but it is short and bounded. *Two-stage financing* confines it.
- *Operation*: about **23 years**, where the remaining risk is almost entirely the offtaker. "The physical asset is not the risk. The counterparty is."
- Evidence: SECI, Eskom with the Treasury guarantee, Zambia with an **IDA partial risk guarantee** (6.02c against 12c: "Half the tariff disappeared, and what disappeared was not hardware").

**Case 2: guarantees buy cost of capital with flexibility.**
- The government gives up the ability to change its mind. SA in **2017**: the energy minister announced renegotiation of BW4 solar contracts above a threshold after Eskom had delayed signing for nearly 3 years, and credibility took years to recover.
- The offtaker gives up dispatch flexibility (take-or-pay).
- The developer gives up upside.
- The guarantor commits balance sheet "in the tail".
- "Nobody is getting a lower cost of capital for nothing. They are getting it in exchange for commitment." This is the transaction framing; it replaces "risk engineered away".

**Case 3: currency can be hedged.**
- **TCX**, established 2007: hedges in more than **140** currencies. By 2024 it had enabled more than **$10bn** of local-currency funding on a capital base of about **$1.8bn**, and laid off about **$3.5bn** to private counterparties (OECD 2025 case study).
- Commercial frontier hedging costs **600–1,200 bp/yr**, which equals the gap between the 6% and 12% rows of the table.
- Hence: *priced* not hidden; *shared and reduced* (donor capital in TCX, **partial indexation** that hedges only the imported share, the declining tariff); *avoided* (half of SA's surveyed solar projects were local-currency financed; India's rupee bond market; **green savings accounts** in smaller and poorer countries).
- "Currency risk … is the largest single component of the emerging market premium after sovereign risk."

**The correlated shock (sizing).**
- The stack absorbs losses in order: developer equity, then the sovereign (first loss as a *ledger entry*: "arrears to the guarantor accumulate rather than crystallize"), then the equity buffer, then AAA.
- Example: **$100bn** guaranteed portfolio, **5%** buffer, **15%** of offtakers in arrears simultaneously, and the currency component of the affected PPAs losing a quarter of its value. Loss of about **$15bn**; the buffer covers **$5bn**; the guarantor bears **$10bn**, with recovery rights on sovereign ledgers. This is "roughly what MIGA or a reinsurer does".
- **"Used but not used up", made precise:** the rating is *lent* in normal times at the cost of a tail reserve, and *spent* only in the correlated case. "True in expectation and false in the tail."
- Mitigants: hedged FX moves to the hedge counterparty; the sovereign layer is a debt with a strong preferred-creditor repayment record; the asset keeps generating with no fuel bill.
- **Retraction (l. 1058):** the ch 10 claim is "retired". The structure adds a contingent liability "conditional on the government's own conduct … a liability one controls is a different thing from a liability one is exposed to". DSFs should distinguish the two (ch 25). Key for **[S2.1]** and **[S3.2]**.

**Rates have moved (worked example).**
- $1m project, 80% debt, **$100k/yr** distributable.
- Construction debt at 8%: refinancing at **4%** raises equity income from $36k to **$68k**; at **6%**, to **$52k** (+44%); at 8%, no gain.
- In a developing country with 12% construction debt: interest is **$96k**, equity gets **$4k**; refinancing at 8% gives **$36k**, at 6% **$52k**.
- "The gain … is the same four points of spread whether the world's safe rate is 2 percent or 5." Higher base rates *increase* the financing share (30% to 41% between the 4% and 6% rows), so the argument is "more urgent, not less true".

**One structure, several views (ll. 1070–1078).**
- Ch 10 is the *worked example*; ch 11 the *procurement framing*; ch 18 the *policy-lending* application; ch 19 the *institutional form* (a FIF with an explicit three-layer stack); ch 20 the *international incentive* (beneficiary accounts that grow with action and shrink with retreat, and the trust that separates control from benefit).
- The "five mechanisms" fold in:
  - **KPI bonds** and the **trusted intermediary model** are the *lending side*.
  - **The Trust** is the *equity side*.
  - **The clean energy financing model** ("selling power as a service per megawatt-hour" for the poorest countries) is the *investment function*.
  - **The risk absorption model** (country policy guarantees counter-guaranteed by the pool and a AAA entity) is the *guarantee stack*.
- "None is a sixth fund."
- **WB-INTERNAL:** footnotes 13 and 14 (ll. 2229–2236) quote what reads as a WB internal concept note ("we will consider whether WB…", "100% Clean Energy Transition Program Assistance", "Multi-Tiered Sustainable Infrastructure Project Portfolio Loan Guarantee System"). Fn 14 is substantively the CIT three-tier guarantee: host-government first line (or defaults added to the country's WB debt); second line a **country-specific buffer account in the "Climate Investment Trust"**; WB or high-income sovereigns as last resort.

**The seven-layer stack (l. 1080), the canonical 2.1 waterfall.**
1. **Contract**: an auctioned PPA or CfD, in a currency mix that matches revenues or is hedged, declining in real terms.
2. **Offtaker guarantee**: a SECI-like intermediary, or a sovereign guarantee routed through an MDB, that pays on time and recovers over time.
3. **Two-stage financing**: construction equity and short debt, refinanced into long-term guaranteed paper.
4. **Vehicle**: a ringfenced SPV that receives PPA payments directly and pays bondholders first.
5. **First loss**: the sovereign, by indemnity, as a ledger obligation.
6. **Second loss**: the equity buffer pool.
7. **Final guarantee**: a AAA sovereign, MDB or reinsurer, reserved against the correlated tail.

### Ch 15. Overcoming the Balance Sheet Limit (ll. 1096–1239) **[S2.2]**

- Three problems must be solved at once: standardisation and derisking (bankability), low-cost finance at scale, and incentivising the policies that create projects.
- Constraints on any solution: fair, not wealth-concentrating, rewards early action, no huge grants, no unrealistic action by developing countries, and arrangeable by existing institutions ("a new institution requires a treaty, and treaties take a decade").

**Three components.**
1. **A guarantee combining IBRD and MIGA**: the sovereign indemnity reduces risk, and reinsurance of the residual "removes what remains from the guarantor's books". "Existing practice uses one or the other. Using both is what makes the structure scalable."
2. **Derisked AAA green debt**: a new asset class eligible for pension funds and CBs. "The constraint … is … the quantity of money permitted to buy the instruments on offer."
3. **A blended equity-insurance fund** that recycles. All three are created "*in parallel*" to MDBs.

**Sector adaptation of the same schema.** Power: secure and securitise the PPA. Grids: user fees or land charges. End-use: property-tax (PACE) or PAYG.

**Numbers.**
- IEA about $3tn/yr, about a third in EM excluding China.
- **WBG $21bn in climate finance 2018–2022, about 40% of all MDB climate finance**, about 1% of the required annual scale (fn 15: DevCommittee 2022 paper; total green finance in MICs and LICs about $300bn/yr). **DOUBT:** WBG climate finance was about $30bn+ in *each* of FY21 and FY22, so "$21bn over 2018–2022" is likely a misread (perhaps an annual figure or a subset).

**Six barriers.** Growth (scale versus increment); exhortation without incentives; the fiscal capacity paradox; the energy price problem (the answer is feebates); bankability by design; fiscal risk from financing.

**Project categories.** Economic and financed; economic only with cheap capital (for example at a 7% WACC); never economic. **Defined: *funding* versus *financing*.** Funding provides the revenue; financing lends against it. Solar has migrated into the second category, so "the derisking agenda is now doing work that used to require grants". Precondition: *sufficient ambition*, and "finance itself must be a component of the incentive" (**[S3-ov]**).

**Finance availability.** "Every dollar it lends to a solar park is a dollar it does not lend to a hospital." What is needed is *additional* low-cost funds.

**Four elements:** overcome the IBRD scale constraint; derisk at scale; platforms to organise projects; a *scaling dynamic*.

**The sum (ll. 1187–1213), the sizing table for [S2.2].**
- Basis: IEA about **$1.35tn/yr** clean energy investment needed in EMDEs excluding China over 2026–30. About a third goes to power supply and about half of generation is solar.

### Ch 16. The Safe Intermediary Strategy: Toward a Theory of Concessionality (ll. 1241–1291) **[S2.1, S3.2]**

- **Principle.** (1) A safe AAA institution does the borrowing and passes the money through. (2) The long-term relationship between that institution and the borrower does the risk reduction.
- **Central trick:** "guarantee a *state agency* — for example, the agency that sets the long-term contracts — rather than short-term private sector agents." The state agency is a repeat player; the developer may never need the guarantor again.
- An equity fund makes the arrangement self-sustaining.
- **Theory of concessionality:** concessionality can be *manufactured* "out of credit ratings, relationships and diversification … The World Bank's AAA rating is a resource. Used correctly, it can be lent without being spent."
- **Objection:** "socializes risk and privatizes return". **Answer:** a *public* global equity fund in the same capital structure captures the enhanced return. "The equity fund is not an optional add-on … It is what makes the architecture defensible."
- Other sectors need their own securable revenue: user charges for grids, capacity payments for storage.
- **Supply-chain credits:** each MW of solar installed earns a defined credit toward financing battery, PV or mine supply chains. These must *not* be folded into carbon markets. **Cross-reference error:** "discussed at length in Part II", but that material is in RPWT.
- **Four finance-minister questions:**
  1. Design for financing: "the two look identical in an emissions model and differ by a factor approaching two in the cost of the electricity".
  2. Allocate and tranche, using an SPV.
  3. Guarantee the offtaker, refinance on completion, and put a first-loss buffer beneath.
  4. Sovereign risk comes from *mismatch*: "do not denominate a twenty-year liability in a currency you do not earn without hedging it, and do not fix a real-terms tariff for twenty years against a technology whose costs are falling."

### Ch 17. Why the World Bank (ll. 1293–1337) **[S3.2, S3.3]**

- Paris requires emissions under about 1 t per person per year plus removals.
- Temperatures are about 1.2°C now. The minimum feasible peak without large-scale negative emissions is "at least 1.5C".
- Not every country will decarbonise (Russia is named), so the architecture must "reward participants rather than penalize non-participants".

**Why the WB.**
1. It connects money to reform through DPF, which "is the instrument by which a country can be rewarded for running an auction programme or guaranteeing an offtaker".
2. It has been more forceful historically.
3. It sets the template others follow.
4. It combines knowledge, MIGA, IFC and country offices under one roof.

**Why it has not happened (added in PR round 3).**
- *Capital adequacy framework*: the 2022 G20 CAF Panel found the rules more conservative than the loss record, so MDBs could lend "several hundred billion dollars more". Each step means shareholders accept a marginally higher chance of a capital call ("stretch every dollar").
- *Shareholder politics*: "Any expansion of climate lending that is not additional … is opposed by whoever loses the envelope. The additionality requirement … is the condition under which the shareholders will agree."
- *Risk culture*: "A guarantee that is never called looks … like a guarantee that was never needed." Hence a **separate vehicle with its own capital, mandate and success metrics**.
- *Missing functions*: IFC equity is de-prioritised; MIGA writes less political-risk cover than the market could absorb; there is no global equity buffer anywhere in the WBG.

**Design principle:** off balance sheet, separately capitalised, additional to the existing envelope, and measured by its own results.

### Ch 18. Scaling Up Policy Lending (ll. 1339–1436): core for **[S3.3]**, part of **[S3.2]**

**Numbers.**
- IEA about **$1.35tn/yr** for EMDE excluding China, 2026–30. **DOUBT:** attributed in the chapter references to IEA (2021) / WEO 2022. The $1.35tn for 2026–30 looks like a later IEA figure (2023 WEI or the EMDE private-finance report). Verify before reuse.
- CPI 2021 (fn 16): **$632bn** total climate finance in 2020, of which only **$100bn** went to developing regions: **$19bn SSA, $16bn MENA, $35bn LAC, $30bn South Asia**. "Roughly six-sevenths … to roughly one-seventh of the world's people." CPI says climate finance must rise at least 590%.

**Proposal.**
- A WB-managed global equity fund, plus regional funds at regional MDBs. It is a risk-absorption system for **IBRD-rate policy and investment lending beyond the existing envelope**.
- Three functions: early-stage investment in standardised projects; absorbing risk from *additional* policy lending; profit-sharing incentives to early movers.
- Following **Heine et al. (2023)**, the incentive must come through an *additional* climate lending envelope beyond country limits. **WB-INTERNAL:** Heine et al. (2023) is cited as "EFI Staff Discussion Note … (unpublished)".

**Scalability.**
- *Equity* goes only into the early stage (construction plus 1–2 years of operations), then exits. The co-investment is a quality signal.
- *Loans* at IBRD-level rates, with a **multiple-layer guarantee scheme** built on donor guarantees, WB guarantees and local government guarantees.
- Selected limited partners join over time.

**Risk absorption.** The fund acts as a MIGA-style *guarantor* to policy lending, or provides risk-absorbing equity in a separate structure "similar to the SDR-funded investment accounts in the IMF's **Resilience and Sustainability Trust**". It is capital-efficient because preferred-creditor loan books show "extremely low non-accruals" (CAF Panel 2022). India is named as a priority country.

**Dynamic incentives (ll. 1379–1385), central to [S3.2] and [S3.3].**
- If the fund's return exceeds a target, early-acting countries receive **share allocations** that *dilute* the original impact investors down to their agreed maximum return. "That is how the excess return is transferred without anyone writing a cheque."
- Allocations go into **special national reward funds**, conditional on policy actions.
- Each sovereign must be **first-line risk absorber for portfolio losses in its own territory**.
- Dividends can buy physical investment or climate-risk insurance.

**Fund of funds and governance.** A global fund first, regional funds later. Distributed shares carry no voting rights, but **Bank-proposed changes require a super-majority of shareholders at the time of the change**, so terms cannot be rewritten after commitment.

**Application to infrastructure.**
- MIGA-style guarantees indemnified by the sovereign support loans to private developers. A co-investing equity fund sits alongside. SECI-model national or regional agencies are associated.
- A "specialised global or regional lending agency" makes IBRD-rate loans in countries that sign a **framework agreement**, whose key component is a **standardised format for multi-year CfDs or PPAs**.
- The agency lends *directly to developers*, keeping the debt off the sovereign balance sheet. The loans are backed by government policy guarantees intermediated through MIGA and the IBRD: "Governments contractually agree to indemnify the Bank for any portfolio losses caused by policy changes they themselves make … the government guarantees only what it controls."
- The agency must absorb FX risk. Persaud's **Global Climate Mitigation Fund** (Bridgetown 1.0, 2022) is a precedent.

**Low-capacity countries (ll. 1407–1413)** **[S2.1 SIDS]**
- Clean energy is provided "as a *service*" for LDCs with low fiscal capacity and thin capital markets, "particularly those that import fossil fuels".
- Through the specialised agency with an implementation consortium, the country pays a rate that **declines over time and is priced below fossil fuels**. The decline is a projection (**Wright's law**).
- The financial engineering is done by the IFC. "What the country commits to is an energy bill, not a debt." **CONFLICT:** this is in tension with the ch 14 contingent-liability concession. DSF and PPP accounting may score a long-term take-or-pay energy service contract as a liability. The 2.1 draft should state the conditional-liability argument, not "no debt".

**Currency.** **Green savings accounts**: guarantees that eliminate debt-servicing risk on a defined clean-energy portfolio let domestic savers deposit local currency, creating "the functional equivalent of a green bond market in a country that has no bond market".

**Why the fund appreciates (worked example, ll. 1421–1436).**
- **$1m** project: **$200k** equity and **$800k** debt; WACC **10%**; distributable revenue **$100k/yr**; 1-year build, 10-year life.
- WACC breakdown: 80% × 8% = 6.4% (**$64k** interest); 20% × 18% = 3.6% (**$36k** to equity).
- Refinance at **4%** after completion (AAA guarantees, optionally CB purchase): interest **$32k**, equity **$68k**. "The shares appreciate by a factor of two in a single year."
- The text flags the returns as not necessarily feasible. It assumes the auction price does not adjust, and competition will compress returns.
- "Conventional financing prices both the same because it cannot separate them."

**References in the chapter:** CPI (2021); Heine et al. (2023, unpublished); IEA (2021); IEA (2023) WEO 2022 (sic); CAF Panel (2022).

### Ch 19. The Climate Investment and Insurance Fund (ll. 1438–1530) **[S3.2 / S2.2]**

**Context numbers.**
- IEA about **$4tn/yr** globally (fn 17: NZE 2021) and **$1.35tn/yr** in middle-income and less-developed countries 2026–30 (fn 18: IEA 2021 Financing Clean Energy Transitions). **DOUBT:** same as ch 18.
- **G20 IEG "Triple Agenda" (June 2023)**: about **$3tn/yr** additional spending by 2030, of which **$1.8tn** is climate (a 4× increase over 2019) and **$1.2tn** other SDGs (+75% health and education). (There is also a 2023 G20 IEG report.)
- LSE 70 pp against 10 pp (fn 19).
- "Stretch every dollar" (fn 20, FT); the Evolution Roadmap.

**The Bank's three roles.** Enable low-cost finance; provide policy incentives; *mobilise domestic savings* ("create domestic financial assets where none existed").

**Situating the proposal (Paris Summit, June 2023).** WB Roadmap; Triple Agenda; CAF reform; **Bridgetown 1.0/2.0 Climate Mitigation Trust**. WP 1.8 maps the CIIF onto the Roadmap Table 4 items "Portfolio guarantees and guarantee platform" and "sources of concessionality for GPG projects", and onto the CAF lever "Expand use of financial innovation".

**Two cases.**
1. Primary investment and policy lending to states: scale up IBRD operations plus a climate VP or institution.
2. PPA-backed IPPs: a clean energy equity fund that also serves as a derisking buffer (fn 21: "initially one fund").

**Additionality again.** The book raises three routes to risk-absorbing capital: shareholders, originate-to-distribute, or "easier": guarantees with loans insured by a WB-managed FIF.

**The Fund.**
- A FIF called the CIIF, with the goal of mitigation including clean energy supply.
- Long-term prices derisk operation, so the main risk is *development*.
- **Governments guarantee the contracted price per MWh** (and hence revenues) for qualifying projects. The fund provides a second guarantee. **The WB provides a final guarantee "for the contracted price"**. "This both uses and protects the Bank's credit rating: the loans carry the Bank's rating, but the buffer equity fund covers the risk." Note this is phrased as a guarantee of the *contract price*, which supports 2.1, while "the loans carry the Bank's rating" muddies it.
- **As co-investor**: equity during construction, financed by issuing **AAA green bonds** to institutions and **green deposit certificates** to retail investors (**CONFLICT, internal:** the fund *raises equity by issuing debt*, which leverages the buffer itself), sold on completion, including to EMDE institutions.
- **As insurer** against non-performing loans to power projects, for a fee charged to developers.
- **Regional funds** as for-profit vehicles for small EMDE investors, linked to pension and social-protection schemes; "social security contributions invested directly in funds that support the contributors themselves".

**Prioritisation.** The first business line is replacing coal with dispatchable clean capacity (renewables plus storage). Systematise *Just Transition for All* and JETPs so that a JETP package exists for **every coal plant globally**, extending to gas. Global WB-led and regional MDB-led funds share common standards.

**Financial products.** An *equity buffer fund* that later guarantees other green products. **Cheap green bank deposits and green deposit certificates** give a local-currency green-bond equivalent. Two reasons for local currency are given (the text says three): no depreciation mismatch, and competition with wide bank spreads, so that "the intermediation margin is captured for the transition rather than by the banks".

**First loss.**
- Three buffers: (1) **participating developing-country government** first loss; (2) **equity fund**; (3) **AAA guarantees** from the WB or a strongly rated sovereign.
- "If there is a default, payments are covered by the World Bank, but the government indemnifies — so the loss is added to existing sovereign obligations owed to the Bank."
- In exchange, governments receive a share of net revenues, credited to **national reward accounts as fund shares**, with larger stakes for early movers.

**Generalisation.** Revenue guarantees for the supply chain (Gujarat battery and solar gigafactories) and critical minerals (contracts to sell minerals or buy panels at set prices). "Find the contract, secure it, securitize it." Retail access via smartphone apps compressing the loan-deposit spread.

### Ch 20. The Climate Incentives Trust (ll. 1532–1736): flagship source for **[S3.2]**, with **[S3-ov]** and **[S3.1]**

**Objective.** Expand "global, for-public-good, loss-absorbing capital: for mitigation finance before 2050, and for loss and damage protection after 2050". Three functions: insurance of risks in exchange for sovereign commitments; investment in early-stage blended project equity; incentives through profit-sharing. It creates "nationally-allocated beneficiary funds that protect against the long-term fiscal risks … in the second half of the century".

**Summary of the solution.**
- It insures to mobilise cheap debt (KPI bonds, trusted-intermediary finance), invests, and incentivises. Partners include Scaling Solar and the ETPs.
- "National-scale accounts that can act as collateral … a reward, and … security."
- Like an insurer, it invests. The investment function is "strictly non-core", but "a pot of donor money that grows is worth qualitatively more than one that is spent".
- Insurance contracts are MIGA-managed. Investments are diversified green blended equity in construction, with implementers including the IFC.
- **Insurance and incentive functions come first; investment later.**

**Trust structure.**
- Funded by donor countries and managed by the WBG.
- **Formal end beneficiaries: the post-2050 populations of participating recipient countries.** In the interim, they benefit through risk protection.
- Profit allocations to beneficiary accounts provide the incentive. The accounts are buffer funds for insuring **national policy guarantees and financing**, and support populations after climate disasters post-2050.
- Some proportion sits in a **global benefit account** not yet allocated.

**Scope limitations (italicised):**
- Each country needs a net-zero deal (plan plus auctions).
- In low-capacity countries, project finance is "provided as a service".
- "Offer a whole clean energy system deal to a small country or a municipality … so long as the price paid … is below … fossil fuels". This is the SIDS link for **[S2.1]**.
- No profit-sharing or KPI formula is given.
- FX and critical-minerals insurance are excluded.

**Why international incentives fail (ll. 1576–1600), core for [S3-ov].**
- **Additionality** creates a perverse incentive to delay. The remedy is *universal incentives*, defined now for all countries.
- Sovereignty precludes global taxation. At **$100/tCO2e**, an international carbon tax raises **nearly $5tn** and requires about **$1tn/yr from China** before any use of revenue. "No amount of elegant mechanism design changes that."
- **Global subsidies:** if $100/t cut global emissions 10% in year 1, the cost is about **$500bn/yr**, rising. They face the baseline problem.
- **Limited scope** (regional or sectoral), for example coal retirement only. This is where the Trust begins.
- **Trade policy**: CBAM covers only energy-intensive industry. Closed economies such as India can build their own supply chains; small countries would need trade blocs.
- **International feebate**: fee and rebate on carbon-intensity improvement netting to zero, "the feebate of the companion volume applied at the international level". The required transfers are smaller, but negative transfers to sovereigns are infeasible, "**Unless, that is, they are deductions from funds held in trust.** That is the pivot on which this whole proposal turns."
- **Beneficiary accounts** allocate on the basis of policy actions "and would *tax it back* if those actions were reversed". Current finance can also be allocated according to account size, which gives accounts immediate value.
- **Dynamic incentives**: falling auction prices reward early movers.
- Finance: the KPI bond (**Estevao 2023**) offers cheap finance for KPIs. **DOUBT:** Estevao 2023 is not in the book's References. Investors *and* the Bank or intermediary must be derisked.

**Risk-absorbing global equity.**
- Three uses: project equity ("halo effect"); protecting institutions like the WB from guarantee losses; national-scale collateral.
- **Capital and insurance are substitutes** (CDS versus capital; FX swap versus equity; WB loan versus MIGA cover), so loss-absorbing equity is a general-purpose instrument.
- WB capital increases are worthwhile but limited ("more than an order of magnitude" short).
- The approach is a WB or equivalent guarantee "while *protecting against all the risks the Bank incurs*". Intermediary examples: the WB (preferred creditor status) and SECI.
- **"Dual indemnification with a credit line"**: AAA protected first by the government (IBRD-style), second by the global equity fund, and third by the WB or another AAA entity.

**High returns.**
- The initial equity investors decide whether a project happens. Reinsurers (Swiss Re) hold ample buffers and invest them; returns are underwriting plus investment. Berkshire Hathaway is the model in which investment returns dominate. This does not cover systemic risks (global recession, pandemic).
- Three gaps: public-benefit capital; high-return equity; dynamic incentive structures.

**Proposal: "Insurance Buffer Funds for Policy Commitments".**
- Insurance of *contractual policy guarantees*, investment in early-stage RE, and profit-share incentives.
- Requires a **deal**: a net-zero *schedule* with procurement auctions and sectoral pricing (feebates, fuel taxation).
- Partners: Scaling Solar, the Transition Partnerships, the "Indian global solar initiative" (presumably the ISA), and the IFC.
- In exchange for hard commitments (for example annual guaranteed-price auctions plus grid investment), donors create buffer funds giving **second-line insurance**.
- Accounts are held in trust for the **mid-century population of each country that commits to mid-century net zero**, become fully liquid at mid-century, and serve as collateral meanwhile.
- First investors are **"donate-for-invest"** donor countries. Countries and small private investors can co-invest.
- **Implementation starts with insurance only. "While it is acting as collateral it would not invest in renewable projects."** This supports the draft's "capital held not spent".

**Financial summary (ll. 1660–1680).**
- Control and benefit are separate. **Voting shares are held by donors**; beneficiaries are post-2050 populations. Developing countries and their populations may donate for their own future populations.
- Regional child funds come later with regional and national DBs, and national funds act as "climate investment pension funds".
- The comparison is the **IMF RST** investment accounts (funded via SDRs there; directly here).
- **Private stakes** (pension funds, SWFs, Norway named) pay a **fee of about 25% of investment returns** to the trust and **vote only on the investment part**, not on the fee, insurance or incentive functions.
- **Illustrative structure:** **$5bn trust = $1bn donor + $4bn developing-country co-investment "financed by long-term development policy financing"**; **$95bn equity** from, for example, SWFs; leverages **$400bn debt**; total about **$500bn**. "Tentative and indicative … would need to be justified by formal modelling."
- With a 2-year construction recycle, about **$250bn/yr**.
- **Seven regional child funds** (China, South Asia, Europe and Central Asia, East Asia and Pacific, Africa, North America, South America), "of the same size and initially managed centrally", add **$1.75tn/yr**, for **$2tn/yr** in total, "or a balance sheet of $4tn".
- Eight funds give about **$4tn** leveraged after two years on an **$800bn equity** balance sheet, of which **$40bn** sits in beneficiary accounts. A 10% return on $800bn with **2.5%** paid to the trust (**$20bn/yr**) means the trust component "would double every two years".
- **Sectoral start:** global coal phase-out and rainforest protection. Target **−10%/yr** emissions with a **$100/tCO2** incentive on about **10 GtCO2e**, costing about **$100bn** in allocation and profit sharing.
- Delivered by profit-sharing against **defined baselines**, or "more radically" through **DPF with KPI-oriented *repayment amounts*** rather than KPI-oriented interest rates (**[S3.3]**).
- Constraints: "net national beneficiary returns must always be positive" (participation constraint), plus an incentive to outperform peers.
- Fallback: ***notional capitalized value***. Value the remaining Amazon at, for example, **$1tn**, and pay the *interest* into the beneficiary account. Two accounts result: the beneficiary account and the NCV account (**[S3.1]** link).

**Leverage.**
- *Financial*: $5bn, then $95bn, giving $100bn equity and $400bn debt, about $250bn/yr.
- *Effectiveness*: a **15%** return on $100bn equity and a **10% fee on profits** to national accounts gives about **$1.5bn/yr**, "doubling the total size of the beneficiary accounts roughly every three years". Regional extension: "Doubling the size of each fund every three years".

**Who speaks for 2050 (PR minor point).** Allocation and deduction rules are written in advance and hard to change (super-majority, as in ch 18). Transparent per-country balances and movements. The beneficiary country gets a standing seat on *rules and audit, not investments*. Independent trustees; endowment-style protections. "A trust that begins informal never becomes formal later."

**FAQs.**
- Returns come from refinancing with "synthetic AAA-rated bonds or bank loans" after grid connection.
- Poor countries: project finance as a service, with "an overall declining charge for its energy system, with no direct debt".
- Initial derisking applications: **surety on PPAs (SECI model)**, **derisking KPI-related sovereign bonds**, **cheap infrastructure lending through trusted intermediaries**, cheap mobile-app deposits, and **arbitrage between DFI-issued and sovereign bonds**.

**Underlying challenge.** "Standardizing and enhancing the accessibility, to developing country governments and to small investors, of sophisticated financing products." SLBs and green bonds focus on capital markets, but "**Many small island developing states cannot issue bonds at all.**" The three load-bearing ideas are two-stage finance (ch 18), a solar payment guarantee for asset-backed debt (ch 8), and an accessible early-stage equity fund (ch 13 in its most radical form).

**Conclusion.** "A payment to a government can be reversed by the next government. An account held in trust for a country's future population, which grows when the country acts and shrinks when it retreats, creates a constituency for persistence."

**CONFLICT / inconsistency checks against the CIT draft.**
- (a) **The draft's numbers match the book.** But the book's $4bn country co-investment is *financed by DPF*, so it is a borrowing from the WB. The draft should say so, because it affects the incentive story and debt sustainability.
- (b) **Fee inconsistency inside ch 20:** 25% of private returns (giving 2.5% of a 10% return, doubling every 2 years) against a 10% fee on a 15% return (giving $1.5bn/yr on $5bn, doubling every about 3 years). The draft should pick one.
- (c) **The ch 20 summary says "Trust investment of $1 billion per fund, mobilized private equity of $100 billion"**, which conflicts with $5bn trust capital and $95bn equity.
- (d) **The regional child-fund list includes North America, Europe and Central Asia, and China.** That sits oddly with "beneficiaries are post-2050 populations of participating *developing* countries". It also implies the $2tn/yr is global, not EMDE.
- (e) The "**$2tn/yr**" headline equals about **$250bn/yr per fund × 8**, built on the 2-year recycle and 4:1 debt. It is gross leveraged investment, not trust capital.
- (f) **Second-loss layer:** the book oscillates between "the global equity fund" (ll. 1620, 1734) and the *country's own beneficiary account* as buffer (ll. 1556, 1692; fn 14). The draft's "trust second loss" should specify which, or say pooled with country sub-accounts.
- (g) **Beneficiaries:** the book has three formulations: "post-2050 populations of participating recipient countries", "mid-century population of each country that commits to mid-century net zero", and a "global benefit account" not yet allocated. The draft's "post-2050 populations" matches the first.
- (h) "**Capital held not spent**" matches the insurance-first phase. But the mature CIT is an *investor* (blended early-stage equity), and private equity of $95bn invests in projects.
- (i) **Use of the NCV:** ch 20 uses notional capitalised value as a fallback when incentives exceed profit-sharing funds. The 3.1 and 3.2 papers should cross-refer consistently, since ch 21 is the NCCV proper.

## 6. Part V: The Hard Cases (chs 22–27, plus 28–29 briefly)

### Ch 22. The Offtaker's Own Economics (ll. 1800–1832) **[S2.1 precondition, S3.3 conditionality]**

- Utilities are insolvent mainly because retail tariffs are below cost.
- **India:** AT&C losses **15%** in 2022–23 (17% the year before); dues of **Rs 779bn** in early 2024.
- **Vietnam:** EVN, rated **BB**, bought at a **9.35c** FiT and sold below it.
- **Eskom:** below cost recovery for a decade, hence the Treasury guarantee.
- "A guarantee makes the generator whole. It does not make the utility solvent."
- **Not a reason to stop.** Cheap solar shrinks the *cost* half of the gap (buying at 3.6c instead of 6.5c). At the extreme, the cost of supply falls below the political tariff. IEEFA on Vietnam: auctions would have cut EVN's burden to "a fraction". The loop runs both ways: weak utility, higher cost of capital, higher cost, weaker utility.
- **Precondition:** the utility must be "on a credible path to cost recovery": a scheduled tariff adjustment and an explicit treasury-funded residual gap. India's mechanism is credible because the centre has twice funded the Discoms. "The guarantee and the fiscal transfer are the same policy seen from two sides."
- **DPF conditions** can include tariff reform, regulatory independence and a **payment security fund**, and the Part III guarantees become the *reward*. "A country that will not put its utility on a path to solvency should not receive a guarantee … the guarantee would be insuring a loss that is certain rather than a risk that is contingent." This is directly usable for **[S3.3]** codification and the 2.1 eligibility criteria.
- **Distribution:** below-cost tariffs mostly subsidise the better-off and the connected. Cheap solar turns tariff reform into a reform that *lowers* bills.

### Ch 23. The Other Half (ll. 1834–1870)

- **Wind:** the same logic. Less predictable output and larger civil works give a modestly higher premium. SA procured wind and solar side by side, with wind the larger share by value.
- **Hydro:** construction takes 5–10 years, so two-stage financing helps less. The structures apply to the operating phase and run-of-river plants.
- **Storage:** revenue comes from arbitrage, capacity and grid services, and can be contracted. SA's first solar-plus-storage financial close in **early 2026** used a **capacity-and-availability contract**. The IEA 2023 survey found EM battery cost of capital tracks solar's. "The other half needs new contract types as much as it needs money."
- **Transmission:**
  - Vietnam built more than **21,000 MW** of solar and wind in 2018–2023, then curtailed.
  - SA **BW6** awarded only **6 solar projects**; **27** others in the Cape could not be procured for lack of grid.
  - Lines have no PPA. They earn a regulated charge on the insolvent utility's balance sheet.
  - Three responses: (1) **contract the wire**: independent transmission concessions with availability charges (Brazil, Chile, Peru, India); (2) **bundle** the connection into the generation auction (works for the last few km, not backbone lines); (3) treat backbone lines as public infrastructure financed by **DPF conditioned on tariff reform**, with Part IV concessionality directed there. "A transmission line that lets 2,000 megawatts of already-contracted solar dispatch is … one of the best investments available."
- **Summary:** the logic extends completely to wind, largely to storage, partially to hydro, and to transmission only where contracted or bundled. "The place where a development bank's scarce concessional capital does the most is very often not the solar park at all, but the line that connects it." This matters for **[S2.2]** scope and for directing **[S3]** concessionality.

### Ch 24. Avoiding the Non-Renewable Energy Debt Trap (ll. 1872–1898) **[S2.1 currency, S3 DSF]**

This chapter grew from reviewer exchanges with "Florent" and "Charl" (WP ll. 2143–2189).

- Imported fossil fuel is an unbooked open position and economically a liability: "riskier … than a fixed-rate loan of equivalent present value."
- Green debt differs because it is covered by electricity payments. In India, solar's *total* cost often beats coal's *variable* cost.
- "A country that borrows to build solar is converting a variable, open-ended, foreign-currency fuel liability into a fixed, finite, amortizing debt." DSFs miss this, which "makes the safer choice look more dangerous".
- **The real liability is in the PPA.** PPAs are "the envelope of long-term commitments". **Cross-reference error:** the text says "the problem set out in Chapter 17"; the material is in ch 8.
- The currency dilemma moves from borrowing to the PPA. The answer is "a basket rather than a single currency", **certainty**, and **decline in real terms** "so that the tariff paid under old contracts stays close to that of new ones".
- In the WP exchange, Charl raises SOE ownership and contingent liabilities, and Stephen's reply favours arm's-length IPP financing with the SOE as offtaker.

### Ch 25. Debt Sustainability and Green Investment (ll. 1900–1978) **[S3.3, S3-ov]**

- Pre-COVID global debt of **$253tn, 322% of GDP** (Picolotti and Miller 2020). This is actually IIF data relayed in a debt-for-climate-swaps piece.
- Extreme weather to cost **$6tn over this century** absent action (Maldonado and Gallagher 2022). **DOUBT:** attribution and magnitude.
- Seven of the ten most climate-vulnerable countries are in Africa. African "debt levels currently total **$400bn**" (Shipalana 2020). **DOUBT:** the WP source note says "interest repayments on its debt amount to $400 billion", which is itself implausible; the book silently changed it.

- UNFCCC 2014: global climate finance in 2012 of **$340–650bn**, of which developing countries spent "only **$22.5bn** on climate mitigation programs (NGFS 2021)". **DOUBT:** the WP note shows the source is GGGI (2016) *Mind the Gap*, and the $22.5bn relates to adaptation and public finance for developing countries "in 2014". ### Ch 26. Phasing Out Coal: New Investment (ll. 1980–2002)

- **1,175 GW** of new coal cancelled since 2015, "greater than China's entire current coal capacity". 2020 was "plausibly the first year" with no MDB coal project finance. More than 100 globally significant financial institutions have divested. **DOUBT:** the figures are uncited (likely Global Energy Monitor or E3G).

### Ch 27. Retiring Existing Coal Assets (ll. 2004–2058) **[S2.2, S3.2 sectoral start]**

- The harder half: write-offs and take-or-pay lock-in.
- **Buy-and-retire:** purchase and close, compensating from cheaper replacement power. The **ADB** has a Southeast Asia portfolio "with an initial budget of **USD 4 million** for the design phase", which shows how early the work is (uncited; the ETM).
- **Self-reference error:** the text says "Chapter 27 set out the financial mechanics" inside ch 27, and the sub-part "Even When a PPA Is in Place … This short chapter" is the merged former chapter (PR round 2).
- Gaps: carbon floors stop restarts; coordination; explicit assumptions; recipient-country risks from new coal assets; fossil subsidies; documenting successes.
- **Securitisation as solvent:** where the *total* cost of solar beats the *variable* cost of coal (fn 22: Shrimali 2020, *Energy Policy* 139), replace the coal plant's PPA with a renewables-plus-storage contract of equal value. "The plant owner can be paid to exit, the offtaker can pay less for power, and the financier can earn a return, all out of the same saving." "Whether a coal phase-out is a distributional fight or a mutually profitable transaction depends on the discount rate."

### Ch 28. Vietnam (ll. 2060–2106) and ch 29. Two Worlds (ll. 2108–2148), briefly

- **Vietnam timeline:**
  - 2017 FiT of **9.35 US c/kWh** for 20 years, for commercial operation by mid-2019.
  - 2019: **91** farms, **4,550 MW**. 2020: about **9 GW**, the world's #3 market that year. By 2023: more than **21 GW** of solar and wind.
  - About **58%** domestic investment and **27%** domestic-foreign JV.
  - The EVN PPA had no international arbitration, inadequate termination compensation and no curtailment limit: "investable but … not bankable" (Vietnam Business Forum).
  - Curtailment in 2020.
  - **Retroactive revision in 2024–25 for 173 projects, cutting revenue by 25–46%** (IEEFA). 2030 targets: **73 GW** solar and **38 GW** wind.
  - **JETP of $15.5bn** (December 2022) stalled because the government would not guarantee investor–EVN agreements.
  - Counterfactual: India's auctions took solar from **9.72c in 2014 to 3.04c in 2024**; add a bankable PPA, a SECI-like offtaker guarantee, and bundled or policy-financed transmission.
  - "The money existed. The projects existed … The missing element was the guarantee on the offtaker." This is the opening case study for **[S2.1]**.
  - IEEFA and the Vietnam Business Forum are not in the References.
- **Two Worlds:** the remedies come in four layers: *contract*, *guarantee* (at the offtaker), *structure* (two-stage, SPV, sovereign first loss, buffer second, AAA tail) and *institution*. Costs are stated plainly; the dissolvable premium is "a third and a half … less in the poorest".

## 7. RPWT (*The Right Policy at the Wrong Time*): the international-incentive and forest material

**Framing (RPWT Preface, ll. 45–61).**
- Carney abolished Canada's consumer carbon tax on **14 March 2025** but kept the industrial output-based system.
- Claim 1: separate the *marginal signal* from the *net burden* through **feebates**.
- Claim 2: the carbon price should be **discovered by competition** at the cost of inorganic removal. "One ton emitted would be offset not by one ton removed but by however much mitigation the full price can buy, which is usually several tons of forest protection."
- The sequence: subsidy, then feebate, then tax.
- **Citation inconsistency:** the companion is called "*Time and Chance: The Elements of Scale in the Green Transition*", but "The Elements of Scale" was the *old* T&C ch 15 title, renamed after PR. The subtitle needs checking.
- The meeting point between the two books: quantity instruments create the revenue streams that cheap capital needs.

### RPWT ch 5. Pricing Across Borders (ll. 277–311) **[S3-ov]**

- **International problem:** at $100/t, nearly $5tn and about $1tn/yr from China (the same as T&C ch 20). Global subsidies need non-existent money and a baseline, which invites inflation and delay ("the additionality problem … at the level of states").
- **Feebate between countries:** fee and rebate on *national carbon intensity*, netting to zero. Faster improvers receive; slower ones pay. Transfers are "a fraction of what a tax would move, because most countries sit near the benchmark". One form is a **double-quota structure** (from agricultural production control; RPWT ch 3 applies it to EU gas burden-sharing). It needs only agreement on quotas, not the tax level.
- **The negative-transfer problem is solved by the trust:** "if the rebates are held in accounts that grow with performance and shrink with its reverse, the negative transfer becomes a deduction from money a country has not yet received … **The international feebate, in other words, needs a trust to hold the pot.**" This is the explicit hinge between carbon pricing (Series 1) and the CIT (3.2) for the 3-overview.
- **CBAM as a feebate:** transitional since 2023, charging from 2026. It charges imports on embedded emissions above the EU benchmark, so clean foreign producers pay nothing. It phases in as free allocation phases out. Coverage: iron and steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, hydrogen. Limits: sectoral coverage, measurement, and a perception of protectionism.
- **Clubs:** a common minimum price with a border adjustment for non-members. Club logic and feebate logic are the same. "Set the benchmark at a level that rewards what clean producers are already doing, and the club is a coalition of the willing. Set it at a level that punishes, and it is a trade war." The feebate "can be adopted one jurisdiction at a time and still add up."

### RPWT ch 6. The Price Set by a Technology Competition (ll. 313–377): **the forest-value argument for [S3.1]**

This is the passage T&C ch 21 refers to as "developed at length". Its key steps:

1. **Record.**
   - Guardian / Die Zeit / SourceMaterial (January 2023): more than **90%** of Verra rainforest offsets represented no real reduction (inflated baselines). "Contested in their details and confirmed in their thrust by later academic work" (no academic citation given).
   - Voluntary market about **$2bn** in 2021, under **$1bn** by 2023. Nature credits at **$5–10/t**, some categories under **$2**.
   - DAC at **$600–1,000/t** at the first plants. US tax credit **$180/t**.
   - **Frontier** (Stripe, Alphabet, Shopify) has committed more than $1bn.
   - "Two prices": a few dollars to *claim*, a few hundred to *remove*.
2. **Proposal.** The social cost of carbon is set by the **competitively bid cost of inorganic removal**. An offset is certified only if the buyer pays that price. **The money need not go to DAC.** A second market allocates it to the highest mitigation per dollar, so "one ton of emissions, offset at the air-capture price, might purchase **five tons of avoided deforestation**". Rich-country net-zero commitments thereby pay for conservation of "carbon stocks that are genuinely irreplaceable" while funding removal technology.
3. **Three institutions:**
   - Global auctions for inorganic removal. Each contract is about **20 Mt** lifetime (**1 Mt/yr for 20 years**), and the winning bid sets the price, "the instrument that drove solar costs down … applied to a different technology".
   - A scientist-designed global assessment of cost-effectiveness for organic protection and removal.
   - A standards body certifying "gold standard" only at the auction price. ICVCM Core Carbon Principles (2023) are "a step … what they lack is the price".
4. **Price level:** "somewhere around **$200/t**", below current DAC cost, roughly where a mature auction might settle, and **40×** a rainforest credit.
5. **Theory.** Pay the marginal cost *at the social optimum*, not at today's under-abated equilibrium ("famine-era rationing prices"). The cost of removal is a reasonable *upper bound* on the optimal SCC and, given catastrophic risk (trillions in coastal property), plausibly also the *lower bound*. "When the plausible upper and lower bounds converge … the number is usable."
6. **Practice.** **Financial net zero**: developed countries and consumers offset *all* sectoral emissions well before territorial net zero, with the money directed to protecting existing stocks, creating more, and funding removal markets. Paying for multiple tons hedges impermanence (fire, logging after the contract).
7. **Non-uniform valuation of forest carbon (l. 375):** "If we know that deforesting the next **2 percent of the Amazon would trigger the loss of 75 percent of the remainder** — through the forest-feeds-rain-feeds-forest dynamic — then that particular carbon is immensely more valuable than carbon at the margin elsewhere." Preservation mechanisms can be competitive, paid for "in different parts of the forest". "Not all tons are equal, and the Verra scandal was in large part the consequence of pretending they were."
8. "This is an argument *for* carbon markets." Aviation is a legitimate offsetting use.

**For [S3.1]:**
- The argument values forest carbon *stocks* at the removal-cost SCC and gives tipping-point carbon a premium. The NCCV can cite this as the valuation basis.
- **DOUBT:** the "2% leads to 75%" Amazon claim is uncited and should be sourced (for example Lovejoy and Nobre 2018 on tipping thresholds of 20–25% deforestation) or softened.
- RPWT does not itself propose using the value as *guarantee capacity*. That step exists only in T&C ch 21 (and the ch 20 fallback of paying interest on a notional $1tn Amazon value).
- Related: WP "Research ideas" (WP l. 2213) lists "**Stock externalities** — incentives to align with … forest cover. Potentially stock rather than flows could also apply to emissions too" (marked "concrete operational link" with forestry colleagues).
- T&C ch 25 compares the Eliasch $17–33bn/yr with the ch 21 valuations.

### RPWT ch 7. Carbon Markets Under Net Zero (ll. 379–443) **[S3-ov, S3.1]**

- **Sellers disappear.** About **140 countries** (about 90% of emissions and output) have net-zero pledges. *Global* net zero requires sinks, not offsets. Under stringent developing-country targets, overachievement (supply) vanishes. **Article 6** rules were settled at **Baku 2024**, and "corresponding adjustments" make a sold ton costly to the seller: "correct accounting, and it is fatal to the old model".
- **What carbon markets were also for:** *burden-sharing and transfer* to give developing countries a reason to participate. Losing the transfer channel is the real loss, so side payments are needed.
- **Five requirements:** (a) sink logic; (b) side payments for stringency; (c) an additional funding source; (d) market-based selection; (e) fair burden sharing. Ch 6 meets (a) and (d); "the rest of this book and its companion" address (b), (c) and (e).
- **Funding, three sources:**
  1. Gold-standard corporate net-zero commitments in the 2030s (fragile, as the Verra episode showed).
  2. **Sovereign co-guaranteed long-term borrowing (100-year maturities) repaid in proportion to historical emissions** (the T&C Preface mechanism).
  3. **The concessionality component of scaled-up MDB lending**, which "could plausibly fund side payments, though probably not drawdown".
- **Political economy, three components:**
  - *Between nations*: prisoner's dilemma; reward early movers and make defection visible.
  - *Within nations*: concentrated fossil interests, so instruments needing no net revenue travel better.
  - *Across time*: "sacrifice later is considerably easier to agree to than sacrifice now", so front-load the incentive and back-load the payment as a design parameter.
- **Legal character of an emission (ll. 429–433), key for [S3.1]:** emitting "inflicts damage on identifiable agents across the world, in the future. Where there is potential legal liability for future damage, the appropriate response is to set aside money now … it is a ***capital* requirement, not an expense**. This is the concept underlying both corporate emissions liability and national cumulative responsibility. An emitter who has not provisioned against the damage … is running an unreserved liability." This is the mirror image of NCCV: an emission as an unreserved liability, a carbon stock as notional capital.
- **Three allocations:**
  1. *Efficient use of money*: market design; reverse auctions for removal; **intertemporal not static optimality** ("static cost-effectiveness would never have funded solar in 2005, and it will not fund direct air capture in 2026").
  2. *Burden-sharing*: low-cost finance at scale for MICs. The form of subsidy is unclear and funds are insufficient, so "both carrots and sticks".
  3. *Intergenerational*: Pareto improvement through borrowing from the future (the same wording as the T&C Preface).

### RPWT ch 11. The Sequence (ll. 539–583) **[S3-ov]**

- **The missing middle:** no policy exists for mass deployment between subsidy and tax.
- **Three phases:**
  - Early: subsidy is close to optimal by collective-action logic; the IRA is phase-one for hydrogen and capture.
  - Intermediate: subsidies become too expensive because they succeed (the German surcharge). Revenue-neutral tax with sectoral rebating (a feebate) and **reverse auctions** belong here.
  - Late: carbon tax with general recycling. The EU ETS rose from under €10 for most of a decade to **€80–100** after 2021.
- **Solar arc:** 2000–10 FiTs in Germany, Spain and Italy, module prices more than halved. 2010–20 auctions (SA 2011, India, Chile, Brazil, Mexico), Spain's retroactive cut, module prices down another **80%**. 2020 onward, solar the cheapest source.
- **Failure mode:** starting at the end (transport fuel taxes in Australia, France, Washington, Canada).
- **Six propositions.** Number 3: "Secure property rights and derisked investment are prerequisites … the subject of the companion volume."

### RPWT ch 12. Dynamic Incentives (ll. 585–615) **[S3.2, S3.3]**

- Static incentive: waiting is free. Dynamic incentive: "pays more for acting sooner". The additionality problem is a static-incentive problem, and dynamic incentives are its solution. Auctions became dynamic incentives as a by-product.
- The companion's policy-lending fund (early movers get larger equity shares) and the CIT (accounts grow with action and shrink with reversal) "convert the question 'will you act?' into … 'will you act *now*?'"
- **Time consistency:** front-loaded rewards are more credible, and a smaller late reward is "doing what the schedule already said". Spain's and Vietnam's retroactive cuts illustrate the problem.
- **Voting scheme (sketch only):** participants vote on allocation weighted by **contribution** (money or verified action), so early movers set the rules, like a mutual or cooperative. This complements the trust. Open questions: measuring contribution, entry of new members, avoiding a club of the rich. A possible governance option for **[S3.2]** that differs from the ch 20 donor-vote model.
