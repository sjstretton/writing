## 1. Abstract

**Current abstract.** It states the problem (prices too weak), the diagnosis (three constraints), the proposal (feebate plus destination-based charge) and the VAT analogy. That is the right content.

**Problems.**
- It gives no finding or result, only "we present" and "appendices provide".
- It says "we" for a single author.
- It names a "primary-to-quaternary structure", but the body says "primary–tertiary".
- It uses acronyms (CCP, OECP, CBAM) that an abstract should avoid.
- It does not say what is new relative to border carbon adjustments and the EU CBAM.

**Proposed abstract** (about 170 words; it describes the paper after the upgrade, so some sentences depend on sections still to be written):

> Carbon prices remain too low in most countries to drive rapid decarbonization, mainly because a tax on domestic production raises costs for exporters and for firms competing with imports. This paper proposes Open Economy Carbon Pricing, which splits carbon pricing into two instruments. A revenue-neutral feebate gives producers a strong incentive to cut emissions intensity without raising their average costs. A destination-based charge on the carbon embodied in goods consumed at home applies equally to imports and domestic products and exempts exports, as a value-added tax does. Under stated conditions, the combination removes the competitiveness case against high carbon prices and the incentive for leakage, and a simple model shows that the politically feasible incentive rate rises above that of a conventional carbon tax. The paper sets out an implementation route through existing customs and value-added tax systems, compares it with the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, and assesses compatibility with World Trade Organization rules.

## 2. Structure

**Current order:** Abstract → Introduction → Literature review (six strands) → Two-plus-one functions → Structural constraints → OECP framework → Primary–tertiary argument → Implementation → "Link to appendices" → Appendices A (trade model), B (political economy), C (implementation), D (WTO, three sentences).

**Problems.**
- The framework is described twice (in "Introducing OECP" and again in "Implementation"), and the feebate formula appears three times.
- References to "Part I/II/III" point to parts the paper does not have. "Forward links" and "Link to appendices" are boilerplate.
- The literature review comes before the reader knows the proposal. It is organized by strand rather than by the gap the paper fills.
- The "two-plus-one functions" section is a separate idea. It interrupts the argument and is better as its own concept paper (concept 2 in `PAPERS.md`).
- The constraints section asserts and does not evidence. "Feasible prices remain in the €30–€60/tCO₂ range" has no source.
- There is no worked example with numbers and no comparison with what the EU already does.

**Proposed structure** (World Bank working paper form; about 7,000 words):

1. **Introduction.** The problem with numbers: the share of emissions priced and the average price, from the World Bank's *State and Trends of Carbon Pricing*. Then the question, the answer, and the contribution against border carbon adjustments, EU CBAM with free allocation, and consumption-charge proposals.
2. **Why carbon prices stay low in open economies.** The trade, political-economy and transition constraints, each with evidence.
3. **Open Economy Carbon Pricing.** The two instruments and border treatment, with Propositions 1–3 (no leakage, export neutrality, a higher feasible rate) and their conditions.
4. **A worked example.** Steel, with stylized but sourced numbers: costs, intensities, the feebate, the charge and revenue.
5. **Implementation.** Customs defaults, the VAT chain, customs unions, and WTO compatibility, expanding Appendix D into the main text.
6. **Objections and limits.** See section 4 below.
7. **Policy implications.** For the EU as free allocation phases out under CBAM, and for developing-country exporters and customs unions.
8. **Conclusion.**

The appendices keep A (the model, with derivations behind the propositions), B (political economy) and C (implementation detail). The 8,900-word consumption-tax background paper stays outside the paper; cite it as a working paper or cut it to a short appendix.

## 4. What's missing in the argument

1. **Revenue is spent three times.** The consumption-charge revenue is recycled to households (Appendix B), reserved for remediation and compensation (the two-plus-one section) and used to fill the feebate gap ("Transfers and Feebate Gaps"). Pick one primary use and show the arithmetic.
2. **Results are asserted, not derived.** "t_OECP > t_CCP" follows only if the voter constraint binds less once rebates are paid. State the conditions: revenue neutrality of the feebate needs a fixed output mix, and "no leakage" needs the charge to equal true embodied emissions.
3. **Embodied emissions for domestic goods.** Pricing the carbon embodied in all domestic consumption, not just imports, is the hardest administrative step. The paper says "mirrors VAT" but does not show how intensities pass along the VAT chain. This is the main feasibility question a referee will ask.
4. **No comparison with the EU.** The EU runs output-based free allocation plus CBAM, which is close to this proposal on the production side. Say what OECP adds: a domestic consumption charge, exempt exports, simpler import defaults.
5. **Exporters still face foreign carbon charges.** Export competitiveness depends on the importing country, so the race-to-the-top claim needs the incentive spelled out, ideally with Nordhaus's clubs.
6. **Developing countries.** The World Bank audience will ask what this means for exporters facing CBAM-style charges, and for countries without strong customs or VAT administration.
7. **WTO.** Appendix D is three sentences. The feebate's subsidy element needs a sentence on the SCM Agreement (specificity) and the export side.
8. **No numbers.** There is no figure or table. Add one worked example (steel) and one figure comparing conventional pricing with OECP.
9. **Text defects.** "Part I/II/III" references, the unsourced €30–€60 range, the unsourced "10% / 2–3%" example, and "we" for a single author.
