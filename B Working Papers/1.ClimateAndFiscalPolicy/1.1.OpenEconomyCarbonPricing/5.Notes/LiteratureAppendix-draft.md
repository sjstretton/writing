## 1. Carbon leakage and competitiveness under unilateral pricing

### Model-based estimates and meta-analysis

**BrangerQuirion2014.** Branger and Quirion, "Would Border Carbon Adjustments Prevent Carbon Leakage and Heavy Industry Competitiveness Losses?", *Ecological Economics* 99 (2014). [text: HAL preprint] This is a meta-analysis of 25 modelling studies from 2004–2012, with 310 estimates of the leakage ratio, plus a meta-regression on model assumptions. Leakage without border adjustment ranges from 5 to 25 percent (mean 14 percent). With border carbon adjustments (BCAs) it ranges from −5 to 15 percent (mean 6 percent). Holding other factors constant, a BCA lowers the leakage ratio by 6 percentage points. Leakage falls as the coalition grows. Among BCA design features, covering all sectors and rebating exports reduce leakage most. Output losses in energy-intensive, trade-exposed (EITE) sectors are 0–4 percent (mean 2 percent) in general-equilibrium models and more than double that in partial-equilibrium models. In CGE models some leakage survives a BCA, through the international fossil-fuel price channel.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the source for the "5–25 percent" range in Related literature. Note that the range is B&Q's and not EMF 29's. The finding that export rebates are among the most effective BCA features supports zero-rating exports on the consumption track. The surviving fossil-fuel price channel is leakage that neither track touches, so Proposition 1's "no leakage" should read "no leakage through the goods channel".

**BoehringerBalistreriRutherford2012.** Böhringer, Balistreri and Rutherford, "The Role of Border Carbon Adjustment in Unilateral Climate Policy: Overview of an Energy Modeling Forum Study (EMF 29)", *Energy Economics* 34(S2) (2012). [metadata] The paper synthesizes 12 CGE models that compare unilateral pricing with and without BCAs. VERIFY the numbers before citing. From prior knowledge, the leakage rates are roughly 5–19 percent (mean about 12 percent), BCAs cut leakage by about a third, and BCAs shift the burden of abatement to non-coalition countries.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the second citation for the leakage range. Check its own numbers rather than citing it jointly for "5–25".

**Mattoo2009.** Mattoo, Subramanian, van der Mensbrugghe and He, "Reconciling Climate Change and Trade Policy", World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 5123 (2009). [text: World Bank OKR] This is a CGE analysis of unilateral emission cuts by high-income countries and of alternative border taxes. Leakage is small, with at most a 2.5 percent increase in emissions elsewhere, but output and exports of energy-intensive manufactures fall, which creates pressure for trade action. A border tax based on the carbon content of imports, applied to all merchandise, would cut China's manufacturing exports by one-fifth and those of low- and middle-income countries by 8 percent. Real incomes would fall by 3.7 and 2.4 percent respectively. A border tax based on the carbon content of *domestic* production, applied to imports and exports, addresses competitiveness concerns with far less damage to developing countries.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the development-cost case against charging imports on their own (high) intensity. It is relevant to Proposition 7 and to the "generous defaults" design. It cuts against the paper: a destination charge on actual intensity is exactly the design Mattoo et al. find most damaging to developing-country exporters, unless exporters respond as Proposition 7 assumes. State this trade-off in Implementation.

**WeisbachKortumWangYao2023.** Weisbach, Kortum, Wang and Yao, "Trade, Leakage, and the Design of a Carbon Tax", *Environmental and Energy Policy and the Economy* 4 (2023), 43–90 (NBER WP 30244). [text: NBER WP] The paper solves analytically and calibrates a model in which one region taxes and the rest of the world is passive. It finds four things. (1) The optimal policy taxes both the supply of fossil fuels (extraction) and their demand. (2) On the demand side it taxes both production and consumption, with a *lower* rate on production because of leakage. (3) Taxing production only, combined with extraction, is "almost as effective" as also taxing consumption, and avoids border adjustments on goods. (4) That last result depends on a low foreign elasticity of energy supply. The authors stress that Markusen (1975) already contained the core idea.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the strongest modern statement of the Markusen logic behind Proposition 1. It partly supports the paper (optimal policy prices both bases) and partly challenges it. Weisbach et al. find the consumption leg adds little when foreign energy supply is inelastic, and they put the missing instrument on extraction, not on intensity. The two-track paper should say why it does not use an extraction tax. The likely answer is that most adopting countries are not fossil-fuel suppliers.

**CopelandTaylor2004.** Copeland and Taylor, "Trade, Growth, and the Environment", *Journal of Economic Literature* 42(1) (2004) (NBER WP 9823). [text: NBER WP] This survey of theory and evidence on trade and the environment introduces the scale/composition/technique decomposition and the distinction between the pollution haven *effect* (regulation shifts trade at the margin) and the pollution haven *hypothesis* (liberalization moves dirty industry to weakly regulated countries). Its conclusion is that the evidence supports a pollution haven effect but that factor endowments often dominate.
*Use in 1.1:* this is background for the intensity versus quantity decomposition in the Introduction. The technique effect corresponds to the intensity margin and the composition effect to the quantity margin. It replaces the old CopelandTaylor2005 conflation flagged in the Review.

### Ex-post evidence

**DechezlepretreSato2017.** Dechezleprêtre and Sato, "The Impacts of Environmental Regulations on Competitiveness", *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy* 11(2) (2017). [text: LSE repository] This is a review of ex-post studies of trade, location, employment, productivity and innovation. Leading on environmental policy produces small but statistically significant short-run adverse effects, concentrated in a small group of energy-intensive sectors with limited pass-through. These effects are small compared with transport costs, proximity to demand, labour quality and agglomeration.
*Use in 1.1:* this supports the claim that "ex-post evidence of output losses is smaller and concentrated in a few sectors". It also means the political constraint in the Introduction is mainly about *expected* and sector-concentrated losses, not observed aggregate ones.

**NaegeleZaklan2019.** Naegele and Zaklan, "Does the EU ETS Cause Carbon Leakage in European Manufacturing?", *JEEM* 93 (2019). [text: EconStor postprint] The paper estimates a gravity model of bilateral trade in value and embodied carbon (GTAP, 2004/2007/2011), with country-pair, country-year and sector-year fixed effects. It finds no evidence that EU ETS costs changed trade flows or embodied-carbon flows in manufacturing.
*Use in 1.1:* this confirms there is no detectable leakage at ETS prices up to about 2011, which were low and came with free allocation. Use it to say the leakage problem is prospective at the rates (\$80–100) the paper targets.

**Dechezlepretre2022.** Dechezleprêtre, Gennaioli, Martin, Muûls and Stoerk, "Searching for Carbon Leaks in Multinational Companies", *JEEM* 112 (2022), 102601 (CEP DP 1601). [text: CEP DP] The paper uses Carbon Disclosure Project data on regional emissions of 1,122 multinationals (261 under the EU ETS) for 2007–2014. It finds no displacement of emissions from Europe to the rest of the world, even within energy-intensive firms. The authors attribute this either to generous free allocation or to low ETS prices (€0–30).
*Use in 1.1:* as above. The authors' own explanation, that free allocation may be what prevents leakage, supports Track 1's role. Free allocation is the incumbent output-based rebate.

**Colmer2024.** Colmer, Martin, Muûls and Wagner, "Does Pricing Carbon Mitigate Climate Change? Firm-Level Evidence from the European Union Emissions Trading System", *Review of Economic Studies* 92 (2025). [abstract] Using administrative data, the paper finds that the EU ETS cut regulated French manufacturing firms' emissions by 14–16 percent with no detectable fall in economic activity and no outsourcing. The cuts came from targeted investments that lowered emissions intensity.
*Use in 1.1:* this is direct evidence that a carbon price under free allocation works on the intensity margin rather than the quantity margin. That is exactly the margin the feebate keeps (Proposition 2). It is useful in the Cost section.

**Martin2014.** Martin, Muûls, de Preux and Wagner, "Industry Compensation under Relocation Risk: A Firm-Level Analysis of the EU Emissions Trading Scheme", *AER* 104(8) (2014). [text: LSE repository] The paper derives an efficient compensation rule: equalize damage-weighted marginal relocation probabilities across firms. It applies the rule using interview-based relocation-risk scores for European manufacturers. The EU's free-allocation criteria substantially overcompensate for a given leakage risk, and an efficient allocation would cut aggregate job-loss risk by more than half at no higher total compensation.
*Use in 1.1:* this supports the Objections point that a feebate targets transfers at performance rather than incumbency. It also warns that any benchmark-based rebate can overcompensate if benchmarks are set leniently, which bears on Proposition 5.

**GanapatiShapiroWalker2020.** Ganapati, Shapiro and Walker, "Energy Cost Pass-Through in US Manufacturing: Estimates and Implications for Carbon Taxes", *AEJ: Applied* 12(2) (2020) (NBER WP 22281). [text: NBER WP] For several US manufacturing industries (cement, concrete, ready-mix, boxes, bread and others), 70 percent of energy-cost changes pass through to consumers in the short to medium run. Because of imperfect competition, consumers' share of the welfare cost is 25–75 percent smaller than complete-pass-through models imply.
*Use in 1.1:* **this partly contradicts** the Related literature claim that EITE firms "pass through a fraction of carbon costs, typically under half" [NeuhoffRitz2019]. Pass-through is industry-specific. Cite both and say the evidence ranges from below half (EU trade-exposed sectors) to about 70 percent (US domestic-market industries). The difference itself supports the trade-exposure story.

**Grubb2022.** Grubb, Jordan, Hertwich, Neuhoff, Das, Bandyopadhyay, van Asselt, Sato, Wang, Pizer and Oh, "Carbon Leakage, Consumption, and Trade", *Annual Review of Environment and Resources* 47 (2022). [text: Leiden repository] This review covers production- versus consumption-based accounts, leakage evidence and policy options. Net emission transfers from developing to developed countries peaked around 2006 and have declined since. Studies find no evidence of leakage, partly because industry is shielded, which the authors consider incompatible with deep decarbonization. They classify price-based options by where the incentive is placed: (a) at production or import (BCAs); (b) at final consumption (a carbon-footprint consumption charge); (c) "separation of the incentives between point of production and a direct charge on the use of carbon-intensive materials". Option (c) is a climate excise contribution on materials at default intensities combined with benchmarked output-based free allocation. They note that a consumption charge on actual footprints would give exporting jurisdictions an incentive to shift to low-carbon production, but that tracing actual embodied emissions through value chains is administratively very demanding.
*Use in 1.1:* **this is the closest prior statement of the two-track idea and must be cited in "What is new".** Option (c) is a production/consumption split. The 1.1 contribution relative to it is: (i) a revenue-neutral feebate with an updated benchmark rather than free allocation inside an ETS; (ii) a VAT carbon component across goods rather than an excise on a few materials; (iii) charging on rebuttable *actual* intensity, which is what makes Proposition 7 work. The excise uses a uniform default and gives foreign producers no incentive. Grubb et al.'s administrative warning about actual footprints is the main objection to (iii).

### Optimal policy theory

**Markusen1975.** Markusen, "International Externalities and Optimal Tax Structures", *Journal of International Economics* 5(1) (1975), 15–29. [metadata] VERIFY content. This is the classic result that with a transboundary externality and trade, the optimal unilateral policy combines a production tax and a consumption tax (or trade taxes), not a production tax alone. Weisbach et al. (2023) attribute the production–consumption split to it.
*Use in 1.1:* this is already cited in Related literature as the theoretical root of Proposition 1. Keep it.

**Hoel1996.** Hoel, "Should a Carbon Tax Be Differentiated across Sectors?", *Journal of Public Economics* 59(1) (1996), 17–32. [metadata] VERIFY content. A unilateral carbon tax should be uniform across sectors if tariffs are available, and differentiated (lower for trade-exposed sectors) if they are not.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the theoretical precedent for setting a different (production-side) price on trade-exposed sectors. The feebate is a cleaner way to cut the average price while keeping the marginal price.

**KeenKotsogiannis2014.** Keen and Kotsogiannis, "Coordinating Climate and Trade Policies: Pareto Efficiency and the Role of Border Tax Adjustments", *JIE* 94(1) (2014), 119–128 (IMF WP 12/289). [metadata; IMF eLibrary refused access] VERIFY content. From prior knowledge: when carbon taxes are not equalized across countries, border tax adjustments are part of a Pareto-efficient policy, and full BTAs plus suitable carbon taxes can achieve global efficiency.
*Use in 1.1:* this is already cited for the efficiency case for border adjustment. The two-track architecture achieves the BTA's effect on the consumption base through VAT.

## 2. Border carbon adjustments: design, CBAM, WTO law, developing-country exposure

**KeenParryRoaf2022.** Keen, Parry and Roaf, "Border Carbon Adjustments: Rationale, Design and Impact", *Fiscal Studies* 43(3) (2022), 209–234 (IMF WP/21/239; Tokyo College TC-E-21-1). [text: Tokyo College WP] This is the template paper. BCAs are potentially the most effective domestic instrument against competitiveness and leakage concerns, but design is critical. The authors recommend limiting coverage to EITE sectors and benchmarking initially on domestic intensities. Feebates and output-based rebates are "broadly equivalent" to a tradable emission-rate standard: they encourage lower emissions per unit of output, but not lower output. BCAs alone do not solve free-riding but might be a step toward an international carbon price floor. They note the tension between destination-basis BCAs and origin-basis Paris accounting.
*Use in 1.1:* cited throughout. It supports the Proposition 4 framing (the feebate lacks an output margin) and Implementation (domestic benchmarks first, rebuttable defaults later). The paper's treatment of exports as optional contrasts with 1.1's zero-rating.

**Mehling2019.** Mehling, van Asselt, Das, Droege and Verkuijl, "Designing Border Carbon Adjustments for Enhanced Climate Action", *AJIL* 113(3) (2019). [text: Cambridge Core, open] This is a comprehensive legal analysis of BCAs under GATT (Articles I, II:2(a), III, XX) and the SCM Agreement. It proposes a design balancing legal, administrative and environmental considerations: narrow coverage of basic materials, embodied emissions on a default-and-rebuttal basis, credit for foreign policies, and use of revenue for climate cooperation.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the citation for "the open question is whether carbon intensity, a process characteristic, can differentiate otherwise like products" (Implementation, Trade law). Its support for crediting foreign policies backs the paper's call (with @CFPB13) for recognizing non-price instruments.

**Cosbey2019.** Cosbey, Droege, Fischer and Munnings, "Developing Guidance for Implementing Border Carbon Adjustments: Lessons, Cautions, and Research Needs from the Literature", *REEP* 13(1) (2019), 3–22. [metadata; open-access copy exists at VU Amsterdam but was not fetched] VERIFY content. From prior knowledge, this is design guidance: restrict BCAs to energy-intensive trade-exposed sectors, use default intensities with an option for firm-specific data, be wary of export rebates for legal reasons, and treat least-developed countries with care.
*Use in 1.1:* this supports the rebuttable-default design in Implementation.

**IsmerNeuhoff2007.** Ismer and Neuhoff, "Border Tax Adjustment: A Feasible Way to Support Stringent Emission Trading", *European Journal of Law and Economics* 24(2) (2007), 137–164. [metadata] VERIFY content. The paper argues that a BTA levied on imports at the emissions of best available technology (and rebated on exports) can be WTO-compatible.
*Use in 1.1:* this supports the trade-law paragraph. Its BAT benchmark is the origin of the "generous default" design.

**Evans2021.** Evans, Mehling, Ritz and Sammon, "Border Carbon Adjustments and Industrial Competitiveness in a European Green Deal", *Climate Policy* 21(3) (2021). [text: Strathclyde repository] Using a simple analytical framework, the paper shows that replacing free allocation with an *import-only* BCA weakens EU producers' competitiveness in export markets. An import BCA lets free allocation be reduced, but not to zero. Combining an import BCA with free allocation for exports raises legal risk, which careful design can reduce.
*Use in 1.1:* **this directly supports the Introduction's claim that a border adjustment "leaves exports exposed".** It also supports the two-track case for keeping a production-side instrument (the feebate) alongside a destination charge, which is the 1.1 architecture.

**Neuhoff2026.** Neuhoff, Sato, Ballesteros, Böhringer, Borghesi, Cosbey, Deletombe, Felsmann, Ismer, Johnston, Linares, Matikainen, Pauliuk, Pirlot, Quirion, Rosendahl, Sniegocki, van Asselt and Zetterberg, "Industrial Decarbonization in a Fragmented World: Carbon Pricing with Border Adjustments Using Standardized Values", *Energy Policy* 216 (2026), 115405. [text: LSE repository, CC-BY] This is a policy analysis. The current CBAM, based on production-specific intensities, leaves leakage channels open for materials with complex value chains. It does not address exports. It invites *resource shuffling*, in which foreign producers send their cleanest output to the EU and dirtier output elsewhere, and imports can then undercut domestic producers. Standardized default values would discriminate against clean foreign producers and raise WTO concerns. The authors propose keeping ETS free allocation at benchmark level for producers and adding a *standardized liability per tonne of material, independent of production process or location*, applied to domestic and imported material along the value chain with export relief.
*Use in 1.1:* **this is a second close precedent and a direct challenge to Proposition 7.** The architecture matches 1.1 (benchmark-based production instrument plus a consumption-side liability with export relief). But the authors deliberately choose *standardized* rather than actual intensities because actual-intensity charges invite resource shuffling. Proposition 7 requires charges that rise with actual intensity. The paper must answer this. Possible answers: shuffling is a measurement problem that a feebate in the exporting country removes, because the whole sector's intensity falls; and default values set at a high percentile, as in @EU2025DV, limit the gain from shuffling. Cite @BushnellChenZaragozaWatkins2014 for the evidence on shuffling.

**ClausingWolfram2023.** Clausing and Wolfram, "Carbon Border Adjustments, Climate Clubs, and Subsidy Races When Climate Policies Vary", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 37(3) (2023), 137–162 (NBER WP 31310). [text: NBER WP] The paper distinguishes two spillovers: free-riding by less ambitious countries, and competitive distortion between *pricing* and *subsidizing* jurisdictions (for example, the EU against the US Inflation Reduction Act). One policy cannot address both. BCAs and clubs can encourage upward harmonization but carry risks, and divergent approaches can trigger subsidy races.
*Use in 1.1:* this supports the measurement framework. Recognizing non-price policies at the border needs a carbon-price-equivalent metric (@CFPB17). It also frames the feebate as a hybrid of price and subsidy: dirty firms pay and clean firms are subsidized, so it sits between the two camps Clausing and Wolfram describe.

**CBAMExposure2025.** Maliszewska, Fischer, Jung and Chepeliev, *Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) Exposure Indices: Methodological Note*, World Bank (2025), doi:10.1596/43614. [text: World Bank OKR] The note builds trade-exposure and relative-emissions-intensity indices for CBAM sectors and estimates excess carbon payments relative to EU producers as shares of exports, sectoral output and GDP. Macroeconomic impacts are generally small, but a few countries are highly exposed. Mozambique (aluminium) is the leading example.
*Use in 1.1:* this is evidence for the developing-country paragraph (Calibration, "two lessons"). The countries most exposed to a destination charge are those with the largest gain from a feebate (Proposition 7 and the steel table). Not yet cited; add it.

**Shapiro2021.** Shapiro, "The Environmental Bias of Trade Policy", *QJE* 136(2) (2021) (NBER WP 26845). [text: NBER WP] In most countries, tariffs and non-tariff barriers are lower on dirty (high CO₂ per dollar) than on clean industries, mainly because downstream (cleaner) industries are more protected. This creates an implicit global subsidy to CO₂ in traded goods of \$550–800 billion a year. Equalizing trade policy across clean and dirty goods would cut global emissions with little change in real income.
*Use in 1.1:* this shows that existing border policy already prices carbon *negatively*, so a destination charge on embodied carbon corrects an existing bias rather than adding a new distortion. It is useful for the race-to-the-top section and for the WTO paragraph.

**FarrokhiLashkaripour2025.** Farrokhi and Lashkaripour, "Can Trade Policy Mitigate Climate Change?", *Econometrica* 93(5) (2025), 1561–1599. [abstract-level; only the online supplement was downloaded] VERIFY content before citing. From prior knowledge: in a quantitative trade model, unilateral carbon border taxes achieve little global abatement, while climate clubs that use trade penalties can induce broad participation.
*Use in 1.1:* this bears on the race-to-the-top claim. Read the main paper before citing.

**BushnellChenZaragozaWatkins2014.** Bushnell, Chen and Zaragoza-Watkins, "Downstream Regulation of CO₂ Emissions in California's Electricity Sector", *Energy Policy* 64 (2014), 313–323. [metadata] This is already in the 1.1 .bib. It models how first-deliverer regulation of imported power in California invites resource shuffling.
*Use in 1.1:* this is evidence for the Neuhoff2026 objection to Proposition 7. Keep it.

## 3. Output-based rebating, intensity standards and feebates

**Fischer2001.** Fischer, "Rebating Environmental Policy Revenues: Output-Based Allocations and Tradable Performance Standards", RFF Discussion Paper 01-22 (2001). [text: RFF] Tradable performance standards, emissions taxes with market-share rebates and output-based permit allocation are all equivalent to an emissions tax plus an output subsidy. For a given abatement target they rely too much on intensity reduction and too little on output reduction, so marginal abatement costs are higher and output prices lower than optimal. The welfare cost rises with output substitutability and in the long run. Defining product groups creates rent-seeking over benchmarks.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the foundation for Proposition 4. Its point about product-group definition is an objection to the benchmark design not yet discussed in Objections.

**FischerFox2007.** Fischer and Fox, "Output-Based Allocation of Emissions Permits for Mitigating Tax and Trade Interactions", *Land Economics* 83(4) (2007), 575–599 (RFF DP 04-37). [text: RFF DP] The paper compares grandfathering, output-based allocation (OBA) and auctioning in partial equilibrium and in a GTAP-based CGE with labour–leisure choice. OBA's implicit output subsidy mitigates tax interactions and can beat grandfathering. Auctioning with revenue recycling gives the highest welfare. OBA by historical emissions best counteracts leakage but costs more in welfare.
*Use in 1.1:* this supports Proposition 4 and the welfare caveat in Objections (General equilibrium). The two-track equivalence holds allocation fixed but not welfare.

**FischerFox2012.** Fischer and Fox, "Comparing Policies to Combat Emissions Leakage: Border Carbon Adjustments versus Rebates", *JEEM* 64(2) (2012), 199–216. [metadata; the RFF DP 09-02 copy was refused (403)] VERIFY content. From prior knowledge: in a CGE comparison, full border adjustment (import charge plus export rebate) is most effective at reducing global emissions, but where border adjustment is limited, a domestic output-based rebate can be more effective at limiting leakage. Monjon and Quirion (2011) cite it in this way (confirmed).
*Use in 1.1:* this supports Proposition 4 and the Related literature claim.

**FischerFox2011.** Fischer and Fox, "The Role of Trade and Competitiveness Measures in US Climate Policy", *AER: Papers and Proceedings* 101(3) (2011), 258–262. [metadata] This is already in the 1.1 .bib. It is a short comparison of BCAs and output-based rebates for the US.

**MonjonQuirion2011.** Monjon and Quirion, "Addressing Leakage in the EU ETS: Border Adjustment or Output-Based Allocation?", *Ecological Economics* 70(11) (2011), 1957–1971. [text: HAL WP] This is a partial-equilibrium model of EU electricity, steel, cement and aluminium. Auctioning with border adjustment is the most efficient way to tackle leakage and often produces *negative* leakage. Auctioning in power combined with OBA in exposed industries (including indirect emissions) is relatively efficient. OBA is less effective than BAs against leakage but better at protecting production. The main reason is that BAs reduce EU consumption of carbon-intensive products and OBA does not.
*Use in 1.1:* this is direct quantitative support for the architecture. The production instrument protects output, and the consumption-side charge delivers the demand reduction that OBA lacks. Cite it in the Cost section after Proposition 4.

**HollandHughesKnittel2009.** Holland, Hughes and Knittel, "Greenhouse Gas Reductions under Low Carbon Fuel Standards?", *AEJ: Economic Policy* 1(1) (2009), 106–146 (NBER WP 13266). [text: NBER WP] An intensity standard such as a low carbon fuel standard (LCFS) taxes high-carbon and subsidizes low-carbon output. It cannot be first best and can raise emissions if supply or demand of the high-carbon good is steep or the standard lenient. In simulations of a national LCFS cutting intensity by 10 percent, emissions are unlikely to rise. But surplus losses are \$80–760 billion a year, and the average cost is \$307–2,272/tCO₂, against \$16–290 billion and \$60–868/tCO₂ for an efficient policy achieving the same cut.
*Use in 1.1:* this is cited for Propositions 4–5. The magnitudes are a warning that intensity instruments alone can be very costly per tonne. The Proposition 5 condition (output can rise if the marginal producer is clean and the benchmark lenient) is Holland et al.'s mechanism.

**Holland2012.** Holland, "Emissions Taxes versus Intensity Standards: Second-Best Environmental Policies with Incomplete Regulation", *JEEM* 63(3) (2012), 375–387 (NBER WP 15262). [text: NBER WP] With leakage (incomplete regulation) or market power, an intensity standard can yield *higher* welfare than any emissions tax, including the optimal one. Adding a consumption tax corrects the standard's excess consumption and can reach the first best, *even when an emissions-tax-plus-consumption-tax combination cannot*. Holland also notes that a consumption tax "does not violate trade law". Output-based updating does better than a fixed intensity standard because of its flexibility.
*Use in 1.1:* **this is the closest formal precedent for Proposition 1 and the strongest support for it.** An intensity instrument plus a consumption tax is first best under leakage. Cite it in the Equivalence section and in "What is new". The novelty claim must then rest on the destination/VAT implementation, the revenue corollary and Propositions 6–7, not on the pairing itself.

**FowlieReguantRyan2016.** Fowlie, Reguant and Ryan, "Market-Based Emissions Regulation and Industry Dynamics", *JPE* 124(1) (2016), 249–302. [metadata; the NBER number tried, w17985, was a different paper] VERIFY content. From prior knowledge: in a dynamic oligopoly model of US cement, auctioning permits under incomplete regulation can reduce welfare because of leakage and market power. Output-based updating can outperform auctioning.
*Use in 1.1:* this is cited in Related literature. Keep it, but check the claim "OBA can outperform auctioning once leakage and market power are counted" against the paper.

**FowlieReguant2022.** Fowlie and Reguant, "Mitigating Emissions Leakage in Incomplete Carbon Markets", *JAERE* 9(2) (2022), 307–343. [metadata] VERIFY content. It estimates leakage-mitigating output-based rebates by sector for the US using cost pass-through and trade-elasticity estimates.
*Use in 1.1:* this is a candidate source for sector calibration of $k$ and $\eta$ (Calibration section).

**SternerIsaksson2006.** Sterner and Isaksson, "Refunded Emission Payments Theory, Distribution of Costs, and Swedish Experience of NOx Abatement", *Ecological Economics* 57(1) (2006), 93–106. [metadata] VERIFY content. Since 1992 Sweden has charged large combustion plants for NOx emissions and refunded the revenue in proportion to energy output. This is a working revenue-neutral feebate on intensity. From prior knowledge, it is associated with large intensity reductions.
*Use in 1.1:* **this is the main real-world precedent for the Track 1 instrument and is currently missing from the paper.** Cite it in Implementation, "The production track", alongside Canada's output-based pricing system.

**Johnson2006.** Johnson, "Feebates: An Effective Regulatory Instrument for Cost-Constrained Environmental Policy", *Energy Policy* 34(18) (2006), 3965–3976. [metadata] VERIFY content. This is a general treatment of feebates as a revenue-neutral instrument that sets a marginal incentive without a net cost burden.
*Use in 1.1:* this is the definitional citation for "feebate".

**DHaultfoeuille2014.** d'Haultfoeuille, Givord and Boutin, "The Environmental Effect of Green Taxation: The Case of the French Bonus/Malus", *Economic Journal* 124(578) (2014), F444–F480. [metadata] VERIFY content. From prior knowledge, the French car feebate (2008) shifted sales toward low-CO₂ cars but raised total car sales enough that CO₂ emissions *increased* in the short run.
*Use in 1.1:* **this is empirical evidence for the risk in Proposition 5.** A feebate whose rebate side is generous can raise output enough to raise emissions. Cite it in the Cost section with Holland et al.

## Synthesis: where the two-track architecture sits, and what is new

The two-track architecture combines four bodies of work that have mostly developed apart, and it is closer to some of them than v0.3 currently admits.

**The theory of the split is old.** Markusen (1975) showed that with an international externality the optimal unilateral policy taxes both production and consumption. Hoel (1996) showed that a carbon tax should be lower on trade-exposed sectors when tariffs are unavailable. Weisbach, Kortum, Wang and Yao (2023) restate the result in modern form and add a supply-side (extraction) leg. Most directly, Holland (2012) proves that an intensity standard combined with a consumption tax can reach the first best under leakage, and that an emissions tax combined with a consumption tax sometimes cannot. Böhringer, Rosendahl and Storrøsten (2017) appear to show that output-based allocation plus a consumption tax replicates a border adjustment (to be confirmed). Proposition 1 is therefore best presented as a clean, implementable statement of a known allocation result, not as a new theorem. Its corollary on revenue (the difference between the tracks is $t$ times carbon in net exports) is the carbon analogue of the Lockwood–Whalley origin–destination neutrality argument and is genuinely useful for policy, because it tells finance ministries which countries gain and which lose revenue.

**The policy design has close precedents in European proposals.** Grubb et al. (2022) list "separation of the incentives between point of production and a direct charge on the use of carbon-intensive materials" as one of three trade-compatible options. Pollitt, Neuhoff and Lin (2020) quantify a consumption charge on basic materials at about €80/tCO₂ (roughly 10 percent extra emission reduction, no leakage). Neuhoff et al. (2026) propose benchmark-based free allocation plus a standardized liability per tonne of material with export relief. Each of these is a production-plus-consumption architecture. The 1.1 contribution relative to them is threefold. First, a *revenue-neutral feebate with an updated benchmark* replaces free allocation inside an ETS, so the production track needs no ETS and no auction revenue and can be adopted by a developing country alone. Second, the consumption track runs through the *VAT* across all goods rather than an excise on a few materials, using an institution 170-plus countries already operate. Third, and most consequentially, 1.1 charges imports on *rebuttable actual intensity*, while the Neuhoff group deliberately chooses standardized values to avoid resource shuffling and WTO discrimination concerns.

**The third choice is both 1.1's main novelty and its main exposure.** Proposition 7, that each exporter's most competitive feebate rate equals the charge in its markets, holds only if the charge rises with the exporter's actual intensity. Under the standardized-value design that Neuhoff et al. (2026) and Ismer et al. (2016) favor, there is no such incentive. Under the actual-intensity design, Mattoo et al. (2009) find large export and income losses for developing countries if exporters do not respond, and Neuhoff et al. and Bushnell et al. (2014) warn of shuffling. 1.1 should argue explicitly that a sector-wide feebate in the exporting country is the remedy for shuffling, because it lowers intensity across all of the exporter's output rather than in the slice sold to the importer, and that this is why the two tracks belong together internationally as well as domestically.

**The crediting result has a direct antecedent.** Strand (2020) derives, for a donor-supported carbon tax, a break-even payment of one-half of the tax rate when the host keeps the reduction toward its NDC: the same threshold as Proposition 6. He shows the break-even rises to about 1.5 times the rate when the credit is transferred abroad with a corresponding adjustment. 1.1 adds the feebate setting, the best-response result $t = \pi$, and the argument that crediting a sector-wide intensity change avoids the project-additionality failures documented by Probst et al. (2024), with less than 16 percent of credits real, and Calel et al. (2025), with at least 52 percent of Indian wind CDM credits non-additional. But Proposition 6 must be restricted to results-based finance in which the host keeps attribution, or extended to the Article 6 case where the threshold is higher.

**The evidence base supports the motivation but not its strongest form.** Ex-post studies find little or no leakage at EU ETS prices to date (Naegele and Zaklan 2019; Dechezleprêtre et al. 2022; Colmer et al. 2025), partly because free allocation shields industry and partly because prices were low. Model-based leakage is 5–25 percent (Branger and Quirion 2014). Pass-through ranges from under half in EU trade-exposed sectors to about 70 percent in US domestic manufacturing (Ganapati et al. 2020). The paper's case is therefore prospective: it is about the rates of \$80–100 that the High-Level Commission range implies, not about leakage already observed.

**What is genuinely new**, stated narrowly: (i) the specific pairing of a revenue-neutral, updated-benchmark feebate with a destination carbon component of VAT and forward-announced energy obligations; (ii) the closed-form residual-cost result ($\tfrac12 t\Delta e$) and its calibration to EU default intensities for steel; (iii) the exporter best-response result under intensity-based destination charges, with its implication that destination pricing turns leakage into a race to the top; (iv) the extension of the Strand price point to feebates with the $t = \pi$ best response; and (v) the use of a carbon-price-equivalent metric to make non-price production instruments creditable and recognizable at the border. The paper should claim these and cite Holland (2012), Grubb et al. (2022), Pollitt et al. (2020), Neuhoff et al. (2026) and Strand (2020) as the closest prior work.
