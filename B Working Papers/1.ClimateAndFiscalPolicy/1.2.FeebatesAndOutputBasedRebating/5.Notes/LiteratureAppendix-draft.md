## 1. Theory: intensity standards, tradable performance standards, output-based allocation

**Fischer2001**: Fischer, C. (2001). *Rebating Environmental Policy Revenues: Output-Based Allocations and Tradable Performance Standards.* RFF DP 01-22. [Full text]
Fischer asks what happens when emission-tax revenue or permit rents are rebated by output share. She covers tradable performance standards (TPS), emission taxes with market-share rebates, and output-based allocation (OBA). All three are formally a tax on emissions plus a subsidy to output. For any abatement target, they shift effort toward lower emission rates and away from output contraction. Compared with the optimum, they produce higher marginal abatement costs and *lower output prices*. The welfare cost rises with output substitutability and is likely much larger in the long run. Political or market-failure justifications (leakage, market power) may exist, but they depend on industry characteristics.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the canonical statement of the paper's mechanism. It shows that the "no price rise" property is a feature, and that its cost is the forgone demand margin. Cite it in Section 2.3, and in the industrial companion's "Two types of OBR".

**FischerFox2007**: Fischer, C. & Fox, A. K. (2007). Output-based allocation of emissions permits for mitigating tax and trade interactions. *Land Economics* 83(4): 575–599. [Abstract]
The paper compares permit allocation rules in a CGE model with pre-existing tax and trade distortions: lump-sum grandfathering, OBA and auctioning. The output subsidy implicit in OBA mitigates tax interactions, which grandfathering does not. OBA with sector shares based on value added behaves like auctioning with revenue recycling. OBA based on historical emissions supports heavy emitters and counters leakage more effectively, but at higher welfare cost.
*Use in 1.2:* Supports the claim in "Tax Burdens of Different Policy Instruments" that grandfathered permits are a windfall while OBA is not. It also qualifies the claim: the choice of benchmark (emissions-based versus output-based) is itself a distributional choice. This bears on the "grandfathered-for-transition" pool variant.

**HollandHughesKnittel2009**: Holland, S. P., Hughes, J. E. & Knittel, C. R. (2009). Greenhouse gas reductions under low carbon fuel standards? *AEJ: Economic Policy* 1(1): 106–146. [Full text, NBER w13266]
An intensity standard (here, California's LCFS) taxes high-carbon output and subsidizes low-carbon output. It can therefore *raise* emissions, cannot be first-best, and the best feasible standard may lower welfare. Their US simulation is a 10% cut in carbon intensity. It is unlikely to raise CO₂, but it costs $80–760 billion a year in surplus, and its average cost is $307–2,272 per tCO₂. An efficient policy reaching the same reduction costs $16–290 billion, at $60–868 per tCO₂. A perverse emissions increase is more likely when supply or demand for the dirty good is steep and when the standard is lenient.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the main theoretical caution against the paper's claim that OBR delivers "the same decarbonization" as a tax. The output subsidy expands total generation, so emissions fall less than intensity does. The power paper needs a sentence on this, and on why it matters less where demand is inelastic in the short run.

**FowlieReguantRyan2016**: Fowlie, M., Reguant, M. & Ryan, S. P. (2016). Market-based emissions regulation and industry dynamics. *JPE* 124(1): 249–302. [Full text, NBER w18645]
This is a dynamic structural model of the US Portland cement industry, with market power, investment, entry and exit, and import competition. A full carbon price exacerbates the market-power distortion (Buchanan 1969) and induces leakage. Net welfare is negative over the whole range of carbon values considered. Designs that combine an emissions penalty with an output-based rebate *welfare-dominate* a pure tax or auctioned cap-and-trade. A border adjustment on imports delivers sizable gains at high carbon values.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the strongest empirical-structural support for the industrial feebate in concentrated, trade-exposed sectors. Cite it alongside Fischer (2001) to show that the output subsidy can be second-best desirable rather than merely tolerated.

**FowlieReguant2022**: Fowlie, M. & Reguant, M. (2022). Mitigating emissions leakage in incomplete carbon markets. *JAERE* 9(2): 307–343. [Abstract]
The paper derives industry-specific leakage-risk measures and calibrates leakage-mitigating output-based subsidies, using US energy-price variation as a proxy. Absent mitigation, leakage is substantial. Output subsidies targeted on the paper's risk measures reduce it significantly. Coarse targeting on emissions intensity and trade exposure (current practice) delivers "a small fraction" of the benefit at significant cost.
*Use in 1.2:* The industrial paper's rebate is uniform within a sector. This paper suggests that the *rebate rate* should vary with measured leakage risk, not only with the sector average. It is relevant to benchmark design.

**BushnellHollandHughesKnittel2017**: Bushnell, J. B., Holland, S. P., Hughes, J. E. & Knittel, C. R. (2017). Strategic policy choice in state-level regulation: the EPA's Clean Power Plan. *AEJ: Economic Policy* 9(2): 57–90. [Full text, NBER w21259]
Under the Clean Power Plan, US states could choose rate-based (lbs/MWh) or mass-based (cap-and-trade) compliance. Rate standards can be efficient only if the carbon price equals the social cost of carbon and the rate is uniform across states. Uncoordinated choices "scramble" the merit order. In the western US simulation, state-specific caps raise average abatement cost 16% above a uniform cap. State-specific rate standards can nearly double it, and mixed regimes are 17–29% costlier. Consumers prefer rate standards (lower prices). Producers can prefer inconsistent regimes. The treatment of new plants strongly affects entry.
*Use in 1.2:* A power feebate is a rate-based instrument. This paper is direct evidence that it lowers consumer prices relative to a cap, which supports the paper. It is also evidence that it must cover the whole interconnected market with one benchmark, or dispatch is distorted. This is relevant to an Indian state-level scheme like the paper's "Ambo–Eebo" example.

**Goulder2022**: Goulder, L. H., Long, X., Lu, J. & Morgenstern, R. D. (2022). China's unconventional nationwide CO₂ emissions trading system: cost-effectiveness and distributional impacts. *JEEM* 111: 102561. [Full text, NBER w26537]
China's national ETS is a TPS for power (output-based allocation against benchmarks). The implicit output subsidy makes it less cost-effective than cap-and-trade. Low-intensity units expand output and even emissions, and trading equalizes net-of-subsidy rather than gross marginal costs. Central-case costs are **47% higher** than C&T. Using three benchmarks instead of one raises cost per tonne a further **18%**. In its favor, the TPS produces *smaller electricity price increases* than an equally stringent C&T, which shifts burden from consumers to generators and implies less leakage.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the closest real-world analogue to the paper's power feebate, and the best citation for the price claim. It also quantifies the cost of the separate pools by technology type that the paper proposes ("draw a system boundary around each fuel type"). Multiple benchmarks cost about 18% more.

**WangPizerMunnings2021**: Wang, B., Pizer, W. A. & Munnings, C. (2021). *Price Limits in a Tradable Performance Standard.* NBER WP 28368. [Full text]
This is a Weitzman prices-versus-quantities comparison for TPSs. When the coefficient of variation of the TPS credit price exceeds about 50%, a price mechanism dominates. Gains from moving to a price mechanism are about 60% for China's national market and about 20% for California's LCFS. The paper discusses consignment auctions based on actual or expected output.
*Use in 1.2:* The paper's feebate is a *price*-based intensity instrument (a fixed $t$ with rebate), not a TPS. This paper supports that design choice over a tradable standard.

**BoehringerFischerRivers2022**: Böhringer, C., Fischer, C. & Rivers, N. (2022). *Intensity-Based Rebating of Emission Pricing Revenues.* World Bank PRWP 10069. [Full text]
The paper compares lump-sum rebating, output-based rebating, abatement-based rebating, and two new intensity-based variants (rebates rewarding intensity reductions, either per unit of output or as a share of emission payments). At a given emission price, intensity-based output rebating yields the largest intensity reductions. Abatement-based rebating yields the largest output reductions. OBR puts the least pressure on output and emissions. Partial-equilibrium theory is combined with a multisector CGE model. The intensity-based variants deliver deeper sectoral cuts while keeping output, at the cost of diverging marginal abatement costs.
*Use in 1.2:* This is directly relevant to the "grandfathered-for-transition" variant, which pays more to incumbents who close coal. That is a form of abatement- or intensity-based rebating, and this paper gives its theory. A follow-up is Böhringer, Fischer & Rivers (2024), *Rebating Revenues from Unilateral Emissions Pricing*, WB PRWP 10922 (not read; VERIFY).

**HagemHoelSterner2020**: Hagem, C., Hoel, M. & Sterner, T. (2020). Refunding emission payments: output-based versus expenditure-based refunding. *ERE* 77: 641–667. [Abstract]
The paper compares output-based refunding (OBR) with expenditure-based refunding (EBR), which refunds fees in proportion to spending on abatement equipment. To hit the same abatement target as a tax, the fee must be *higher* under OBR and *lower* under EBR. Both are cost-ineffective, because firms over-rely on technology relative to output reduction. Both may be politically preferred when keeping output is an objective.
*Use in 1.2:* This states the "fee premium" result that 1.1's Proposition 3 derives. It also offers an alternative (EBR) close to the CBAM Article 8(2) carve-out discussed in 1.3, so it is worth a cross-reference.

**BoehringerRosendahlStorrosten2017**: Böhringer, C., Rosendahl, K. E. & Storrøsten, H. B. (2017). Robust policies to mitigate carbon leakage. *JPubE* 149: 35–46. [Metadata only]
The paper is already cited in the industrial companion for the result that OBR combined with a consumption tax is second-best and preferred to OBR alone. The NTNU repository copy could not be fetched this session. VERIFY the claim against the text before relying on it.
*Use in 1.2:* Supports the industrial paper's CBCT (OBR + excise) argument.

**Fischer2011**: Fischer, C. (2011). Market power and output-based refunding of environmental policy revenues. *Resource and Energy Economics* 33(1): 212–230. [Metadata only]
Fischer extends output-based refunding to imperfect competition. **Note:** the paper's Ambo–Eebo example has five generators, so it is an oligopoly, and this is the relevant theory. Results not read; VERIFY before citing.

## 2. Feebates and refunded charges in practice

**DHaultfoeuille2014**: D'Haultfœuille, X., Givord, P. & Boutin, X. (2014). The environmental effect of green taxation: the case of the French *bonus/malus*. *Economic Journal* 124(578): F444–F480. [Abstract]
France introduced a new-car feebate in 2008, with rebates of up to €1,000 and fees of up to €2,600. Sales shifted strongly toward rebated classes. Because the shift increased total car sales, the authors estimate the policy's *environmental effect to be negative* in the short and long run. Feebates "should be designed carefully to achieve their primary goal."
*Use in 1.2:* This is the clearest **contradicting evidence** on the output effect. A feebate whose pivot is set too lenient, or which is not revenue-neutral (the French scheme ran a deficit), subsidizes output enough to raise emissions. The paper's statement that feebates "naturally convert into a carbon tax" should be qualified: they do only if the pivot tracks the fleet average.

**Durrmeyer2022**: Durrmeyer, I. (2022). Winners and losers: the distributional effects of the French feebate on the automobile market. *Economic Journal* 132(644): 1414–1448. [Full text, TSE WP 950 version]
A structural demand-and-supply model with heterogeneous consumers. The feebate reduced average CO₂ emissions of new cars by **1.6%** (2.2 g/km), but raised NOx and PM. Global welfare rose. Consumers lost when taxed to fund the scheme, but French manufacturers' profits more than offset this. With a uniform tax the scheme favors middle incomes. It becomes progressive with an income-proportional tax.
*Use in 1.2:* This is a calibrated benchmark for feebate effect sizes: real but modest intensity effects. The distributional result supports the paper's political-feasibility argument.

**DurrmeyerSamano2018**: Durrmeyer, I. & Samano, M. (2018). To rebate or not to rebate: fuel economy standards versus feebates. *Economic Journal* 128(616): 3076–3116. [Full text, TSE WP 732 version]
The paper simulates France and the US, holding environmental benefit and revenue fixed. Standards cost up to **1.7 times** as much welfare as a feebate. A tradable standard dominates both the simple standard and the feebate. Effects on manufacturers are heterogeneous.
*Use in 1.2:* Evidence that a price-based feebate beats a non-tradable intensity standard of equal stringency. Relevant to the paper's choice of a rebated tax over a mandate.

**SternerIsaksson2006**: Sterner, T. & Höglund Isaksson, L. (2006). Refunded emission payments theory, distribution of costs, and Swedish experience of NOx abatement. *Ecological Economics* 57(1): 93–106. [Metadata only]
The Swedish NOx charge (from 1992) on large combustion plants refunds revenue in proportion to useful energy output. It is the textbook refunded-emission-payment scheme. Abstract not obtained. Prior knowledge: emission intensity fell sharply in the first years at modest net cost to the sector. VERIFY all numbers.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the oldest working analogue to power-sector OBR (energy output as the rebate base). It belongs in Section 1.1 next to German and Meszler (2010).

**Bonilla2015**: Bonilla, J., Coria, J., Mohlin, K. & Sterner, T. (2015). Refunded emission payments and diffusion of NOx abatement technologies in Sweden. *Ecological Economics* 116: 132–145. [Metadata only]
The paper evaluates technology diffusion under the Swedish refunded NOx charge. Not read; VERIFY findings.
*Use in 1.2:* A possible source for the paper's dynamic "race to decarbonize" claim (Table 3), if it shows faster diffusion under refunding.

**PizerZhang2018**: Pizer, W. A. & Zhang, X. (2018). China's new national carbon market. *AEA Papers and Proceedings* 108: 463–467. [Abstract]
Describes the December 2017 launch of China's national ETS. When fully implemented, it would more than double global CO₂ emissions covered by pricing. Its design follows the pilots and differs from US and EU programs (intensity-based allocation, power first).
*Use in 1.2:* A short citation for "China already runs a rate-based (feebate-like) power-sector instrument". It pairs with Goulder et al. (2022) for the cost comparison.

**BhandariShrimali2018**: Bhandari, D. & Shrimali, G. (2018). The perform, achieve and trade scheme in India: an effectiveness analysis. *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews* 81: 1286–1295. [Metadata only]
Evaluates India's PAT (tradable energy-intensity targets for designated consumers). Publisher blocked; not read. VERIFY findings.
*Use in 1.2:* India's PAT is an existing intensity-trading scheme in the paper's own case country. Any India feebate proposal should say how it would interact with or replace PAT, and with the coal cess (not covered here).

## 3. Power-sector transition: coal-to-gas switching, retirements, pass-through, distribution

**Leroutier2022**: Leroutier, M. (2022). Carbon pricing and power sector decarbonization: evidence from the UK. *JEEM* 111: 102580. [Full text, HAL version]
A synthetic-control evaluation of the UK Carbon Price Support (from 2013) against other European power sectors. UK power emissions fell **20.5–26% per year** on average in 2013–2017. The fall came through three channels: lower intensity in remaining plants, closure of high-emitting plants, and faster closure of plants already at risk under the EU Large Combustion Plant Directive. Cumulative abatement equals 60–81% of what the UK's second carbon budget required. External validity rests on coal-to-gas switching potential, limited interconnection (low leakage) and no new coal investment.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the citation the paper lacks for its UK CPS figures in Section 1.3. Its external-validity conditions matter for India and China, where gas switching potential is low: the switch there is to renewables plus storage, which needs investment, not dispatch.

**Gugler2021**: Gugler, K., Haxhimusa, A. & Liebensteiner, M. (2021). Effectiveness of climate policies: carbon pricing vs. subsidizing renewables. *JEEM* 106: 102405. [Abstract]
UK power emissions fell **55% within five years** under the CPS, against little decline in Germany with heavy renewable subsidies and a weak EU ETS price. A modest carbon price (about €30/t) induced large, low-cost abatement where cleaner gas plants could replace coal. Carbon pricing beat wind and solar subsidies in both countries.
*Use in 1.2:* Supports Section 1.3. The authors attribute the effect to the *relative* coal-gas cost shift. That is exactly what the paper argues OBR preserves.

**Gugler2023**: Gugler, K., Haxhimusa, A. & Liebensteiner, M. (2023). Carbon pricing and emissions: causal effects of Britain's carbon tax. *Energy Economics* 121: 106655. [Abstract]
Using hourly plant data and discontinuities at the 2013, 2014 and 2015 rate changes, the authors find a **26% (36.3 MtCO₂) fall in emissions in three years**. The tax changed marginal costs by emissions intensity, pushing coal out and letting gas fill the gap. They flag leakage through imports and the EU ETS waterbed.
*Use in 1.2:* Second, causal citation for Section 1.3. The mechanism, merit-order reshuffling by intensity, is preserved under a revenue-neutral feebate *only if* the rebate does not alter dispatch order. A uniform per-MWh rebate does not.

**FabraReguant2014**: Fabra, N. & Reguant, M. (2014). Pass-through of emissions costs in electricity markets. *AER* 104(9): 2872–2899. [Full text, NBER w19613]
Spanish wholesale market, micro bid data. Emissions costs are **almost fully passed through** to electricity prices. Demand is inelastic, cost shocks are correlated across firms, and price-adjustment costs are small, so firms have little incentive to adjust markups. One implication: free allocation produced windfall profits.
*Use in 1.2:* This underpins the paper's claim that a carbon tax raises power prices roughly one-for-one with the marginal plant's intensity. **Note:** it also implies the *feebate* price effect in a marginal-price market. A uniform per-MWh rebate lowers every bid by the same amount, so the market price moves by $t(e_m - \bar e)$, where $e_m$ is the marginal plant's intensity, not by zero. With coal at the margin (0.9 tCO₂/MWh against an average of 0.66), a $100/t feebate raises wholesale prices by about $24/MWh, roughly 2.4 c/kWh. The paper's own Part 3 text computes the 0.24 difference but then reports a ~1% price effect. The 1% holds for regulated average-cost tariffs or if the rebate is passed to consumers, not for a merit-order market. See the synthesis.

**Hintermann2016**: Hintermann, B. (2016). Pass-through of CO₂ emission costs to hourly electricity prices in Germany. *JAERE* 3(4): 857–891. [Abstract]
Using hourly German spot data and a constructed fossil supply curve, the paper finds allowance costs passed through completely or nearly completely on average, varying over the load curve. There is no economic case for free allocation to power.
*Use in 1.2:* Corroborates Fabra and Reguant (2014). **Note:** it also shows the paper's line "In the EU, there has consequently been a move to output-based allocation" needs care. EU power moved to full auctioning in Phase 3 (2013), and industrial free allocation is benchmark-based with output adjustments [VERIFY wording against the EU ETS Directive].

**DavisHolladaySims2022**: Davis, R. J., Holladay, J. S. & Sims, C. (2022). Coal-fired power plant retirements in the United States. *Environmental and Energy Policy and the Economy* 3: 4–36. [Full text, NBER w28949]
Nearly a third of the US coal fleet retired in the 2010s, and a quarter of the rest has announced retirement. Smaller and older units retire first. A real-options model with machine-learned retirement costs predicts three-quarters of coal capacity retires within twenty years. Policy has limited leverage: a **$20/MWh electricity subsidy extends average life by six years**, and a **$51/t carbon tax brings retirement forward by about two years**.
*Use in 1.2:* This is important and partly **contradicting** for the paper's claim that strong feebate incentives produce "rapid" decarbonization. Retirement timing is driven largely by plant age and retirement costs, and a carbon price at this level shifts it by about two years. A per-MWh rebate that also reaches coal plants (as the paper's simple OBR does) works like the $20/MWh subsidy that *extends* lives. That supports the paper's insistence on a coal moratorium and on restricting rebates to new clean capacity.

**Cui2019**: Cui, R. Y. et al. (2019). Quantifying operational lifetimes for coal power plants under the Paris goals. *Nature Communications* 10: 4759. [Full text, OSTI copy]
Plant-level data in GCAM. Even with no new plants, existing units must retire at about **35 years** for well-below 2 °C, or about **20 years** for 1.5 °C, against a historical average of about 50. Each limit falls a further 5 years if plants under construction come online, and 10 years if all proposed plants are built.
*Use in 1.2:* Gives the paper a number for its "phase-out schedule" paragraph. India's and China's fleets are young, so a schedule consistent with Paris implies the stranded-asset losses the "grandfathered-for-transition" variant is meant to compensate.

**CroninFullertonSexton2019**: Cronin, J. A., Fullerton, D. & Sexton, S. (2019). Vertical and horizontal redistributions from a carbon tax and rebate. *JAERE* 6(S1): S169–S208. [Full text, NBER w23250]
Treasury microdata on 322,000 US families. Measured against consumption, with transfers indexed, a carbon tax is **progressive**. Rebates make it more so. Horizontal variation dominates: within each decile the standard deviation of the burden (1–2% of consumption) exceeds the mean burden (about 0.7%). The poorest decile gains about 1% of consumption on average, but **44% of its families pay more**.
*Use in 1.2:* Qualifies the paper's political-feasibility metric (percent price change). The politically salient losers are within-decile heavy users, which a per-MWh feebate that holds retail prices flat protects better than a dividend.

**Ohlendorf2021**: Ohlendorf, N., Jakob, M., Minx, J. C., Schröder, C. & Steckel, J. C. (2021). Distributional impacts of carbon pricing: a meta-analysis. *ERE* 78: 1–42. [Full text, EconStor OA copy]
An ordered-probit meta-analysis of 53 studies (183 effects, 39 countries) on carbon pricing and fossil-fuel subsidy reform. Progressive outcomes are significantly more likely in lower-income countries and for transport policies. They are also more likely in studies that count indirect effects, demand adjustment or lifetime income.
*Use in 1.2:* Counter-evidence to the paper's implicit premise that power-price rises are a binding regressive constraint in developing countries. The evidence says carbon pricing is often progressive there. The constraint is political salience, not incidence. The paper should say which it means.

**Black2023**: Black, S., Liu, A. A., Parry, I. & Vernon, N. (2023). *IMF Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data: 2023 Update.* IMF WP 23/169. [Abstract]
Global fossil-fuel subsidies were $7 trillion in 2022 (7.1% of GDP), and 18% of that was explicit. **80% of global coal consumption was priced below half its efficient level.** Full price reform would cut CO₂ about 43% below baseline in 2030 and raise 3.6% of global GDP.
*Use in 1.2:* Current data for the paper's 2019-vintage claim that a $70/t tax "more than triples" coal prices. It replaces the outdated World Bank (2018) and IMF (2019) citations.

## 5. Feebates for scarcity: the 2022 gas crisis

**Ruhnau2023**: Ruhnau, O., Stiewe, C., Muessel, J. & Hirth, L. (2023). Natural gas savings in Germany during the 2022 energy crisis. *Nature Energy* 8: 621–628. [Full text, PIK OA copy]
German gas consumption in the second half of 2022 was **23% below** a temperature-adjusted baseline, peaking at 28% in September. Industry cut from September 2021, reaching 27% a year later. Small consumers cut from March 2022, peaking at 28%. Prices drove most of the response. The policy conclusion: keep consumers exposed to prices, and design relief so the savings incentive stays intact.
*Use in 1.2:* This is the empirical foundation for a "scarcity feebate". Relief that is lump-sum, or tied to a baseline quantity rather than current use, keeps the marginal price signal. That is the same design logic as a revenue-neutral feebate. The German gas price brake was set on 80% of past consumption [VERIFY]. It is the obvious policy example, but no paper on it was obtained this session.

## Synthesis (about 700 words)

**Where the paper sits.** The power-sector paper proposes a revenue-neutral carbon charge on generators, rebated per MWh: output-based rebating, which 1.1's series terms call a feebate. Its theory is old and settled. Fischer (2001) showed that a TPS, an emission tax with market-share rebates and OBA are the same instrument: a tax on emissions plus a subsidy to output. Each shifts abatement from the output margin to the intensity margin and keeps output prices below the first-best. Fischer and Fox (2007), Holland, Hughes and Knittel (2009), Hagem, Hoel and Sterner (2020) and Böhringer, Fischer and Rivers (2022) work out the costs. To hit a given emissions target, the charge must be higher than under a tax. Total output expands, and with steep dirty supply or lenient benchmarks emissions can even rise. Against that, Fowlie, Reguant and Ryan (2016) and Fowlie and Reguant (2022) show that in concentrated, trade-exposed industries the output subsidy is a second-best gain, because it offsets market power and leakage. China has already adopted the instrument at scale for power, as a TPS (Pizer and Zhang 2018; Goulder et al. 2022). Sweden has run a refunded emissions charge on combustion plants since 1992 (Sterner and Höglund Isaksson 2006).

**What is new.** The paper adds three things to this literature, and should say so plainly. First, it applies OBR to *electricity as the vector for electrification*, so the output subsidy is a benefit rather than a cost: it avoids raising the price of the low-carbon energy carrier during the transition. Second, the "grandfathered-for-transition" pools pay incumbents to close coal, which is a form of the intensity-based or abatement-based rebating that Böhringer, Fischer and Rivers (2022) analyze. Third, it frames the instrument as a route out of "low-carbon-tax lock-in", a political-economy claim rather than an efficiency claim. Douenne and Fabre (2022) and Dechezleprêtre et al. (2025) give strong support for the premise that visible price increases, misperceived as regressive and ineffective, drive resistance.

**What the evidence says about price effects.** Here the paper needs qualification. In a competitive merit-order market, carbon costs pass through almost one-for-one (Fabra and Reguant 2014; Hintermann 2016). A uniform per-MWh rebate lowers every generator's bid equally. The wholesale price therefore rises by $t(e_m - \bar e)$, the gap between the marginal plant's intensity and the sector average, not by zero. With coal at the margin and the paper's Indian figures (0.9 against 0.66 tCO₂/MWh), a $100/t feebate raises wholesale prices by about 2.4 c/kWh, roughly a fifth of the 11 c/kWh baseline. That is much less than the 54% under a tax, but much more than the "1%" in the paper's table. Goulder et al. (2022) and Bushnell et al. (2017) confirm the direction (rate-based instruments raise prices less than caps) but not "zero". The near-zero result holds in two cases: regulated average-cost tariffs, the norm for Indian state utilities, or a rebate paid to consumers per kWh rather than to generators. The paper should state which market structure it assumes, and recompute the table for both.

**What the evidence says about output and emissions.** The vehicle-feebate evidence is mixed. Durrmeyer (2022) finds a 1.6% cut in average new-car CO₂ intensity. D'Haultfœuille, Givord and Boutin (2014) find that the same French scheme *raised* total emissions, because the rebates boosted sales. A feebate whose pivot does not track the fleet average becomes a net output subsidy. The power-sector evidence on carbon *taxes* is strong: the UK CPS cut power emissions by 20–26% a year (Leroutier 2022), 26% in three years (Gugler et al. 2023) and 55% in five years (Gugler et al. 2021). The authors attribute the effect to the relative coal-gas cost shift, which a revenue-neutral feebate preserves at the margin. Two cautions follow. First, the UK result depended on idle gas capacity, which India and China largely lack; there the margin is new renewables plus storage, which needs capital, not dispatch. Second, retirement timing responds weakly to prices. Davis, Holladay and Sims (2022) estimate that a $51/t tax advances US coal retirement by only about two years, while a $20/MWh subsidy extends plant life by six. A per-MWh rebate paid to coal output behaves like that subsidy. The paper's safeguards are therefore essential, not optional: rebates only for *new* clean capacity, a moratorium on new coal, and pool rules that pay for closure. Cui et al. (2019) give the retirement ages (35 years for 2 °C, 20 years for 1.5 °C) against which the schedule should be set.

**Distribution.** The meta-evidence (Ohlendorf et al. 2021) is that carbon pricing is more often progressive in lower-income countries. Cronin, Fullerton and Sexton (2019) show that within-group losers, not average incidence, drive the politics. Both support the paper's choice of a *price-stabilizing* instrument over a dividend, provided the argument is made on salience rather than regressivity.
