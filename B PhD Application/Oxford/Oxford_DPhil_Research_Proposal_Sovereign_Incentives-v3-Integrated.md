## 1. Background and rationale

The countries where most new clean power must be built pay the most for the capital to build it, and the premium is set less by technology than by doubt that the buyer of the power will pay. Solar and wind plants are almost all upfront cost, so the cost of capital largely sets the price of their electricity. In a stylised solar plant, raising the cost of capital from 4 to 12 percent raises the levelised cost by about 80 percent, from 3.6 to 6.5 US cents per kilowatt-hour (Stretton 2026a). The International Energy Agency reports that a typical solar plant in emerging and developing economies faced a cost of capital two to three times that in advanced economies and China in 2021 (IEA 2025) `[VERIFY: confirm on the Observatory page]`. Modelling shows that such gaps trap developing economies in too little clean investment (Ameli et al. 2021; Calcaterra et al. 2024).

Each part of a remedy already has its own specialists, and they rarely meet. Development banks manage credit and currency risk. Energy regulators write the long-term contracts, power purchase agreements, contracts for difference and regulated asset bases, that make revenue bankable. Macroeconomists study the savings of ageing countries and sovereign funds. Trade analysts study the EU's carbon border adjustment and China's solar exports. Game theorists model climate clubs (Nordhaus 2015). Each literature explains part of the problem; none turns the parts into one proposal a finance ministry could act on. That is the gap this thesis fills.

The starting point is a proposal that has not been worked out. Michael Grubb has argued that Norway's windfall from the energy crisis should be used to underwrite investment in developing countries (Grubb 2023), and Sinha (2023) proposes a highly rated green bank, capitalised by sovereign sponsors, that would make a market in long-term currency hedging. Neither says how the support should be priced so that it rewards a host government's own policy, how its risk should be measured, or why each funder would join. My working papers supply most of the components: a payment intermediary that guarantees the power contract rather than the debt (Stretton 2026b), two-stage finance that turns operating plants into securities pension funds can buy (Stretton 2026c), guarantee capacity that rises with a country's climate performance (Stretton 2026d), and a trust that holds capital and lends its strength (Stretton 2026e). The thesis joins them and tests the result.

Three features of the problem shape the design. First, the binding constraint is the credibility of the payer. Lenders charge less when the party behind a payment cannot walk away: development banks lose very little on sovereign loans because governments keep paying them while restructuring other debt, a preference that is a market custom rather than a contract term, sustained because the value of future access outweighs the cost of repaying (Cordella and Powell 2021 `[VERIFY: citation]`). The same logic holds within countries, between a state-owned utility and its government, or between a government and its central bank or taxpayers. Second, much lending to developing countries carries no collateral, and where collateral is used it is often hidden: about half of China's official lending is effectively secured by cash accounts the lender controls (Gelpern et al. 2021; Kiel Institute 2025 `[VERIFY: author and title]`). Transparent, standard collateral could do openly what escrow accounts do in secret. Third, existing carbon-market incentives run the wrong way: under Article 6 a more ambitious national target makes each credit sale costlier to the host (World Bank 2023), so the market rewards weak targets.

The project also adds time. A government deciding whether to reform its power sector can wait, and waiting has value when reform is costly to reverse (Dixit and Pindyck 1994). Support may matter most when it raises the payoff to acting now. The thesis tests a "use it or lose it" rule, under which guarantee capacity rises with credible early action and lapses if unused, against one-off support.

The question is geographical because financing risk is set by place and institution. The same solar plant costs more to finance where the buyer is a weak state utility or contracts are hard to enforce, and country, sector and technology determinants of financing cost interact and respond to policy (Steffen and Waidelich 2022). The thesis treats the country, its power-sector institutions and its relationships with external creditors as the unit that prices risk. It builds on the Oxford Sustainable Finance Group's work on energy-transition risk and the cost of capital (Zhou, Wilson and Caldecott 2021).

## 2. Research questions

The core question is: **can a climate reserve funded by surplus sovereigns, lending its strength through guaranteed contracts whose capacity rises with credible policy and lapses if unused, bring the cost of capital for clean power in developing countries close to the host sovereign's own, at an expected cost the funders will bear, and make sustained decarbonisation each member's best choice?**

Three subsidiary questions make it answerable in three years.

1. **Risk.** How much of the gap between a clean-power project's cost of capital and its sovereign's can guaranteed contracts, currency cover and collateralised refinancing remove, per dollar of expected loss and capital used by the reserve?
2. **Incentives.** What sovereign incentive is needed to induce a host government to adopt a given policy package once fiscal costs, contingent liabilities, political costs and the value of waiting are counted, and does use-it-or-lose-it capacity supply it at lower expected cost than one-off support?
3. **Coalition.** Under what conditions does each prospective member gain more inside the arrangement than outside it: the EU (pricing carbon at its border), China (selling solar equipment it has the capacity to make), surplus sovereigns such as Norway (seeking safe long-term returns), development banks and host governments?

The project tests three hypotheses that can fail.

- **H1 (contracts, estimated).** The fall in the cost of capital associated with multilateral support is larger where national policy already provides long-term contracted revenue than where projects sell at merchant prices. H1 is rejected if the association is absent or reversed.
- **H2 (threshold, simulated and tested against cases).** A reduction in the weighted average cost of capital changes a sovereign's adoption decision only if it exceeds a threshold, Δr\*, set by the package's political cost net of its domestic benefits and the value of delay. H2 is rejected if reforms followed support well below the calibrated threshold, or failed despite support well above it.
- **H3 (binding relationships, estimated).** For comparable projects and country risk, the cost of capital is lower where the payment is backed by a party locked into a long-term relationship with the payer (a multilateral bank with preferred-creditor treatment, or a government standing behind its own utility) than where it is backed by an arm's-length guarantor. H3 is rejected if the difference is absent once country risk is controlled for.

## 3. Framework

The framework has three layers: the host sovereign, the reserve and its coalition, and the market that buys the resulting securities.

At the host layer, the government chooses whether to adopt a policy package now or wait. It adopts package P when S + F(Δr) − C − λL ≥ O, where S is the domestic net benefit, F(Δr) the present value of the financing saving from a cost-of-capital reduction Δr, C the contingent liability the government takes on, L the losses concentrated on organised groups such as incumbent generators, λ the political weight on those losses (Grossman and Helpman 1994) and O the option value of waiting. Solving for equality gives Δr\*. S and L come from the Climate Policy Assessment Tool's sectoral and distributional estimates; C is measured with the IMF's public-private partnership fiscal risk model (IMF 2019); λ is bounded by revealed preference from each case's history. Use-it-or-lose-it capacity enters as a fall in O: unused capacity lapses, so waiting costs more.

At the reserve layer, a trust holds capital in safe assets and uses it as second-loss cover on guaranteed contracts, behind the host government and ahead of a highly rated backer (Stretton 2026e). Its capital can come from surplus sovereigns' windfalls, from reserve assets (the IMF Board allowed Special Drawing Rights to buy hybrid capital in multilateral banks in May 2024), and from coalition members. The trust's cost is expected loss plus the cost of holding a buffer sized for a 1 percent annual chance of exhaustion. On the development banks' record of sovereign losses, each dollar of expected loss buys about 80 dollars of interest saving; under market-like losses without preferred-creditor treatment, about 2.7 (Stretton 2026d, 2026e). Which of these cases applies depends on whether the trust inherits the binding relationship that multilateral banks enjoy, which H3 tests.

At the coalition layer, each member's payoff is modelled explicitly. The EU's border adjustment entered its charging phase in January 2026, with 75 percent of revenue going to the EU budget from 2028 `[VERIFY: Commission source]`; a destination-based design that prices carbon where goods are consumed (Stretton 2026f) gives exporters a reason to price carbon at home and the EU a revenue stream that could capitalise the reserve. China has roughly twice the solar module capacity that world demand absorbs (`[VERIFY: consultancy estimate]`), export prices near 0.11 US dollars per watt in October 2026, and zero tariffs since May 2026 for imports from the African countries with which it has diplomatic relations; guaranteed contracts in those countries raise demand for equipment China already makes. Surplus sovereigns gain a safe, long-dated return. The arrangement is stable if each member's gain inside exceeds its gain outside.

The securities layer closes the loop. Once payment risk is removed, operating plants can be pooled across countries and split into a first-loss tranche, a thin mezzanine and a senior "synthetic sovereign bond" rated on the guaranteed contracts and the cushion beneath them rather than on the host (Stretton 2026c). In that design the levelised cost of solar falls by about a fifth (14 percent with a thick 30 percent cushion). Senior tranches that are standard and collateralised could be bought by pension funds and, where eligible, accepted by central banks as collateral, which is how a niche product becomes part of the international financial system.

## 4. Methods and data

Two modelling work packages form the core; a data package and a case package support them. Each quantity is estimated from data, calibrated from public documents or simulated, and the thesis keeps the three apart.

**Work package 1 (data, year 1).** A country-technology panel of financing costs from the IEA Cost of Capital Observatory and IRENA's 45-country survey (IRENA 2023), using the methods of Steffen (2020), joined to the World Bank's Private Participation in Infrastructure database, which flags multilateral support. Each country is coded for its revenue contract (auction, power purchase agreement, contract for difference, merchant), its payer (state utility, intermediary, private buyer) and the relationship behind the payment (multilateral, sovereign, arm's-length). Regressions of the cost of capital on country risk, contract type, relationship type and their interactions test H1 and H3. With observational data these are associations, and the thesis will say so. Default and recovery data come from the GEMs Consortium; collateral practice is compared with the AidData contract sample (Gelpern et al. 2021).

**Work package 2 (core: guarantees and reserve, years 1–2).** A transparent project-finance model of utility-scale solar and wind computes debt and equity cash flows, the cost of capital, the levelised cost and the value of waiting, under merchant, contracted and guaranteed revenue, with and without currency cover and two-stage refinancing. A reserve module computes expected loss, buffer and capital use for each instrument across loss cases. The output for each instrument is the reduction in financing cost per dollar of expected loss and capital used, and the remaining distance to the sovereign's own cost (RQ1).

**Work package 3 (core: incentives and coalition, year 2).** The adoption model computes Δr\* for each case and compares one-off support with use-it-or-lose-it capacity (RQ2, H2). The coalition module computes each member's payoff inside and outside the arrangement, using public trade, border-adjustment and investment data, and the macro-fiscal-energy scenario tool I have built for the debt and growth effects (Stretton 2026g) (RQ3).

**Work package 4 (validation, year 3).** Two or three country cases, chosen in year 1 for variation in contract type and relationship type. Candidates are Kenya, India and Indonesia, where my working papers already hold calibrations. Each case uses public documents and about 15 to 20 semi-structured interviews with finance ministry, utility, regulator and development-bank staff, to test whether the H2 threshold explains observed adoption or delay.

**Ethics and feasibility.** Interviews need approval from the University's research ethics committee and will use informed consent and anonymised notes. The data are public; no World Bank internal or confidential material will be used. The scope fits three years because the components already exist in working-paper form: the thesis integrates, calibrates and tests them rather than building from nothing. If time runs short, the coalition module is reported as a calibrated illustration rather than an estimate.

## 5. Contribution and fit

The thesis makes three contributions. It gives a single framework that joins risk management, guaranteed contracts, sovereign incentives, coalition payoffs and the macro-fiscal effects of clean-power finance, which the literature treats separately. It tests, with public data, whether binding relationships and contract design explain financing costs once country risk is held fixed. And it turns a proposal that exists only as an opinion piece into a costed design that a finance ministry, a development bank or a surplus sovereign could evaluate.

The project fits the Smith School's work on transition finance and the cost of capital, and Dr Shrimali's research on de-risking and transition finance. I bring a practitioner's knowledge of the instruments: I am a climate-fiscal economist at the World Bank, working on climate-fiscal analysis, power-sector modelling and the Climate Policy Assessment Tool, and I have worked on climate-policy design since 2005 `[STEPHEN TO CONFIRM: wording, given the contract end date]`.
