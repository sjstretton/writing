I want to find out how multilateral development banks can use financial de-risking to lower the cost of capital for clean power while making credible climate policy a government's own best choice. The countries where most new clean power must be built pay the most for capital, and because solar and wind plants are almost all upfront cost, that premium sets the price of their electricity. Development banks can cut it with guarantees, credit enhancement and concessional finance. They cannot set a country's power-market rules or carbon price. My proposal asks how de-risking can reward national policy rather than stand in for it, and what each unit of mitigation costs the bank.

The question is geographical, which is why I am applying to the School of Geography and the Environment. The same solar plant costs more to finance where the buyer of its power is a weak state utility or where contracts are hard to enforce. I treat the country and its power-sector institutions as the unit that prices risk, and I will test two hypotheses: that development-bank support lowers financing costs most where governments already offer long-term contracts through auctions or contracts for difference, and that cheaper capital changes a government's decision only above a threshold set by the political cost of reform.

My career has prepared me for this question from both sides of the balance sheet. I built credit-risk and capital models for investment banks, including a valuation and risk suite for a corporate loan portfolio at Barclays Capital, and later modelled climate mitigation as a research associate at Cambridge. Since 2019 I have worked at the World Bank on climate and fiscal policy tools for finance ministries, above all the Climate Policy Assessment Tool (CPAT), which estimates the fiscal, distributional and emissions effects of carbon pricing and related reforms. What I lack is formal training in empirical cost-of-capital estimation and real-options methods, which the DPhil would give me.

The Oxford Sustainable Finance Group is the natural home for this work. Dr Shrimali leads its transition finance research, and his work with Christian Wilson finds that cheaper debt raises low-carbon investment more than high-carbon investment, and that stronger climate policy amplifies the effect. That is the relationship my first hypothesis tests at project level. The group's India Transition Finance Programme matches my first case study, India, and its work on the energy transition and the cost of capital gives me data and methods to build on.
