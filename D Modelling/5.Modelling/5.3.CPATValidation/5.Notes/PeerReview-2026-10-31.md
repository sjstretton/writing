# Peer review of 5.3, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "Validating CPAT: Baselines, Back-Casts and Model Comparisons" (v0.1, 29 Oct 2026, about 5,100 words of body), written from a full reading before any revision. Every number in the paper was checked against its source: the CPAT methodology as published in the public CPAT-AI repository and website (https://github.com/cpmodelai/cpat_ai, read at commit 108f976 of 31 Oct 2026; https://cpmodelai.github.io/cpat_ai/), and the external literature. The paper had no policy brief; the deck was the old four-slide format.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a useful question (how far can a finance ministry trust CPAT's numbers?) and gives a sensible answer (for ranges and policy comparisons, not point forecasts). Its main value is assembly: the validation work scattered through a 57,000-word methodology chapter is set out as tables, with the replication evidence from the CPAT-AI rebuild added. Most numbers are transcribed correctly. But the paper has four substantive problems. (1) Its reading of the ex-post evidence compares two different metrics and omits the largest recent meta-analysis, which points the other way; the conclusion that CPAT's responses are "at or above the top" of the evidence does not survive. (2) Its conclusion that CPAT's road-transport and residential-electricity responses "may be too large" rests on one panel estimate (gasoline, one estimator) that sits below the meta-analytic literature; the wider evidence brackets CPAT's value. (3) Several numbers are copied from sources that are themselves wrong or internally inconsistent (the Egypt calibration gap, the direction of the coal-price error), and some source statements have changed since v0.1 (proprietary tables, tolerances, bug counts, file paths). (4) The contribution is not stated against the literature on evaluating energy and integrated assessment models, which has a framework (Wilson et al. 2021) the paper should use. It also lacks the executive summary and the policy brief the series now requires, and it does not say clearly which numbers are the documentation's and which are new.

## Major points

**M1. The ex-post comparison compares unlike metrics.** CPAT's "semi-elasticity" in the documentation is the average *level* gap between the policy run and the baseline, averaged over 2019–2023 (−2.4 percent) or 2019–2035 (−6.2 percent), for a $10/tCO₂ step. Green's (2021) "0–2 percent per year" is an annualized reduction for whatever prices and coverage the studied schemes had, and Rafaty, Dolphin and Pretis (2020) estimate a reduction in the *growth rate* of emissions (0.05 percent a year per $1/tCO₂, imprecise). The paper sets −2.4 percent against "0–2 percent a year" and calls it "at or above the top". On a common basis the gap is smaller. If a $10 price cuts the growth rate by *g* a year, the level gap after *k* years is about *gk*, and its average over the first *N* years is *g*(*N*+1)/2. CPAT's −2.4 percent over five years then corresponds to *g* ≈ 0.8 percent a year, and its −6.2 percent over 17 years to *g* ≈ 0.7 percent a year (author's calculation; it assumes the gap builds linearly, which overstates *g* for a model whose response is partly immediate). Rafaty et al.'s point estimate implies *g* ≈ 0.5 percent a year at $10. So CPAT is of the same order as the best cross-country estimate, somewhat larger, not "beyond the top". *Fix:* state the metrics, give the conversion with its assumption, and drop "at or above the top".

**M2. The strongest counter-evidence is missing.** Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al. (2024), a systematic review and meta-analysis of 80 causal ex-post evaluations of 21 schemes (483 effect sizes), find statistically significant reductions of 5 to 21 percent (4 to 15 percent after correcting for publication bias), "despite the low level of prices in most instances". Best, Burke and Jotzo (2020) find that emissions from fuel combustion grew about 2 percentage points a year more slowly in the 43 countries with a carbon price. Read alongside Green (2021) and Rafaty et al. (2020), the ex-post literature does not show that CPAT over-responds; it shows a wide band, and its authors themselves name the price elasticity of emissions as the main evidence gap. *Fix:* add both, present the ex-post literature as a range with the reasons it is wide, and say plainly that it cannot yet test CPAT's per-dollar response.

**M3. The elasticity conclusion overstates one estimate.** The paper concludes that CPAT's responses for road transport and residential electricity "may be too large", citing the documentation's panel estimates (gasoline −0.19 against CPAT's −0.61; residential electricity −0.1 against −0.4). The documentation itself says the gasoline comparison rests on the one estimator (common correlated effects) that passed its tests, and that this estimator's ability to capture long-run effects in a static model "is not clear". The meta-analytic literature puts the long-run price elasticity of gasoline demand well above −0.19: Brons et al. (2008) find a mean of −0.34 in the short run and −0.84 in the long run. CPAT's −0.61 lies inside that range. *Fix:* report the panel result as one estimate, set it against the meta-analyses, and conclude that the gasoline elasticity is uncertain, not that it is too high; keep residential electricity and the service sector as the clearest gaps (the documentation's own conclusion), and leave the full assessment to paper 5.4.

**M4. Numbers copied from wrong or inconsistent sources.**
- *Egypt calibration gap.* The paper (from the CPAT-AI documentation) says the calibrated baseline runs "16 to 29 percent below the inventory for 2022–2024". Recomputed from the documentation's own table (inventory 252.2, 267.4, 285.3 Mt; calibrated 211.1, 182.9, 203.5 Mt): 16.3, 31.6 and 28.7 percent. The range is 16 to 32 percent; the 2023 figure also includes a separate fuel-use shock (register issue I92). The 19 percent jump is 19.5 percent (252.2/211.05).
- *Coal-price direction.* The paper says CPAT's projected coal prices for power were "on average above observed prices". The documentation's text says so, but the same sentence says the ratio of projected to observed prices is concentrated "under the value of 1", which means below; and the China case (projected below observed, emissions overstated) points the same way. The source contradicts itself. *Fix:* report the contradiction rather than one side of it.
- *The duplicated coal-price RMSE table* (noted in v0.1) is confirmed: it repeats the energy CO₂ table digit for digit.

**M5. Statements that have changed since v0.1.** The CPAT-AI repository moved on between 29 and 31 October 2026. Six tables are proprietary, not four, and the public data edition (Public01) holds Egypt's rows of them perturbed within ±3 percent. The power engineer replication now states its tolerance (208 legacy rows to 1e-13), where Table 5 said "tolerance not stated". The road replication compares exactly 1,091 series per run, and its relative difference is taken against max(10⁻⁶, |legacy|), not max(1, |reference|) as the table note says; the legacy comparison uses max(|legacy|, 1). The bug register at release 1.1.0 lists 231 legacy bugs (24 high, 63 medium, 144 low), against 228 at release 1.0.2. All repository paths in the `.bib` (for example `docs/CPAT_ai_documentation_qmd/05_Power.qmd`, `06_Testing.qmd`, `excel_ai/BUGS.md`) no longer exist; the model documentation is now `docs/CPAT_model_documentation_qmd/`, the register `excel_ai/core/BUGS.md` and `ISSUES.md`. The methodology is published as a corrected 2023 edition on the project website, with its validation section unchanged.

**M6. The contribution is not placed in the model-evaluation literature.** The paper cites the Energy Modeling Forum, energy-forecast retrospectives and Sargent's verification-validation distinction, but not the recent synthesis of how integrated assessment and energy-economy models are evaluated: Wilson et al. (2021) set out six evaluation methods (historical simulations, near-term observations, stylized facts, model hierarchies from simple to complex, model intercomparison projects, and sensitivity analysis), note that historical simulations are "relatively uncommon", and argue for combining the methods. Its four tests map onto that framework, and the paper's contribution (applying it to a single-country fiscal tool in daily use, adding replication, and naming what is missing) should be stated against it. O'Neill and Desai (2005), who find that US energy-consumption projections were within about 4 percent at 10–13 years only because large errors in GDP and energy intensity offset, are the closest prior evidence for the paper's "right trend, wrong components" reading of the back-cast.

**M7. Which numbers are the documentation's and which are new is not clear.** The credit footnote says "every number in its tables is the documentation's own", but the paper also reports numbers it derives (shares of rows, the 16–29 percent gap, the range "20 to 35 percent"). *Fix:* say in the introduction and in each table's source line what is reported from the documentation, what is recomputed, and what is the author's own calculation (after M1, the growth-rate conversion; after M4, the Egypt gaps; the reading of Table 4 as a range).

## Minor points

1. *Back-cast fit and trend.* Normalized RMSE measures the level error, not the trend. "Tracks the trend ... with a normalized RMSE of 5 percent" conflates the two. Say that the level error is 5 percent of mean emissions, and that the documentation judges the trend separately, from charts.
2. *Retrospectives are not back-casts.* Craig, Gadgil and Koomey (2002) and Koomey et al. (2003) argue for looking back at how past forecasts fared, not for re-running models from a past base year. Re-word.
3. *Enerdata comparison.* "CPAT's simpler 'average' power model comes closer to Enerdata" comes from the Enerbase comparison of power-sector CO₂; the carbon-price sensitivity test found "good alignment" for both power models. Separate the two.
4. *Table 1's title* ("only four report a summary statistic") is contestable: the air-pollution comparison reports shares of countries and the elasticity work point estimates. The real point is that the three comparisons with other energy models report no measure of fit. Re-title.
5. *EPM appendix.* All 20 Appendix H countries are African; the main text's 14 include five outside Africa (Iraq, Jordan, Türkiye, Ukraine, Vietnam). "34 mostly African countries" is right (29 of 34); "almost all in Africa" for the appendix should be "all in Africa".
6. *The 2023 back-cast and the IEA comparison.* The documentation says the IEA comparison covers "2019, 2020 and 2030" in one place and "2019, 2020 and 2021" in another; the paper uses the first. Note it.
7. *One term for one thing.* "Back-cast" (paper) and "hindcasting" (documentation): keep "back-cast" and say once that the documentation calls it hindcasting. "Total effective carbon price" should be "Total Effective Carbon Price" as in 6.1.
8. *Sensitivity design.* One-at-a-time changes miss interactions (for example, high elasticities with the engineering power model), and the documentation does not say what the "standard deviation" of an elasticity is. Say so; it bounds the "20 to 35 percent" reading.
9. *Road transport flag.* The long sentence in the "fits" section (three descriptions of road transport) is hard to read; split it.
10. The credit footnote should name the methodology as published (2023 edition, corrected online 31 Oct 2026) and the CPAT-AI release used (1.1.0).
11. The working paper needs an executive summary (chapter 1), a link to the brief, and section ids that the brief can link to.

## Checks that passed

Table 2 (all twelve back-cast statistics, rounded correctly; the implied mean observed emissions, RMSE divided by normalized RMSE, are plausible for each country, for example about 9.9 Gt CO₂e for China and 7.0 Gt for the United States, 2000–2017); Table 3 (all ten semi-elasticities); Table 4 (every row, including the asymmetric price-elasticity effects: +10, +6, −9, −22 percentage points); the panel elasticities quoted (−0.1 against −0.4; 0.16 against 0.65; −0.19 against −0.61; 0.33–0.56 against 0.81); the functional tests (seven tax levels, two power models, 826 output parameters, 218 countries); the EPM set-up (about $40/tCO₂ by goal seek, 2030 against EPM's 2040); the air-pollution shares (61, 55, 44 percent); Table 5's road (3.8e-14), power-price (5.6e-17), distribution (4e-4, 7e-6), Excel-AI (about 5e-15, 5e-14) and Coded CPAT (7,458 codes; 1,684 codes; 1e-9) figures; the legacy comparison (272 rows; 81 and 91 percent of energy rows in 2022; all 15 CO₂ rows within 5 percent, median 1.2 percent; 2030 median CO₂ gap 12 percent; 12 percent of price rows within 5 percent); Coded CPAT's speed ("under a second"); register issue I100 for the road-transport flag; Egypt's 2041 population in the UN projection (147.0 million, also in the repository's population table).

## The strongest objection

A skeptic will say that a model tested mainly by its own authors, on a six-country back-cast that feeds in observed world prices and on chart comparisons with no metric, has not been validated at all, and that "use it for ranges" is a polite way of saying so. The answer is partly yes: the paper should say that the published evidence is weak by the standards of Wilson et al. (2021), and that its value is in showing exactly which tests are missing and how cheap they now are to run (Coded CPAT runs a country in under a second). The defensible claim is that CPAT's *relative* answers (rankings of policies, the ratio of power to transport response, the sign and order of revenue) are supported, while its levels are not tested.

## What the brief and the deck need

The brief should be written for the people who commission or use CPAT runs (finance-ministry economists, World Bank and IMF country teams): what the checks show, how to read a CPAT number (a range, with the elasticities and the power model named), what to ask for in every run, and what validation to fund. No equations; one table (the sensitivity of the G20 result) and one figure (the back-cast errors). The deck should move from four slides to about ten: the question; the four tests; base-year fit and the calibration trap; the back-cast; the comparisons; the ex-post evidence; sensitivity; replication; how to read a CPAT number; what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.2 and the new brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). M1–M3 changed the paper's conclusions on the ex-post evidence and the elasticities; M4–M5 corrected numbers and sources; M6–M7 added the literature and a statement of which numbers are new. Two source errors found here (the duplicated coal-price table and the contradiction on the direction of the coal-price error) and the 16–29 percent gap in the CPAT-AI documentation are recorded for the CPAT-AI register, which this paper cannot edit.
