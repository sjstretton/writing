# Peer review of 5.4, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "Are CPAT's Price Elasticities Right? A Check Against the Evidence" (working paper v0.1, 29 Oct 2026; about 5,600 words of body; four-slide deck; no policy brief), written from a full reading before any change to the master. Every number in the paper was recomputed from `2.Source/elasticity_check.py` and the CPAT legacy defaults, and every cited number was checked against the source or against the CPAT documentation in the public CPAT-AI repository (`docs/CPAT_full_documentation_qmd/03_Mitigation.qmd`, including the image that holds its Tables A–C) and the 2022 CPAT technical notes. The reference check is recorded in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a useful question that nobody has answered in print: whether the default price elasticities of the Climate Policy Assessment Tool (CPAT) match the published evidence, pair by pair. Its structure is sound (how CPAT works, what the evidence says, how CPAT compares, what the differences do, a protocol), its arithmetic is right, and its two structural findings (one-year adjustment and no cross-price terms) are the most important things in it. But the central comparison has a circularity problem, several literature numbers are second-hand and one set is misattributed, the evidence base omits the most relevant independent studies, and three claims overreach. Fixed, the verdict shifts: CPAT's defaults sit at or below the uncorrected meta-analytic means for most pairs, two pairs are clearly too high, and the problem is less the level than publication bias and timing. The paper also lacks an executive summary, a policy brief and a proper deck.

## Major points

**M1. The comparison partly tests CPAT against itself.** The evidence range for every fuel includes the "mean" and "adjusted" values of Labandeira, Labeaga and López-Otero (2017) *as reproduced in CPAT's documentation (Table A)*. These are the numbers CPAT was calibrated to. A default "in range" of a set that contains its own target is a weak pass. *Fix:* keep CPAT's calibration source in the table but mark it, add independent evidence, and report the verdict against the independent evidence alone and the ratio of CPAT's value to the independent median.

**M2. The numbers attributed to Labandeira and coauthors are CPAT's, not the authors'.** The paper says "the long-run means are −0.51 for electricity, −0.57 for natural gas, −0.53 for gasoline and −0.39 for diesel" and calls them Labandeira's. These are the sample means in CPAT's Table A. The authors' own published summary of their global meta-analysis (Labandeira, Labeaga and López 2016, *Papeles de Energía* 2, Table 2; working-paper version, 416 studies and 1,942 estimates) gives long-run values of −0.69 for electricity, −0.85 for natural gas, −0.72 for gasoline and −0.60 for diesel, and for the EU −0.60, −0.78, −0.79 and −0.64 (Table 4). The 2017 journal article itself could not be read (paywalled; the working-paper PDFs were unreachable), so the paper must say which numbers come from where. Also, Table A's "Total (assumed transport)" row is a sector category that *CPAT* assumed to be transport; the paper's "the remaining, mostly transport, estimates" is an inference the source does not support.

**M3. The evidence base misses the most relevant independent studies.** For electricity: Espey and Espey (2004), residential long-run mean −0.85 (mostly US studies); Csereklyei (2020), EU long-run −0.53 to −0.56 residential and −0.75 to −1.01 industrial. For gasoline in the countries CPAT is most used for: Arzaghi and Squalli (2015), long-run −0.25 in 32 fuel-subsidizing economies, directly relevant to subsidy reform. For income elasticities: Burke and Csereklyei (2016), the source CPAT itself cites (residential electricity 0.78, transport energy 0.86, industry 0.88, 2010 cross-section of 132 countries); Havranek and Kokes (2015), gasoline income elasticity 0.66 uncorrected and 0.23 corrected for publication bias. The IMF's own description of CPAT's calibration (Black et al. 2022: long-run elasticities "generally between −0.5 and −0.8") is the natural benchmark for what the tool claims. *Fix:* add all of these (all verified), and recompute.

**M4. With the corrected evidence, the verdict changes.** Recomputed: eight of ten pairs are in range on the 0.05 tolerance rule; services electricity (−0.70 low and middle income) now sits at the elastic edge of an electricity range whose most elastic estimate is −0.69, not "high"; industrial diesel (−1.06) and services gasoline (−1.04) remain well above every estimate for their fuels. Against the independent medians, CPAT is *below* (less elastic than) the uncorrected means for six of ten pairs (ratios 0.64–0.93) and above them for the four pairs built on sector deviations or one study (services electricity 1.32, coal 1.16, services gasoline 1.51, industrial diesel 1.71). The paper's story, "three too high", should become: the levels are at or below the uncorrected means; two sector-deviation values are too high and a third is at the edge; and the real question for the large pairs is publication bias, documented for gasoline (corrected −0.31, against CPAT's −0.64) and for fuel-subsidizing countries (−0.25). Abstract, introduction, figure, deck all need the new numbers.

**M5. The timing calibration needs justification and bounds.** The paper sets the usage-margin speed to 0.30 a year from the *total* short-run to long-run ratio for gasoline (0.09/0.31), which conflates margins, and applies vehicle turnover (6.7 percent a year, verified in Black et al. 2022, Annex C) to coal in industry and household electricity, where the capital stock turns over more slowly (the same annex uses 1.2–1.8 percent a year for buildings). The headline ("after one year about a fifth; after five about 60 percent; five-year responses overstated by 45–70 percent") is right for the central case (recomputed: one-year share 20–26 percent; five-year share 58–69 percent; overstatement 45–72 percent), but the paper should show bounds. Recomputed: if usage responds fully within the year, the five-year overstatement is 29–52 percent; with building-speed capital turnover, 67–108 percent. The case is also stronger than the paper says: CPAT's own 2022 technical notes list "short and long-term elasticity distinctions not included" as a known issue, noting that the developers "eliminated the distinction" and that residential power demand "is inelastic in the short term", and link the ad hoc Covid adjustment to "too high price elasticities in the short term". Cite it.

**M6. One claim about emission cuts contradicts the paper's own limits section.** "In CPAT, which has little switching, the demand margin carries nearly all of the cut, so an overstatement of demand elasticities by a factor of two overstates CPAT's emission cuts by close to a factor of two." CPAT's power sector does switch, through the generation-cost elasticity (−0.5 for nearly every technology and country in the public data) and the engineering model, and the limits section itself says the power response often carries the largest cut. *Fix:* restrict the claim to end-use cuts outside power.

**M7. The panel estimate is not clean long-run evidence.** The CPAT documentation's panel estimate for road gasoline (−0.19) comes from a static common-correlated-effects estimator, and the documentation itself says "the extent to which it captures long-term impact of driving variables in a static model is not clear". The paper treats it as one of "three independent lines of evidence" for a lower long-run value; the third line (Galindo et al. 2015, −0.30) is reported only second-hand through the same documentation. *Fix:* present the panel estimate as a caveated signal, and rest the argument on the verified bias-corrected meta-analysis and the subsidizing-economy estimate.

**M8. The rebound comparison compares different things.** CPAT's documentation (and the paper) say the product term offsets 15 percent of the total elasticity when both margins are −0.3, "below the 26–29 percent" long-run rebound in Dimitropoulos, Oueslati and Sintek (2018). But the meta-analysis measures rebound as the share of an efficiency gain taken back by more use, which in CPAT's equation is −ε_U: 30–40 percent for road gasoline and diesel. On a like-for-like definition CPAT's rebound is at or slightly above the meta-analytic long-run value. *Fix:* correct.

**M9. The contribution needs stating against the closest prior work.** The closest prior work is CPAT's own: the documentation's calibration tables (A–C), its panel estimation, and the sensitivity analysis reported in paper 5.3. The paper should say what it adds beyond them: an outside, pair-by-pair register with verified numbers, the separation of calibration source from independent evidence, the bias-corrected and subsidizing-country benchmarks, the timing bounds, and the effect on the Total Effective Carbon Price. It should also note a discrepancy in the documentation: Table C reports CPAT's road gasoline elasticity as −0.77, while the defaults give −0.64 (low and middle income) and −0.58 (high income) and the panel section quotes −0.61.

**M10. The strongest objection is not stated.** A meta-analytic mean is not a parameter of CPAT's two-margin equation: published estimates differ in horizon, in what they hold constant (other fuel prices, the capital stock) and in country mix, and the evidence on tax salience (Andersson 2019: a carbon-tax elasticity of gasoline demand three times the price elasticity) cuts the other way from publication bias. The paper mentions salience but not as an objection to its own method. *Fix:* state the objection and answer it: the check is a test of consistency, not an estimate; bias and salience pull in opposite directions; the recommendation is a publication-bias case beside the central result, not a cut in the defaults.

## Minor points

1. Huntington, Barrios and Arora (2019) is described as a review "for Brazil, China, India, Mexico and Russia"; it *emphasizes* those five but covers many other developing countries (Iran, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, Venezuela and others). Its diesel average of −0.50 rests on two estimates (China households −0.80; Nigeria +0.10); say so. Its residential electricity figure is −0.32 in Table 9 but −0.49 in Table 13; use Table 9 and note it.
2. "Dahl's survey values" and the script's "Dahl (2004)" are Dahl and Roman (2004), for industrialized economies, as reported by Huntington et al. (Table 13). Label correctly.
3. Li, Linn and Muehlegger (2014): "about three times as much" is not in the abstract or the NBER summary checked here; reword to "more strongly".
4. Serletis, Timilsina and Vasetsky (2010): "lowest in power generation and transport" could not be confirmed; the working-paper abstract says the sectoral estimates are "consistently below unity". Reword.
5. "Nobody has published a fuel-by-fuel check": CPAT's documentation contains one against its own targets (Table C). Say "no outside check against the wider literature".
6. The documentation says the upward adjustment for developing countries is on the "extensive margin"; the defaults put it on the usage (intensive) margin (−0.1 more in low- and middle-income countries for every usage elasticity; efficiency elasticities identical). Note the discrepancy.
7. The power sector's conditional elasticity is −0.5 "for nearly every technology and country", not "every": the public data show a few exceptions (for example −0.01 for coal in Botswana).
8. "Ten fuel-sector pairs that carry most of the emissions" is not shown; say "ten main pairs".
9. The income-elasticity comparison relies only on Huntington et al.; add Burke and Csereklyei (2016) and Havranek and Kokes (2015) (M3). Residential electricity at 0.8 is then at the top of the evidence (0.53–0.78), not clearly high; diesel in transport (0.6) is low against 0.86–1.13; gasoline (0.7) is in the uncorrected range but three times the bias-corrected 0.23.
10. Old numbering in the folder: the bib header says "paper 4.4", the script says "Paper 4.4" and "paper 1.7"; the copied model's header points to "B Working Papers/". Fix.
11. The five-year overstatement is 45–72 percent, not "45 to 70".
12. The working paper cites paper 5.1 for the ranges of CPAT's elasticities; cite the data directly.
13. CPAT country coverage: the series uses "more than 180 countries" (5.0 cross-review); the 2022 technical notes say "more than 150" and Black et al. (2022) "over 170". Keep the series wording but do not attribute it to these sources.
14. The paper has no executive summary, and the first italic line does not link to a brief.
15. Several "Source:" lines put a code path in the caption; keep them, but tables and figures should state their message in the caption.

## What the brief needs

There is no brief. Write one (Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 5.4) for finance-ministry and development-bank teams that use CPAT results: what an elasticity does to a CPAT number, what the check found (levels mostly consistent; two sector values too high; publication bias for road fuels; the timing problem matters most), what this means for decisions (read near-term cuts and revenue with care; the Total Effective Carbon Price is robust), and what to do. One table (the comparison) and the timing figure, with the working paper's numbers. No equations.

## What the deck needs

Four slides in the old format. Rebuild at about ten on `make_deck.py`: title; why elasticities set the size of CPAT's answers; how CPAT turns prices into fuel use; what the evidence says (with the publication-bias warning); the comparison figure; the table of verdicts; timing; no cross-price terms and what that means; what errors do to cuts and to the Total Effective Carbon Price; what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.2, the new brief v1.0 and the ten-slide deck (31 Oct 2026). M2 remains partly open: the 2017 journal article's own tables could not be read, so the paper reports both CPAT's Table A and the authors' 2016 published summary and says which is which. The Galindo et al. (2015) figures are kept only as reported in CPAT's documentation, and labeled so.
