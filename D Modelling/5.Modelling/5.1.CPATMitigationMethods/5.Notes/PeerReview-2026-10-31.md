# Peer review of 5.1, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "The CPAT Mitigation Module: Methods in Brief" (v0.1, 29 Oct 2026, about 9,100 words, drafted by an agent and not reviewed before), written from a full reading and before any change to the master. Every equation was checked against the CPAT-AI equation list (`docs/CPAT_model_documentation_qmd/A_Equations.qmd`), the Excel-AI methods notes (`excel_ai/mitigation/docs/`), the bug and issue registers (`excel_ai/core/BUGS.md`, `ISSUES.md`) and the public CPAT documentation (`docs/CPAT_full_documentation_qmd/03_Mitigation.qmd`), all read from a fresh clone of the public repository `cpmodelai/cpat_ai` (commit 108f976, 31 Oct 2026). Every number in Tables 1 and 2 and the background-note example was recomputed (`2.Source/worked_examples.py`), and every derivation in Section 7 and Appendix B was redone by hand. The paper had no policy brief; this review also says what the brief and the deck need.

## Summary judgement

The paper does what it sets out to do: it states CPAT's mitigation module as a short chain of equations an economist can audit, and it is accurate on the core. Equations (1) to (11) and (16) to (18) match the rebuilt model's equation list, the demand arithmetic of Table 1 is right, and the velocity rule and its active-set correction are derived correctly. The question and answer are clear. The problems are of four kinds. (i) The paper has gone stale in three days: the CPAT-AI project has published a corrected edition of the documentation (31 Oct 2026) that fixes all twenty documentation errors the paper lists, the bug register has moved and grown (231 legacy bugs, not 228), and the public data edition now ships perturbed Egypt data. (ii) Several statements about defaults are wrong or attributed to the wrong source: the default cost of capital, the wind and solar scale-up limits, one legacy bug, and the asymmetric floor on existing taxes, which the paper omits. (iii) The case for the penalty-based rule rests on a claim that is not true: that the logit's curvature has no economic interpretation, while the velocity rule's parameter is in dollars. The CPAT logit is itself the solution of an entropy-penalized cost minimization whose penalty weight is in dollars per megawatt-hour, so the real difference between the two rules is the shape of the penalty, which is a cleaner and more defensible contribution. One stated drawback of the velocity rule is also wrong. (iv) The paper has no executive summary, no brief, and a four-slide deck on the old builder.

## Major points

**M1. Say which documentation is wrong, and that it has now been corrected.** The abstract and introduction say the documentation "disagrees with the model" and that "a reader of the documentation alone would get several equations wrong". That is true of the public site (cpmodel.github.io/cpat_public, the CPAT team's 2023 text, published 23 January 2024). But on 31 October 2026 the CPAT-AI project published a corrected edition of the same text (its "Methodology" tab, with a Corrections chapter): DOC1–DOC20 corrected, 14 in full and 6 in part, with a new section on the GDP feedback. The paper must distinguish the 2023 edition from the corrected edition and cite the corrections chapter, or it will tell readers to distrust a document that has been fixed. *Fix:* re-word the abstract, introduction and Appendix A; head Appendix A's DOC rows "2023 edition".

**M2. Bring the register facts up to date.** The register now counts 231 legacy bugs, 22 prototype bugs and 20 documentation bugs, and 135 issues (`BUGS.md`, `ISSUES.md`, generated for release 1.1.0; the workbook is at 1.2.1). The paper says 228 and cites "version 1.0.2" and file paths (`excel_ai/BUGS.md`, `docs/CPAT_coded_documentation_qmd/A_Equations`) that no longer exist. The data shipped publicly are now the "Public01" edition: public tables plus six proprietary tables replaced by Egypt values perturbed by up to 3 percent. *Fix:* 231; cite the register by its current location; say what the public data edition is (this also strengthens the confidentiality position: no real country result can be read from the public model).

**M3. The default cost of capital is stated wrongly.** The paper (and, through the cross-review, papers 5.0 and 6.3) says the default weighted average cost of capital is 7.5, 10, 12.5 and 15 percent by income group. That is the 2023 documentation's description of the "Income*" option (citing Steffen 2020 for 7.5 and 10 percent in OECD and non-OECD countries). The legacy workbook's setting, which Excel-AI follows (`methods/Power.md` §1.4: `D_WACCSource` = "Tech"), is a country cost of capital from the workbook's cost-of-capital table (7.5 percent where a country is missing) plus a technology premium by income group. *Fix:* state both, and which one the corrected model uses. **Coordinator:** 5.0 and 6.3 repeat the income-group rates as "the default"; they need the same correction.

**M4. The wind and solar scale-up default and the cap's base are misstated.** The paper says 2 percentage points of generation each "(2.5 for China)". The 2023 documentation says 2 percent, 2.5 for China; the legacy data the corrected model reads give 2 percent for most countries and 3 for China and India (`methods/Power.md`, Power rows 2349–2570). And the cap applies to total effective capacity (capacity times capacity factor), which is close to, but not the same as, generation. *Fix:* "2 percentage points a year each in most countries (country-specific; higher for China and India)", "of total effective capacity".

**M5. One legacy bug is described wrongly, and two are left without codes.** The paper says legacy "normalized the investment logit over non-permitted technologies as well, which leaked investment". Bug L6 is that legacy divided each cost by the cheapest technology, including technologies that may not be built; the corrected model divides by the cheapest permitted technology. The "one-third storage allocation applied twice" is L4. Appendix A lists both under a dash. *Fix:* describe L6 correctly and give L4 and L6 their codes; drop the unsourced "one of the ten power bugs fixed in version 0.1.06".

**M6. The pass-through rule omits a floor that changes its meaning.** Equation (3) is right as the floating rule, but the model then floors a positive existing tax at its last historical level (`A_Equations` A2.1). So with $pcc = 0$ the retail price stays at its last observed value only while world prices fall or where the fuel is subsidized; for a taxed fuel a rise in world prices passes through once the tax would fall below its last level. The paper says "with $pcc = 0$ the retail price before new policies stays at its last observed value". *Fix:* add the floor and its effect.

**M7. The case for the penalty rule must be stated against the right benchmark.** Section 7 argues that the logit's curvature $K$ "has no economic interpretation" and contrasts this with $\lambda$, "measured in dollars per megawatt-hour". But the CPAT logit share $e^{-K C_i/C_{min}}/\sum_j e^{-K C_j/C_{min}}$ is exactly the minimizer of expected cost plus an entropy penalty, $\sum_i C_i s_i + \tau\sum_i s_i \ln s_i$, with $\tau = C_{min}/K$, a weight in dollars per megawatt-hour (22.5 at the Table 2 costs with no carbon price). This is the representative-consumer reading of the logit [@AndersonDePalmaThisse1988]. The velocity rule is the same problem with a quadratic penalty in place of the entropy one, which is the Euclidean projection onto the simplex known in machine learning as sparsemax [@MartinsAstudillo2016]. So both rules have a dollar parameter; what differs is the penalty's shape. The entropy penalty's marginal cost becomes infinite as a share goes to zero, so no technology ever drops out; the quadratic penalty's marginal cost is finite, so uncompetitive technologies drop out exactly. And CPAT's $\tau$ moves with the cheapest cost, which is why an equal charge on every technology changes logit shares. This reading sharpens the contribution, removes an overclaim and tells a ministry what $K = 2$ means. *Fix:* rewrite the opening and closing paragraphs of Section 7 and add a paragraph and a derivation (Appendix B).

**M8. One stated drawback of the velocity rule is false; a real one is missing.** The paper says adding a technology, "even an irrelevant one that ends at zero", changes the others' shares through $N$ and $\bar C$. Under the exact active-set rule a technology that ends at zero has no effect: adding a sixth technology at 300 dollars per megawatt-hour leaves the Table 2 shares unchanged (recomputed). The real problem, shared with the logit, is duplication: splitting solar into two identical entries raises solar's combined share from 36.2 to 58.0 percent under the velocity rule and from 38.4 to 55.5 percent under the logit (recomputed). *Fix:* replace the claim with the duplication point; it argues for a fixed, stated technology list in either rule.

**M9. The executive summary, the brief and the deck are missing.** The paper has no non-technical summary; a ministry reader would not get past the price block. *Fix:* add an executive summary as chapter 1 (no equations, real numbers from Tables 1 and 2), write a brief (who should care: finance ministries and the World Bank and IMF teams who run CPAT; what the method means for how to read CPAT results; what to do) and a deck of about ten slides.

**M10. The strongest objection deserves a direct answer.** A sceptic will say that a reduced-form model with long-run elasticities that act in one year, own-price responses only, and a power sector whose investment response depends on two judgmental parameters (the logit curvature and the scale-up caps) should not be used for fiscal advice at all. The paper's answer is scattered across Sections 2, 9 and 10. *Fix:* state the objection once, in the limitations section, with the answer: the module is a first estimate for phased, medium-term reforms, its biases have known signs, the price response is checked against meta-analytic evidence (paper 5.4) and other models (paper 5.3), and the results that depend on judgment are named.

## Minor points

1. **Producer subsidy sign.** Equation (2) is right only with the convention that $ps$ is negative where a subsidy lowers the price ($ps = -PROS/D$, `A_Equations` A2.5; documentation error DOC9 is exactly this). State it.
2. **Equation (6) omits the policy-induced efficiency gain** $\alpha^+$ that enters beside $\alpha$ in the rebuilt model (M3.1). Add it, or say the equation shows the default with no additional efficiency policy.
3. **Equation (18):** the recycled revenue enters with the impulse measured from the carbon-pricing start year and with use shares in percent (M8.2). Note it.
4. **Rebound formula in Table 1's note** has the wrong sign: the share of the gross response offset is $-\epsilon_U\epsilon_F/(\epsilon_U+\epsilon_F)$. The numbers (17, 25, 15 and 20 percent) are right.
5. **"Most of the emission cut ... comes from coal"** does not follow from Table 1, which shows percentage changes in fuel use, not tonnes. Say that coal shows the largest percentage cut, and that the tonnes depend on each country's fuel mix.
6. **Table 1 elasticities** are the legacy defaults for low-, lower-middle- and upper-middle-income countries (all three take the same values; `data/Public01/tidy/xai_elasticities.csv`), not "lower- and upper-middle-income". Correct the note. The legacy ranges (usage −0.1 to −0.7, efficiency −0.2 to −1.2, income 0 to 1.2, efficiency trend 0.5 or 1 percent) were recomputed from the same file and are right.
7. **Rebound comparison.** The documentation compares CPAT's price-driven cross term with the 26–29 percent long-run rebound in @Dimitropoulos2018 (confirmed from the abstract: 74 studies, 1,120 estimates; short run 10–12 percent). The two measure different things: the meta-analysis measures the response of driving to fuel efficiency or fuel cost per kilometre. Say the comparison is loose.
8. **Labandeira et al. (2017).** "About 2,000 elasticities from 430 studies" is the CPAT documentation's description; the abstract gives no count, and the full text was not reachable. Attribute the count to the documentation.
9. **Gertler et al. (2016)** estimate how household purchases of energy-using assets in Mexico respond to income growth (abstract checked); the hump in CPAT's income adjustment, peaking at about \$3,000 per capita, is the CPAT team's inference from that work. Say so. Burke and Csereklyei's aggregate elasticity of 0.74 is confirmed in their text.
10. **Bauer, Hallegatte and McIsaac (2025)** is cited for "quadratic penalties on concentration and on speed of the same kind". The bibliographic record is right, but the article's text could not be reached and the World Bank working-paper version could not be read either, so the claim is unverified. Cut the sentence.
11. **Mercure (2012).** FTT:Power uses "a dynamic coupled set of logistic differential equations" (abstract). "Replicator-style" is a fair gloss, but say "logistic share equations", which is what the source says.
12. **Origins and users.** "Used ... for the Coalition of Finance Ministers for Climate Action" has no source in the cited documents. @black2023cpat say CPAT "evolved from an earlier IMF tool" and list IMF uses (Article IV reports, technical assistance, Climate Macro Assessment Programs, Resilience and Sustainability Trust support); the documentation names the IMF 2019 Board paper. Re-word to those.
13. **Fiscal multipliers.** The source is the World Bank's macro-fiscal model, MFMod [@Burns2019], or panel local-projection estimates (documentation chapter 4). Cite MFMod.
14. **The price dataset** is the IMF's fossil-fuel subsidy and price data [@BlackFFS2023]. Cite it once where the dataset first appears.
15. **Base year.** The 2023 edition states the price window inconsistently (DOC10); the corrected edition gives a 2022 base year and price data to 2022, while Excel-AI reads prices to 2024. "The published documentation uses a 2019 base year" is true only of the emissions calibration. Re-word.
16. **COVID adjustment years** are given as 2020–2021 in one place in the documentation and 2021–2022 in another. Say "2020–2021" with the citation, or drop the years.
17. **"About eighteen equations"** in the conclusion counts the four equations of the proposal. The module itself is fourteen numbered equations. Say so.
18. **Figure 1.** The caption says electricity prices feed back with a lag, but the figure shows only the GDP feedback; and the block numbers (emissions 4, revenues 5) do not match the calculation order of the rebuilt model (revenues before emissions) or the text. Redraw with the tariff lag and the right order.
19. **Logit gas share** at 75 dollars is 7.5 percent; the text says 8. Use 7.5.
20. **Cross-references and ids.** Hard-coded equation numbers "(1)"–"(18)" and "Table 1"/"Figure 1" in captions will clash with the cross-reference filter. Use `{#eq-}`, `{#tbl-}`, `{#fig-}` labels and refer to sections by name. Appendices need `{#sec-}` ids.
21. **Acronyms.** ETS, VAT, NDC, LULUCF, IEA, IIASA, GAINS, WACC: define once; spell out those used fewer than five times.
22. **First italic line** should link to the brief: `[Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 5.1](brief:)`.
23. **README** says "No unverified markers remain" but the `.bib` notes say only "Checked: Crossref" for most entries: metadata, not content. Record what was read in each note.

## Closest prior work and the contribution

The closest prior descriptions are @black2023cpat (the model for an IMF audience, 74 pages), the CPAT technical notes [@cpat2022technotes, about 400 pages] and the documentation itself. The paper's first contribution, a complete statement of the module on a few pages with the corrected equations, is real and correctly stated against these. The second, the corrections, is now shared with the corrected edition of the documentation; the paper should say it reports them and was written alongside them. The third, the penalty rule, should be stated against discrete-choice theory (the logit as an entropy-penalized optimum) and against the projection literature (sparsemax), not as a new idea in isolation; the novelty is its use as an investment-allocation rule in a policy model, the active-set correction and the acceleration and combined variants.

## What the brief and the deck need

**Brief (new, v1.0).** About 2,000 words for a finance-ministry reader and for the teams who run CPAT for them: what CPAT computes in plain words, why a \$50 carbon price bites on coal and barely on road fuel (Table 1), why the power results depend on two judgment calls (the logit and the scale-up caps) and what that means for reading them, what the rebuild found and fixed, and what to do (use it for phased reforms; read prices before quantities; replace defaults with country evidence; test the penalty rule behind a switch; fund partial adjustment and fuel switching). No equations. One table (Table 1's numbers) and one figure (investment shares, Figure 2's numbers).

**Deck.** The current deck is four slides on the old spec. Target ten on the new builder: title; the problem in numbers; the chain (Figure 1); prices; demand and Table 1; power; the logit and the penalty rule (Figure 2); what the rebuild fixed; limits and the strongest objection; what to do.

## Disposition

All major points (M1–M10) and minor points 1–23 are addressed in working paper v0.2 and the new brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026), except that M3's correction is made only in 5.1: papers 5.0 and 6.3 repeat the income-group cost of capital as the default and need the same fix by the coordinator. Bauer et al. (2025) is cut; nothing else is left unverified.
