# Peer review of 7.2, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of *A Global Fiscal Indicators Dashboard for Climate-Fiscal Analysis*, working paper v0.1 (29 Oct 2026, about 4,000 words of body), written from a full reading before the v0.2 revision and the first policy brief (v1.0). The build pipeline and the panel sit in the private Climate Fiscal AI Tools repository, which is not available to this review; every number in Table 2 was therefore rechecked against the publishers' own public releases instead (script and output: `2.Source/check_public_sources.py`, `check_public_sources_output.txt`): IMF World Economic Outlook and Fiscal Monitor (October 2026, through the IMF DataMapper API), IMF WoRLD (release of 18 February 2026, through the IMF SDMX API), the IMF's fossil fuel subsidy update of December 2025 (Annex III) and Ember's yearly electricity data. Every external source was checked against the source itself (see `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`).

## Summary judgement

The paper has a clear question (how can an analyst get a comparable, sourced first view of a country's climate-fiscal position in minutes, in a form someone else can rebuild?) and a clear answer (a panel of 80 public indicators for 217 economies, scored 1–10 within blocks that each answer one question, built by a script that never imputes and records the year and source of every value). The build rules in the pipeline section are the paper's real contribution and are well described. The numbers in Table 2 largely survive an independent check: tax revenue, net interest to revenue, the excise share and the subsidy ratios reproduce from the publishers' files, and every score recomputes from equation (2) and the panel's percentiles.

Five things keep it below the standard. (i) The headline fiscal indicator is mislabeled: what the paper calls "interest-to-revenue" is *net* interest over revenue, and the stated filter drops the countries in the strongest position. (ii) Two numbers rest on the wrong release or a denominator the paper does not explain: the 2024 subsidy values come from the IMF's 2025 update, not the 2023 update cited, and they differ from the IMF's own published shares of GDP; the panel's renewable shares differ from Ember's observed shares by 5–19 points. (iii) The subtitle promises "a build anyone can rerun", but the build is in a private repository and two blocks are read from a non-public compilation. (iv) The contribution is stated against three databases but not against the closest prior work: the IMF's Climate Change Indicators Dashboard, the cross-country fiscal space database as a *framework*, and the composite-indicator literature that bears directly on the paper's main design choice (no overall index). (v) There is no executive summary, no related-literature section, no strongest objection, and no brief.

## Major points

**M1. "Interest-to-revenue" is net interest, and the filter removes net creditors.** Equation (1) divides the primary balance less the overall balance by revenue. In the WEO and Fiscal Monitor that difference is net interest payable (interest paid less interest received), not interest paid. The check confirms the panel's values exactly from the October 2026 Fiscal Monitor: Colombia 13.5, Indonesia 15.1, Morocco 7.9, South Africa 18.9 percent. Two consequences. First, the label overstates what is measured for countries with large interest receipts and understates the gross burden: South Africa's interest paid is 5.3 percent of GDP in the IMF's Public Finances in Modern History database, close to its net figure, but this is not true everywhere. Second, the stated rule "kept only where ... the result lies between 0 and 100 percent" drops every country whose net interest is negative, which are those that receive more interest than they pay, the strongest fiscal positions. Coverage falls from 191 economies (gross debt) to 184. Dropped countries become "missing" rather than scoring 10. *Fix:* call the indicator "net interest, % of revenue" throughout; say the filter is a known weakness and that negative values should be kept and clipped to the top score (they fall below the 5th percentile); add it to the limits.

**M2. The subsidy values are from the IMF's 2025 update, and their ratios to GDP are not the IMF's.** The paper cites Black et al. (2023), the 2023 update, whose latest actual year is 2022, for "2024 values". The 2024 values are in the December 2025 update (Black, Celniak, Garcia Huitron, Parry, Schulz Antipa and Vernon-Lin, IMF WP/25/270), which covers 170 countries and is also built with CPAT. The panel's ratios equal the 2025 update's dollar estimates divided by WEO GDP for 2024 (Indonesia 42 and 128 billion dollars over 1,396 billion: 3.0 and 9.2 percent; South Africa 52 over 401: 13.0), whereas the IMF's own Annex III gives 2.7 and 8.5 percent for Indonesia, 11.9 for South Africa and 1.8 and 4.8 for Morocco. The difference is in the GDP denominator; the dollars agree. *Fix:* cite the 2025 update; state in Table 2's note that the dashboard's ratios use WEO GDP and give the IMF's own ratios for comparison; keep one set of numbers (the dashboard's) across the papers, the brief and the deck. Also: the panel holds subsidy values for 185 economies against 170 in the 2025 update; the build's source file should be checked (see "Still open").

**M3. The panel's renewable shares disagree with Ember.** The paper says Ember supplies generation by source, and its Limits section says the renewable share in Context 3 comes from the IMF's CPAT-based dataset. The panel's 2024 renewable shares (Colombia 79.9, Indonesia 28.0, Morocco 29.5, South Africa 7.0 percent; from `illustrative_comparison_output.txt`) differ from Ember's observed 2024 shares (61.4, 18.1, 24.4, 12.5 percent) by 5 to 19 points, in both directions. The paper does not quote these values, but its deck did not either and a reader of the profile would. *Fix:* in the limits, give this as the concrete example of a modeled series diverging from an observed one, and recommend that the dashboard read the renewable share from Ember (observed) and keep the CPAT series only where model consistency requires it, labeled as modeled.

**M4. The excise row measures the denominator as much as the excise.** The excise share divides WoRLD excises (GFS 1142) by WoRLD total revenue (GFS 1, which includes social contributions and grants). Recomputed from WoRLD: Colombia 3.0 percent (2023), Indonesia 8.6, Morocco 6.9, South Africa 7.8 (2023), all as in Table 2. But as a share of GDP, Colombia's excises (1.06 percent in 2023) are the same size as Indonesia's (1.11 percent in 2024); Colombia's share of revenue is lowest because its revenue (34.7 percent of GDP in 2023, including 5.7 percent of social contributions) is high. The paper's reading ("the lowest excise share of revenue ... suggests room in excise policy") is therefore not supported by the row it rests on. Paper 7.1 reports excise revenue as a share of GDP (Sierra Leone 1.3, Liberia 0.4, African average 2.4), so the two papers measure the same thing differently. A further caveat: WoRLD's coverage is central government for Colombia and Morocco (sector code S1311) and general government for Indonesia and South Africa (S13). *Fix:* add excise revenue as a share of GDP to Table 2 (computed from WoRLD, labeled as not a dashboard indicator); re-word Colombia's reading; recommend that the dashboard carry excise revenue in percent of GDP, the measure 7.1 uses; note the coverage difference.

**M5. The subtitle overclaims, and Table 1 hides the compilation.** "A Build Anyone Can Rerun" is not true today: the pipeline is in a private repository, and part of Appendix C and all of Appendix E are read from a World Bank climate fiscal risk compilation (the paper says so only in the Limits). Table 1 lists the public sources of those appendices as if they were read directly, and lists a "climate in budgeting" indicator whose source is not named anywhere. *Fix:* change the subtitle (the title is unchanged, so no CFPB key is affected); in the text, say what is public (every input) and what is not (the build, two appendices' route to their data); in Table 1, mark the two appendices as read through the compilation in version 1.3 and drop the unsourced indicator from the examples.

**M6. The contribution needs to be stated against the closest prior work.** Three bodies of work are missing.

- *Cross-country climate and fiscal dashboards.* The IMF's Climate Change Indicators Dashboard (launched April 2021) already brings together climate indicators, including government policy indicators, on one platform. The fiscal space database of Kose et al. (2022) is itself a framework of 28 indicators in four groups for up to 200 countries. The paper should say what neither does: put fiscal space, delivery capacity, the cost of capital and the energy-fiscal position on one page, with the year and file of every value, and feed the same panel to a model (paper 6.5).
- *Composite indicators.* The OECD/JRC *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators* (2008) is the standard reference on normalization, weighting and aggregation. It warns that composite indicators "may disguise serious failings in some dimensions" and that min-max normalization is distorted by outliers. Both points support the paper's two main design choices (no overall index; clipping at the 5th and 95th percentiles). The paper makes these choices without citing the reason they are standard.
- *What the indicators mean.* Low tax revenue as a problem has a literature: Gaspar, Jaramillo and Wingender (2016) estimate a tipping point at about 12¾ percent of GDP, below which growth is slower; Indonesia (10.1 percent) is below it. The efficiency of public investment, the delivery block's subject, has the IMF's 2015 estimate of an average efficiency gap of about 30 percent. The cost-of-capital block has Ameli et al. (2021), who show that regional differences in the cost of capital change cost-optimal decarbonization paths in developing economies. Each turns an indicator into a reason to look at it.

*Fix:* add a related-work section organized by idea; restate the contribution as (a) the question-based block structure, (b) the build rules that make every value traceable and stop silent error, (c) the triage reading and its illustration, and (d) the shared data layer with the scenario tool.

**M7. The paper refuses an overall index but computes block averages with equal weights.** "There are no defensible weights" applies inside a block too: the fiscal block averages gross debt, net interest over revenue and net interest over GDP, which share a numerator and move together, so the debt-service dimension counts two or three times. The block averages are also based on however many indicators have data. *Fix:* state that block averages are an equal-weighted reading aid, not a verdict; that correlated indicators count more than once; and that the profile should be read for its lowest indicator and its count of missing values, not its average. State this as the strongest objection to the scoring and answer it.

**M8. Some readings of Table 2 are loose.** (a) South Africa's "fiscal block among the weakest here": on the three scored fiscal indicators in the table, Indonesia's average (5.2) is below South Africa's (5.7). South Africa has the highest debt and the highest net interest burden of the four; say that. (b) Indonesia's debt score of 8.0 is "one of the better results among the four": it is the best. (c) The implicit subsidies of South Africa are 12.9 percent of GDP on the dashboard's denominator and 11.9 on the IMF's (M2). (d) Morocco's gross debt is 67.7 percent of GDP in the panel (WEO downloaded in August 2026); the October 2026 WEO gives 67.0, which would move its score from 5.9 to 6.0. Report the panel's value and note the revision. (e) A reading such as "points straight at subsidy reform" is a recommendation the paper then disclaims ("none of the four readings is a recommendation"); keep the language of "which question to ask next".

**M9. Two claims need a source or a softer statement.** "IMF Public Investment Management Assessment scores are excluded because their publication is consent-based and the published subsample leans toward better performers": the first half is right (the IMF publishes a PIMA report only with the authorities' permission; checked on the IMF's PIMA process page); the second half is not shown anywhere. State it as the author's concern that the published subsample may not be representative. "The Power Sector Reform Tracker ... covers only non-OECD countries": its source (Urpelainen and Yang 2019) covers 142 developing countries, 1982–2013; say that.

**M10. No executive summary, no literature section, no brief.** *Fix:* add an executive summary as chapter 1, a related-work section, and the policy brief (v1.0).

## Minor points

1. Section references are typed by hand ("Section 5"), and equations are numbered by hand; use `@sec-` and `{#eq-}` labels so Word and web number them.
2. The WoRLD title is "World Revenue Longitudinal Database" in the IMF's own metadata, not "Data".
3. The IMF's Investment and Capital Stock Dataset release used runs to 2019 and is the May 2021 version; the bib says 2025.
4. RISE: the edition labeled 2024 ("RISE 2024: The Time Is Now") was published in 2025.
5. The IRENA source is the data appendix to *Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2021* (2022), giving real after-tax WACC assumptions for 2021; say "real after-tax".
6. Black et al. (2023): the fourth author is Nate Vernon-Lin.
7. `2.Source/illustrative_comparison.py` calls the paper "5.3" in its docstring and prints gasoline prices to one decimal, so the table's two-decimal prices cannot be read from its output.
8. "Implicit subsidies ... the IMF's measure of unpriced environmental costs and forgone consumption taxes": right (the IMF says consumption taxes matter "less importantly"); keep, and say that the IMF itself suggests "underpriced environmental costs" as an alternative name.
9. The carbon-pricing gap should name the three existing measures: explicit prices (World Bank Carbon Pricing Dashboard), the OECD's effective carbon rates, and the World Bank's total carbon price (Agnolucci et al. 2024), and then the Total Effective Carbon Price (paper 6.1), using 6.1's term exactly.
10. Tax revenue in WoRLD (GFS 11, "Taxes") excludes social contributions; say so, because Colombia's contributions add 5.7 percent of GDP.
11. The roadmap paragraph in the introduction can go.
12. "Climate Fiscal AI Tools": keep the name, but say once that it is not yet public.

## The strongest objection

A percentile score ranks a country within a sample that mixes small islands, oil exporters and advanced economies, at different vintages, so a "Bad" can mean "unusual" rather than "a problem", and block averages double-count. The answer, which the revised paper should give: the score is a reading aid that sits next to the value, the year and the source; the triage reading uses the lowest indicator and the count of missing values; and the profile tells the analyst which question to ask, not what to recommend. A peer-group option (scoring within income group or region) would answer the heterogeneity half of the objection and is a natural next step.

## What the brief needs

A policy brief for finance ministries, development-bank country teams and anyone preparing climate-fiscal advice: why a first view matters (advice goes wrong when the binding constraint is misread), what the dashboard gives (twelve questions, one page, every value traceable), what the four-country comparison shows (a revenue problem against a debt-service problem, with different energy-fiscal levers), what to watch (vintages, modeled series, denominators), and what to do (use the profile to choose the next question; fill the carbon-pricing gap; publish the build). About 2,000 words, bold lead sentences, Table 2 with the same numbers, one figure, links into the working paper's sections, no equations.

## What the deck needs

The old deck has four slides on the old builder. Target about ten on `make_deck.py`: title and message; the problem (a scramble across a dozen databases); twelve questions in four assessment and three context blocks; the build rules; the scoring and why there is no index; the four-country comparison (figure); the table of selected indicators; what the numbers do and do not say (net interest, denominators, modeled series); the gaps (carbon pricing first); what to do.

## Still open after this review

- The panel's subsidy coverage (185 economies) exceeds the 170 countries of the IMF's 2025 update; the build's source file should be checked in the private repository. The four countries' values match the 2025 update's dollar estimates.
- Gasoline retail prices (Table 2) could not be read from the IMF's dataset in this session (the `.xlsb` link redirected); the 2025 update confirms qualitatively that Indonesia's gasoline price is below supply cost (about 80–90 US cents per liter).
- Python–R parity for the 27 indicators added in October 2026 is unverified (as v0.1 says).

## Disposition

All major points (M1–M10) and minor points (1–12) are addressed in working paper v0.2 and brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). The subtitle is changed (title unchanged). Table 2 keeps the dashboard's values, adds excise revenue as a share of GDP and the IMF's own subsidy ratios in its note, and the indicator is renamed "net interest, % of revenue". The renewable-share discrepancy, the net-creditor filter and the excise denominator are reported in the paper as findings for the dashboard's next version; the tool itself was not changed (it is outside this folder).
