# Peer review of 7.1, working paper v0.3 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "The Excise-Fiscal Diagnostic: Reading an Excise System Against Its Externalities" (working paper v0.3, 30 Oct 2026; about 6,050 words of body; a four-slide deck; no policy brief), written from a full reading before any change to the master. Every number in Sections 3, 6 and 8 was recomputed from `2.Source/stylized_example.py`; every number in Section 7 and Table 5 was checked against `2.Source/country_metrics_SLE_LBR.csv` (the extract from the diagnostic's workbook; the workbook itself sits in a private repository not present in this session); every cited claim was checked against the source (see `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`). The folder's other material was read for confidentiality: the V06 briefing, the "Proposal" deck and the background notes in `3.RelatedPiecesAndLinks/`.

## Summary judgement

The paper describes a useful product and describes it well: a fixed-format report that maps an excise schedule onto 30 standard lines, scales each line three ways and sets fuel lines against one fixed set of damage values. The method sections (3–5) are clear and the stylized arithmetic is right. But the paper's headline comparison is wrong, two of its valuation choices are stated against the wrong source, the "largest unpriced externality" finding overreaches, several reference values are unsourced, one paragraph draws on a World Bank "draft for discussion", and the paper never states the strongest objection to what it proposes. The contribution against the closest prior work (the IMF's efficient-price estimates, WHO's health-tax benchmarks, the excise-design literature) also needs to be stated more sharply. The paper lacks an executive summary and a policy brief; the deck has four slides.

## Major points

**M1. The African benchmark of 2.4 percent of GDP is unsourced and does not match the public data.** The abstract, introduction, Table 5 and deck compare Sierra Leone (1.25–1.28 percent of GDP) and Liberia (0.37 percent) with "an African average of 2.4" (and "low income 2.2", 2021). No source is given; the value comes from the diagnostic's specification. The public series is *Revenue Statistics in Africa 2025* (OECD/AUC/ATAF). Recomputed from the OECD Data Explorer (category 5121, excises; 37 countries with GDP data; `2.Source/africa_excise_benchmark.py`): the unweighted mean is 1.43 percent of GDP in 2021, 1.34 in 2022 and 1.32 in 2023 (median 1.03 in 2023). The 2.4 figure is close to nothing in that series; the broader category 5120 (specific goods and services, which includes customs duties) averages 3.2. *Fix:* use the verified 2023 mean (1.3 percent) and say what it covers. The story changes: Sierra Leone's reported excise is about the African mean, not half of it.

**M2. Liberia's figure compares a narrow legal definition with a broad international one.** The paper's own rule (Section 4.2) counts levies that "work as excises in substance" (road fund, regulator and price levies) in the fuel tax rate. The OECD classification counts them as excises too: in Liberia's OECD data, petroleum taxes and the Road Fund fee are in category 5121 (0.32 and 0.43 percent of GDP in 2023, total excise 0.99). Yet the abstract and headline compare Liberia's legal excise only (0.37 percent) with an average that includes such levies. On a consistent definition Liberia raised 1.25 percent of GDP in FY2024 (the diagnostic's own "excise plus excise-like levies" figure), about the African mean. *Fix:* lead with the consistent figure; report 0.37 as the legal excise and say why the gap matters (fuel sits outside the excise law, so the excise law alone describes a third of the system).

**M3. Sierra Leone's collected figure cannot be reconciled with the public series.** The diagnostic reports excise collected of 1.25–1.28 percent of GDP in 2024–25, 1.12–1.15 from fuel. The OECD series records 0.57 percent in 2023 (petroleum 0.34, road user charges 0.15, other 0.08) and 0.93 percent in 2021. Rate rises between 2023 and 2025, a GDP revision or a classification difference could explain part of the gap; the workbook is not available in this session, so the cause could not be traced. *Unresolved.* *Fix:* report the OECD figure beside the diagnostic's, say that the two differ by a factor of about two and that the reconciliation of collections (one of the diagnostic's own validation steps) is open, and stop calling 1.3 percent a finding.

**M4. The "largest unpriced externality" claim overreaches, and a gap cannot be decomposed as stated.** Section 4.2 says the gap "is decomposed by component so the report can name the largest unpriced externality"; Section 6 says "the largest unpriced externality is local air pollution from diesel, not carbon". The gap is the corrective rate less the current tax; the current tax is not assigned to components, so the gap has no component decomposition without an allocation rule. What can be decomposed is the corrective rate. Recomputed shares of the diesel corrective rate at central values: carbon 31 percent, air pollution 35 percent, distance-related costs after the β adjustment 35 percent (accidents 23). Air pollution leads carbon by 0.02 dollars per liter on stylized inputs: too close to call "largest". The robust finding is that two-thirds of the corrective rate is local (air and road), which supports framing a diesel reform as health and road safety. *Fix:* decompose the corrective rate, not the gap; restate the finding as "two-thirds local".

**M5. The carbon value is a policy floor, not a damage value, and the paper does not say so.** Table 1 sets the carbon price at USD 50/tCO₂ on the basis of the IMF staff proposal for an international carbon price floor among large emitters (Parry, Black and Roaf 2021: USD 75, 50 and 25 in 2030 for advanced, high-income emerging and low-income emerging economies). That is a negotiating floor for six large emitters, not an estimate of damage, which a corrective benchmark requires. The leading recent damage estimate is USD 185/tCO₂ (2020 dollars; 5–95 percent range 44–413) at a 2 percent discount rate (Rennert et al. 2022). At USD 185 the carbon component alone is USD 0.42 per liter of petrol and 0.50 per liter of diesel, more than either country's petrol tax. The choice moves the carbon part of every fuel result by a factor of almost four. *Fix:* keep USD 50 as the default, describe it as a policy-anchored value well below damage estimates, and report the social-cost case.

**M6. The value of a statistical life is described against the wrong recommendation.** The paper says the default income elasticity is 1.0 and that 1.5 is "the upper end of the range recommended for transfers to low- and middle-income countries" (Robinson, Hammitt and O'Keeffe 2019). Their recommended default is a United States base (160 times income per head) with an elasticity of 1.5 and a floor of 20 times income; an OECD base of 100 times income with an elasticity of 1.0, the diagnostic's default, is one of their two sensitivity cases. The difference is large: at an income of USD 800 per head the OECD-based option gives USD 80,000 and the recommended default USD 16,000 (the floor binds), a factor of five (`stylized_example.py`). Since air pollution and accidents make up most of the diesel benchmark, this is the parameter that moves results most. *Fix:* state the source correctly and report both.

**M7. The paper draws on a World Bank "draft for discussion".** Section 8.3's "companion screen" with "six drivers (fiscal pressure, salience, vested interests, distributive design, institutional quality and energy structure)" comes from `3.RelatedPiecesAndLinks/Background-PoliticalEconomyMetric.qmd`, which is headed "World Bank · Draft for discussion · May 2026". Under the brief's confidentiality rules it cannot be used. *Fix:* remove the six-driver screen; cite published work on reform sequencing instead or say nothing.

**M8. Several reference values in Table 5 are unsourced.** "OECD 0.55 (30–50%)" for petrol, "OECD 15–40" for beer and "OECD 25–50" for spirits per liter of pure alcohol, "UK 0.23–0.30" for soft drinks, and the "low income 2.2" are from the diagnostic's specification and carry no public source. The UK rates have also changed: the Soft Drinks Industry Levy is GBP 0.208 and 0.278 per liter from 1 April 2026 (HMRC). *Fix:* replace each with a verified reference or drop it. Petrol is compared with the spine's own carbon component (computed), alcohol is tested for consistency per liter of pure alcohol (as Section 4.4 says it should be), soft drinks with the current UK rates in pounds, cigarettes with WHO's 70 percent target (verified).

**M9. The tobacco benchmark is attributed loosely.** "WHO recommends that excise make up at least 70 percent of the retail price ... with specific or mixed structures and automatic adjustment for inflation." The 2021 WHO technical manual does say "a recommended target ... is to have an excise tax that represents at least 70% of the retail price", and recommends specific or mixed structures and inflation adjustment; but WHO's monitoring benchmark (MPOWER "R") is total indirect tax of at least 75 percent of the price of the most-sold brand. *Fix:* cite both; the diagnostic uses the excise target because it isolates the excise.

**M10. The strongest objection is missing.** There are two. First, a corrective benchmark is not the optimal fuel tax where fuel taxes are also a main revenue source: in the optimal-tax literature the efficient rate adds a revenue (Ramsey) component to the corrective one, and in countries with weak income-tax bases that component can be large (Parry and Small 2005; Cnossen 2005). So "the gap" is a lower bound on a defensible rate, not a target. Second, the AI-assisted process has a measured error rate: the line-by-line review corrected 16 of 49 entries in Sierra Leone and 14 in Liberia and could not verify 4 and 2. That is consistent with published evidence that general-purpose language models often state legal facts wrongly (Dahl et al. 2024). The paper presents the error rate as an argument for review but not as an objection to the process. *Fix:* add a section stating and answering both.

**M11. The contribution is not stated against the closest prior work.** The closest prior work is the IMF's efficient-price and subsidy estimates (Parry et al. 2014; Black et al. 2023), which compute corrective fuel taxes for 170 countries; WHO's tax manuals and global reports for tobacco and alcohol; and the excise-design literature (Cnossen 2005; Chaloupka, Powell and Warner 2019). The paper should say what the diagnostic adds beyond them: a line-level reading of the legal schedule (not a fuel-price comparison), one standard schedule across all excise categories, a common valuation spine for the fuel lines, data-quality scores that travel with each number, and a checked process. It should also cite the economics of the alcohol consistency test: taxing by alcohol content is WHO's preferred design (WHO 2023), but differentiated rates can be optimal when heavy drinkers favor particular products (Griffith, O'Connell and Smith 2019).

## Minor points

1. **CO₂ factor.** "About 2.31 kg per liter of petrol ... on IPCC default factors": IPCC 2006 defaults (69,300 kg/TJ; 44.3 TJ/Gg) give 2.29 kg per liter at a density of 0.745 kg/l (2.27–2.30 at 0.74–0.75). The carbon component at USD 50 becomes USD 0.11 per liter of petrol, not 0.12; diesel (2.68 kg, USD 0.13) is unchanged. Petrol's corrective rate is USD 0.30 (was 0.31), naive sum 0.45 (0.46), range 0.13–0.67 (0.13–0.68); the gap at central values is zero.
2. **Static gain.** "The static gain is USD 500 million, 1.27 percent of GDP": the gain is USD 508 million; 500 million would be 1.25 percent. Report USD 510 million.
3. **Elasticity default.** The −0.3 short-run fuel elasticity is above the global meta-analytic averages in the authors' own published summary (−0.23 gasoline, −0.20 diesel; Labandeira, Labeaga and López 2016), and far above the publication-bias-corrected −0.09 for gasoline (Havranek, Irsova and Janda 2012; see paper 5.4). Report the diesel revenue at −0.1 as well (1.22 percent of GDP against 1.11).
4. **Petrol logic.** "Petrol sits at its central corrective rate only because its 2020 rate ... has eroded" reads backwards. Say: petrol was taxed 40 percent above its corrective rate in 2020 and inflation has brought it down to it; unindexed, it will fall below.
5. **Liberia's dollar rates do erode**, with US inflation; "keep their dollar value" is true only in nominal terms.
6. **Beer in Sierra Leone.** Table 5 reports 2.3–9.1 dollars per liter of pure alcohol "by regime"; imported beer pays 13.6. Add it.
7. **Implied revenue "within a factor of perhaps 1.5"** has no basis. Replace with the real range the diagnostic reports (Sierra Leone tobacco: 0.09–0.17 percent of GDP depending on cigarettes a day).
8. **"National practice where one appraisal guide governs every department's values"** is unsourced; cut.
9. **Table 1, accident costs and air pollution:** "WHO road safety estimates" uncited (cite WHO 2023); the European handbook's first author is Schroten, not van Essen.
10. **Black et al. (2023):** the fourth author is listed by Crossref as Vernon-Lin (published name Nate Vernon); keep the published name and add the note.
11. **The feebate sentence** cites Brief 1.2 for a vehicle feebate; 1.2 covers power and industry. Cite Durrmeyer (2022) on France's vehicle feebate and Brief 1.2 for the instrument in general.
12. **JEL H71** (state and local taxation) is wrong; use H20.
13. **Old numbering** in the scripts: `stylized_example.py` says "paper (5.1, v0.2)", `country_metrics.py` "Paper 5.1", `make_figure_countries.py` "paper 5.1". Fix to 7.1.
14. **Section ids.** Subsections lack `{#sec-...}` ids the brief can link to; there is no executive summary; the first italic line does not link to a brief.
15. **"Health" category includes gambling.** Gambling is not a health excise in WHO's sense; keep it in the typology but call the category "health and gambling".
16. **Roadmap paragraph.** The section-by-section roadmap (Section 1, paragraph 4) is boilerplate; cut to one sentence.

## What the brief needs

There is no brief. Write Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 7.1 for finance ministries and the development-bank and IMF teams that advise them: why an excise schedule needs reading whole; what the diagnostic does (in plain words, no equations); what the two country runs found, on the corrected comparisons; why the stylized fuel benchmark says diesel, not petrol, and local damage, not carbon; why indexation is the cheapest reform; what to do. One table (Sierra Leone and Liberia) and one figure (the stylized corrective benchmark), the working paper's numbers. Title states the finding.

## What the deck needs

Four slides in the old format, with the wrong African benchmark. Rebuild at about ten on `make_deck.py`: title; the problem; what the diagnostic does; three measures; the spine and the stylized fuel result; what the spine depends on (carbon and VSL); the two countries (table); what they show (collection, not legislation); the strongest objection (AI error rate and the corrective-versus-optimal point); what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.4, the new brief v1.0 and the ten-slide deck (31 Oct 2026). M3 remains open: the diagnostic's 2024–25 Sierra Leone collections cannot be reconciled with the OECD's 2023 figure without the workbook, so both are reported and the difference is stated as an open item. The diagnostic's own reference values that could not be sourced (M8) were replaced or dropped; the VSL, carbon and benchmark choices are now stated against the right sources.
