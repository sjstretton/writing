# Peer review of 6.4, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "Activity Levels Across CPAT, SISEPUEDE, CBAM and FTT" (v0.1, 29 Oct 2026, about 4,900 words of body, condensed by an agent from the author's design note and not reviewed before), written from a full reading and before any change to the master. Every claim about the four models was checked against their public code and documentation, read from fresh clones: CPAT-AI (`cpmodelai/cpat_ai`, commit 108f976, 31 Oct 2026: `docs/CPAT_model_documentation_qmd/03_Energy_demand.qmd`, `04_Power.qmd`, `05_Road_transport.qmd`, `A_Equations.qmd`; `data/Public01/tidy/xai_elasticities.csv`; `excel_ai/core/ISSUES.md`), SISEPUEDE (`jcsyme/sisepuede`, commit 9bf976c, 23 Sep 2026: `docs/source/`, `sisepuede/models/energy_consumption.py`, `ippu.py`, `utilities/_toolbox.py`, `ref/examples/input_data_frame.csv`) and FTT (`cpmodel/FTT_StandAlone`, commit 896a3ce, 28 Oct 2026: `README.md`, `settings.ini`, `docs/source/mathematics.rst`, `docs/Update manuals/`, `Utilities/titles/VariableListing.csv`, `Inputs/S0/`, `ftt_source/Freight/ftt_fr_main.py`, `ftt_source/sector_coupling/electricity_demand.py`). Every number in Tables 2 and 3 and Proposition 3 was recomputed (`2.Source/activity_layer.py`). The CBAM Policy Tool's equation was checked against paper 6.2 as it stands in the repository. The paper had no executive summary, no brief and a three-slide deck on the old builder.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a useful, practical question and its answer is right in outline: four models that a finance ministry might link build activity in four incompatible ways, and one constant-elasticity driver layer with a sector concordance and a rule for where the price response goes is enough to put them on one baseline. The concordance (Table 1) is accurate on the structure of all four models: CPAT's fuel equation and power demand, SISEPUEDE's compounding growth factors and its lack of any price response, and FTT's demand inputs all check out against the code. But the paper is weaker than it looks on the numbers it attaches to the models and on the evidence. (i) Several parameter values are attributed to the wrong source or cannot be traced to a public one: CPAT's electricity income elasticity, the km-per-car elasticity and the van-and-truck price response are not CPAT's values; the car-ownership curve is CPAT's Gompertz, not Dargay, Gately and Sommer's; and the SISEPUEDE "medians of calibrated country inputs" come from data that are not public. (ii) The passenger-travel elasticity of 1.24 conflicts with the best evidence, and the layer should correct it as it corrects the others. (iii) Proposition 3 is illustrated in a way that understates it, and the paper's suggested remedy (a falling car elasticity) does not work. (iv) One worked-example paragraph (steel against cement) states a conclusion that the paper's own elasticities contradict. (v) The contribution is not stated against the closest prior work: the common-driver approach of the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways and the soft-linking literature. None of these overturns the method; all change numbers or wording.

## Major points

**M1. CPAT's electricity income elasticity is wrong.** The paper says "0.75 base; about 0.83 at \$3,000 per person, 0.43 above \$22,000". The legacy default the corrected model reads is 0.8 for electricity in homes and in industry, for every income group (`xai_elasticities.csv`: `inc|ecy|res`, `inc|ecy|ind`). The income-level factor is a quartic in log GDP per person divided by 0.74, capped at log 10 (about \$22,000) (`03_Energy_demand.qmd`, constants IncfA–IncfNorm). Recomputed: the factor is 1.15 at \$3,000, peaks at 1.17 near \$2,250 and is 0.605 at the cap. So the elasticity is 0.92 at \$3,000 and 0.48 above \$22,000. *Fix:* Table 2 and Table 3 (electricity grows 31 percent in ten years, not 28; 33 percent with a population exponent of 1).

**M2. Two fleet-model values are misattributed, and the fleet model is misdescribed.** (a) "Km per car: 0.05, CPAT fleet model." The fleet model uses an income elasticity of distance of 0.1 for passenger vehicles (legacy value); 0.05 is the methodology note's alternative, stored but not used (`05_Road_transport.qmd`, parameter tables), and the elasticity applies to total GDP growth. (b) "Vans and trucks −0.2, CPAT fleet model; de Jong." The fleet model applies the road-diesel usage elasticity (−0.3, legacy) to freight distance; −0.2 is the methodology note's unused alternative and, separately, the best-guess fuel-price elasticity of road freight vehicle-km in @deJong2010. (c) The fleet model is called "newer"; it is legacy CPAT's "technoeconomic" road model, carried into the rebuild and switched on per scenario (`TrModel`); the default road model is the fuel equation. *Fix:* km per car 0.1 (CPAT fleet model, legacy value), vans and trucks −0.2 from @deJong2010 only, and describe the fleet model correctly.

**M3. The car-ownership curve is CPAT's, not Dargay, Gately and Sommer's.** The paper attributes "an S-shaped curve whose elasticity peaks near 2.1 at about 90 vehicles per 1,000 people" to @DargayGatelySommer2007. Those numbers are what CPAT's Gompertz constants give ($a = -5.897$, $b = -0.23$ per \$1,000): the income elasticity peaks at 2.17 at \$4,350, where ownership is 91 per 1,000 if saturation is 800 (recomputed). The 2007 article's text could not be read (publisher paywall; the conference version online carries only the abstract). Its abstract supports a saturation level that depends on urbanization and density and a world vehicle stock rising from 800 million (2002) to more than 2 billion (2030); the earlier article [@DargayGately1999] supports the shape the paper uses: income elasticities of about 2.0 at low and middle incomes, falling toward zero near saturation. Both model total vehicles (cars, trucks and buses), not cars. *Fix:* attribute the curve to CPAT's fleet model, the shape to Dargay and Gately (1999), and keep the 2007 article for saturation. The straight-line approximation (2.0 up to 300 per 1,000, falling to 0 at 800) was rechecked against the Gompertz curve: within 0.25 between 30 and 750 per 1,000 (largest gap 0.24 at 300).

**M4. The SISEPUEDE parameter values cannot be traced to a public source.** Table 2 gives 0.39 (home heat), 1.17 (metals), 0.60 (cement) and 0.92 (chemicals) as "medians of its calibrated country inputs" for 2025, and the text says the travel elasticities of 1.12 and 2.64 "are the same in most of its calibrated countries". The public repository holds one example country (Costa Rica, `ref/examples/input_data_frame.csv`); country calibrations are not public. What the public code and example do show: the growth factor is $\prod(1+\varepsilon g)$ ("standard" elasticity, `_toolbox.py`); passenger-km per person grows with GDP per person (two categories, "private and public" and "regional"), tonne-km with total GDP, household energy demand with GDP per person, industrial production with total GDP (`energy_consumption.py`, `ippu.py`); and in the example the industrial and household elasticities move linearly to 1.0 by 2050 (period 35 from 2015) while travel is held at 1.12 and 2.64 and freight at 1.0. The example's own 2025 values (0.62 cement, 0.75 metals, −0.25 chemicals, 0.08 household heat) show how noisy short calibrations are. Also unverified: "about a thousand futures" and "a few dozen countries, mostly in Latin America" (the IDB report's abstract mentions neither). *Fix:* present the layer's income elasticities for heat and industry as the author's assumptions (rounded: 0.4, 1.2, 0.6, 0.9), say what the public example shows, and cut the unverifiable claims.

**M5. The passenger-travel elasticity of 1.24 conflicts with the evidence the layer is meant to apply.** The combined 1.24 is a passenger-km-weighted blend of SISEPUEDE's example values (1.12 and 2.64), which needs an unstated regional share of about 8 percent. The closest evidence, @SchaferVictor2000, finds that a fixed travel money budget makes total mobility rise "nearly in proportion with income", an elasticity near 1, with the extra travel moving to faster modes. The paper says the layer corrects model values that fail against the evidence, and then keeps this one. *Fix:* use 1.0 for passenger-km per person in the layer, report SISEPUEDE's values, and recompute: travel grows 35 percent in ten years, not 41; the car share of travel rises by about a quarter, not a sixth.

**M6. Proposition 3 is understated, and its stated remedy does not work.** The illustration (a 30 percent car share reaches 100 percent "in about 76 years") makes the problem look remote, and the text says the falling car elasticity of a Gompertz curve is the remedy. With M2 and M5 corrected, the gap in elasticities is 2.0 + 0.1 − 1.0 = 1.1 and the crossing comes after 56 years (32 from a 50 percent share). More important, recomputed with CPAT's own Gompertz curve (saturation 800 per 1,000, starting at \$3,000 per person and 42 vehicles per 1,000), car travel still overtakes total travel, in year 54 from a 30 percent share and year 29 from 50 percent, because ownership can rise from tens to hundreds per 1,000 while travel grows a few-fold. Saturation of ownership is not enough; km per car and occupancy must fall as ownership rises, which is what high-income evidence shows (ownership responds to income more than traffic does, @Goodwin2004). The proposition's condition also needs income to grow without bound, not just "at a positive rate". *Fix:* restate the proposition and its proof, give both calculations, and make the per-year check the rule, with km per car as the adjusting margin.

**M7. The steel-versus-cement paragraph contradicts the paper's own elasticities.** The paper says a carbon price raises clinker's cost by far more in proportion than steel's, "so output effects can be large for cement and small for steel". With the tool's elasticities (−0.4 steel, −0.1 clinker) and stylized numbers (steel 2 tCO₂/t at \$600/t, clinker 0.8 tCO₂/t at \$60/t, \$50/tCO₂), the cost rises 17 and 67 percent and output falls 6.0 and 5.0 percent: about the same. The point that product detail matters survives (the two results come from very different cost shares and elasticities), but the stated conclusion does not. *Fix:* rewrite with the stylized calculation, labelled stylized.

**M8. The CBAM Policy Tool's equation is described differently from paper 6.2.** In paper 6.2 the output response is to a domestic carbon price: $Q_i = Q_i^0(1+\delta_i)^{\varepsilon_i}$ with the carbon cost share $\delta_i = (1-\theta)\tau e_i/p_i$, where $\theta$ is the share rebated per unit of output, plus a partial-adjustment block; base output grows with IMF projections scaled by an output elasticity. Paper 6.4 writes the cost as "the net carbon cost per tonne after any price paid at home", which reads as the border charge. The elasticity values (−0.4, −0.5, −0.1) are not in 6.2's public text. *Fix:* match 6.2's definition, say the values are the author's judgment values used in the tool, and use the tool's name ("the CBAM Policy Tool"), as the series does, instead of "a border-adjustment tool".

**M9. FTT: the facts check out, but three statements need care.** Verified at commit 896a3ce: the standalone package has FTT:Power, Heat, Transport, Freight and an Industrial heat module, with 71 regions; the baseline scenario folder holds inputs for power, heat, transport and freight only (industrial heat ships without data); there is no steel module (FTT:Steel appears only as a reference to the E3ME-FTT paper); the power demand input equals generation in the base year to rounding in every region checked (Belgium, Germany, the United States, India, South Africa, 2022), so it includes losses and own use and is net of trade; the car fleet input is zero in 2022 for 21 of 71 regions (Nigeria, South Africa, Pakistan and Kenya among them); freight tonne-km are computed from vehicles, mileage and load, not read; data years are 2022 for power and transport, 2020 for heat and 2023 for freight. But (a) "FTT's standalone baseline inputs come from a run of E3ME, so its electricity demand and vehicle stocks rest on different GDP and population paths" goes beyond the source: the README says E3ME "informs some of the inputs" and that energy demand is an output of the coupled model. (b) In policy runs FTT adds the *change* in each sector's electricity use against its own baseline run (`sector_coupling/electricity_demand.py`), so the rule is that the baseline power input and the baseline sector runs must come from the same layer, not that the power input must contain "only baseline electricity use". (c) "Investors whose cost comparisons differ" is right (heterogeneous agents, pairwise comparison, logistic substitution: `mathematics.rst`, @Mercure2012, @Mercure2016). *Fix:* re-word (a) and (b).

**M10. Liddle (2014) is misquoted.** The paper says the review "concludes that population is best treated as a pure scale factor". The review (read in its MPRA version) says cross-sectional studies find population elasticities near one while time-variant studies vary widely, and it quotes O'Neill et al.'s argument that, if indirect effects are excluded, population can only act as a scale factor with an elasticity of one. *Fix:* re-word to what the review says.

**M11. Proposition 2 is a tautology as stated; its content is the mapping rule, and one double count is missed.** That two power terms multiply needs no proof. What matters is which model carries the price response for which activity, and the paper misses one case: CPAT's usage margin is itself an activity response (how much the stock is used), so a price-adjusted layer feeding CPAT's fleet model double counts even if the fuel equation is off. And the fleet model's distance response (the road-gasoline usage elasticity, −0.4) is about eight times the per-car response implied by the review evidence (traffic −0.3, ownership −0.25, so about −0.05 per car; @Goodwin2004 as summarized in @Litman2026). *Fix:* keep the proposition short, state the mapping rule as the result, add the fleet-model case and flag the gap in the distance elasticity for paper 5.4.

**M12. The contribution is not stated against the closest prior work, and the strongest objection is not answered.** The idea of one set of drivers for several models is the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways' approach: common population, growth and urbanization assumptions used by several integrated assessment models [@Riahi2017]. The soft-link idea goes back to @Wene1996; hard-linked set-ups reach the same equilibrium in tested cases, though the coupling can change the answer [@HelgesenTomasgard2018]. The strongest objection is: why not hard-link the models, or use E3ME-FTT, which already solves demand and technology together? The answer (none of the four is built for a hard link; ministries run them separately; a soft link with explicit rules is auditable) is scattered. *Fix:* state the contribution against Riahi et al. and Wene, and answer the objection in the limits section.

**M13. The paper lacks the series' second form and front matter.** No executive summary, no brief, a three-slide deck on the old builder, no link to the brief in the first line, the PRWP disclaimer missing, hard-coded equation and table numbers, the CPAT documentation cited under a non-standard key and year (CPATDocs2025, "International Monetary Fund and World Bank", 2025) where the series cites the CPAT Team's 2023 text (published 2024) and its corrected edition of 31 Oct 2026, the bug register cited at version 1.0.2 with a file path that no longer exists, and no record of what was read for any reference. *Fix:* all of these, matching 5.1.

## Minor points

1. Equation (1) omits CPAT's policy efficiency term $\alpha^+$, the income-level factor $inc_t$ and the shadow price in the efficiency term; 5.1 states the corrected form. Use 5.1's form.
2. CPAT's power demand uses last year's blended residential and industrial electricity price (M4.1), not this year's power price.
3. Proposition 1's numbers change with M1 and M4: the gap is 0.4 points a year for cement, 0.1 for fertilizers, 0.08 for household electricity and −0.2 for steel at 1 percent population growth.
4. Home heat: the layer uses a population exponent of 1, so it treats households as growing with population. Where household size falls, households grow faster; say so.
5. Home heat price elasticity (−0.2): CPAT's residential usage elasticities, the closest analogue, are −0.3 to −0.5 for low- and middle-income countries; @Sorrell2009 conclude that direct rebound for household energy services in the OECD should generally be below 30 percent. State both and call −0.2 the author's choice.
6. The README's open item on @deJong2010 is resolved: the report's "consistent set of best-guess values" gives −0.1 directly as the fuel-price elasticity of road freight tonne-km, and −0.2 for vehicle-km (long run, long-distance, Europe). No conversion is needed, but the Europe-only evidence should be noted.
7. @Goodwin2004: the publisher page could not be read; the review's figures (a sustained 10 percent rise in the real fuel price cuts traffic about 1 percent within a year and 3 percent in the longer run, and vehicle ownership by 1.0 and 2.5 percent) were read in @Litman2026's summary. Say the evidence is mostly from high-income countries.
8. @Schmidt2006: the −0.14 price elasticity of steel demand in Brazil is confirmed in the abstract.
9. Equation (6) and the base years: add transport (2022) to the list.
10. "Pressing" claims in the introduction ("Any of these gaps can move the estimated emission cut or revenue ... by more than the policy differences") have no number behind them. Cut or show one.
11. Kalra et al. (2023): Crossref and IDEAS list twelve authors (Kalra, Molina-Pérez, Syme and others); the IDB monograph number could not be confirmed and should be dropped; the toolkit is spelled "SiSePuede" in the report and SISEPUEDE in the code. Use one spelling (SISEPUEDE) and say so once.
12. FTT repository credit: the bib entry names "Climate Policy Assessment Community of Models" as author; the repository names no such body. Use the repository owner (cpmodel) and the README's description.
13. Knobloch et al.: published online 2018, in volume 12 (2019). Keep 2019.
14. Add sources for the drivers the paper recommends: the IMF World Economic Outlook database and UN World Population Prospects 2024.
15. Acronyms: TCO, EV, E3ME, CGE, WEO, WPP: define once or spell out.
16. "Several of its key parameters are templates" and "a few dozen countries": cut or source (see M4).

## Closest prior work and the contribution

The closest prior work is (a) the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways, which give several integrated assessment models common population, growth and urbanization drivers [@Riahi2017], and (b) the soft-linking literature [@Wene1996; @HelgesenTomasgard2018]. Against these, the paper's contribution is narrower and practical: a concordance for four tools a ministry may actually run, with a rule for where the price response sits, three checks that catch the most common errors, and the evidence each link needs. That is a real contribution and should be stated that way, without claiming a new model.

## What the brief and the deck need

**Brief (new, v1.0, Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 6.4, key CFPB64).** About 2,000 words for finance ministries and the World Bank, IMF and IDB teams that run CPAT, SISEPUEDE or FTT for them: why linked models disagree before any policy is applied, what a common driver layer is in plain words, why the carbon-price response must be counted once and where, why cars must fit inside travel, what the data gaps are (FTT's 21 regions; SISEPUEDE's non-public calibrations), and what to do. No equations. One table (which model responds to price, and the layer's rule) and one figure (ten-year growth by activity).

**Deck.** The current deck is three slides on the old spec. Target ten on the new builder: title; the problem in numbers; how each model builds activity; the concordance; the rule; price once; Table 3 figure; cars inside travel; data gaps and limits; what to do.

## Disposition

All major points (M1–M13) and minor points 1–16 are addressed in working paper v0.2 and the new brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026). The SISEPUEDE industrial and household elasticities and the CBAM Policy Tool's output elasticities are now stated as the author's assumptions, because no public source holds them; @Goodwin2004's figures are cited through @Litman2026's summary because the publisher page could not be read. Nothing is left marked unverified.
