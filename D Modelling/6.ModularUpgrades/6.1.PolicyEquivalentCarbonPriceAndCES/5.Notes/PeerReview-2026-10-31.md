# Peer review of 6.1, working paper v0.3 and policy brief v1.0 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of both forms of 6.1 before the v0.4 working paper and v1.1 brief, written from a full reading of both masters, the deck, the three scripts in `2.Source/` and the notes. The earlier review (`PeerReview-and-WPOutline-2026-10-29.md`) was of v0.2; its points are fixed in v0.3. This review is of what is there now. Every number in the worked example was recomputed by running `2.Source/tecp_model.py`, `verify_derivations.py` (30 of 30 checks pass) and `make_wp_figures.py`, and by hand where the arithmetic is short; additional sensitivity runs are in the session scratch script (results quoted below).

## Summary judgement

The working paper answers a clear question (what single carbon tax is a policy mix worth?) with a clear answer (the uniform tax that cuts emissions as much, computable in closed form from an energy balance and two elasticities per sector, and splittable by sector, instrument and margin). The derivation is right: Propositions 1–5 and both corollaries check symbolically and numerically, and every number in the worked example reproduces. The contribution is real but is stated against too narrow a set of precedents: two close pieces of prior work are missing (Weil 2021, who defines the same "carbon price equivalent" and proposes it for border adjustment; Carhart et al. 2022, whose "comprehensive carbon price" is exactly the "shadow prices counted as prices" row of Table 1). One statement about calibration bias is wrong. About a dozen citations say more than their sources, mostly found by the parallel reference check of paper 5.4. The paper has no executive summary, and its main text carries a long running commentary on its own earlier versions that a reader does not need. The brief is not yet a brief: it has equations, no key messages, plain-text cross-references and two factual errors.

## Major points

**M1. The closest prior work is incomplete, so the contribution is overstated.** Weil (2021, *Dickinson Law Review*) defines the "carbon price equivalent" as the carbon price that, in an otherwise neutral policy environment, would produce the same aggregate emissions as a jurisdiction's actual policy mix, and proposes it for border adjustment of domestic taxes and regulations. That is Definition 1 and the border use in @sec-uses. Black et al. (2022) compute the same object with CPAT for the G20. Carhart et al. (2022, *Climate Policy*) sum the marginal incentives of carbon taxes, trading, fuel taxes and subsidies, portfolio standards, feed-in tariffs and low-carbon fuel standards, weighted by coverage, for 25 countries, and name emissions abatement as future work. The v0.3 text cites Carhart only second-hand and does not cite Weil. *Fix:* name all three in the introduction; relabel Table 1's "shadow prices counted as prices" row as Carhart et al.'s comprehensive carbon price; state the contribution as the closed form, the margin decomposition, the accuracy result, the non-price theory, the horizon and the band, not the concept.

**M2. A uniform bias in demand elasticities does not cancel from the TECP.** @sec-calibration says that because demand semi-elasticities "enter Λ and the instruments' cuts alike, a uniform bias cancels". Only a common scaling of *all* elasticities, substitution and demand together, cancels (and then only to first order). Scaling the demand elasticities alone shifts weight from the switching margin to the demand margin and moves the TECP of any policy whose margin mix differs from a uniform tax's. Recomputed: halving every demand elasticity raises the exact TECP of the mix from \$36.9 to \$40.2; doubling them lowers it to \$32.7. Paper 5.4 found the same effect with CPAT's demand elasticities (\$36.9 to \$33.1). Publication bias is a demand-elasticity bias (Havranek et al. 2012), so the error matters. *Fix:* correct the statement, report these numbers, and explain the direction (a switching-heavy mix gains when demand elasticities are overstated less).

**M3. The central case is not central, and the text should say how it relates to paper 5.4.** The example's demand elasticities for power (0.2) and industry (0.3) sit at the bottom of their ranges, so the central exact TECP, \$36.9, lies in the upper part of the Monte Carlo band (median \$35.3, 90 percent range \$32.4–38.1). With every elasticity at the midpoint of its range the exact TECP is \$35.2. Paper 5.4 (v0.2) ran 6.1's model with CPAT's implied demand elasticities (0.53, 0.58, 0.66, at or just above the top of 6.1's ranges) and got \$33.1, near the bottom of the band, and \$37.0 with those elasticities halved. The two papers agree, but neither says so. *Fix:* keep the central case (the series quotes \$37, \$32–38 and \$28; changing it would ripple through 1.0, 6.0 and 5.4), say plainly that it is at the low-demand end, report the midpoint and the 5.4 cases beside it in a sensitivity table, and add 5.4's timing finding: a tool that applies long-run responses in the first year, as CPAT does, would score the mix at its long-run \$37 after one year, about 30 percent above the \$28 the partial-adjustment model gives.

**M4. Several citations claim more than the source.** Checked against the sources (details in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`): Stern (2012) is cited for pair-specific numbers (0.4–2.1) taken from a 2009 working paper whose published abstract says something different (oil–coal above one in industry); Serletis et al. (2010) is cited for "lowest in power and transport", which the abstract does not say; the Labandeira et al. (2017) "long-run means" are CPAT's reproduction, not the authors' summary; Li et al. (2014) "three times" is not in the abstract or summary; Goulder et al. (2012) is cited for leakage "from used cars", and the brief turns their 70–80 percent offset into "roughly doubling the cost per tonne" (an offset of that size multiplies the cost per tonne actually avoided by three to five); Marcantonini and Ellerman's €57 and €552 are working-paper numbers, the published abstract says "tens" and "hundreds" of euros; the Fiscal Monitor 2019 is cited as "reportedly" giving estimates for 135 countries (its Box 1.2 describes the weighting and refers to first-pass estimates elsewhere); Abrell et al. (2019) was cited without being read. *Fix:* re-word each to what the source says, cut Abrell, and record the reads.

**M5. The paper needs a non-technical executive summary, and the history of its earlier versions should leave the main text.** A finance-ministry reader will not get past @sec-model. About 900 words of the main text (contribution 1, Proposition 2(iii), the paragraph on version 0.2's appendix, the paragraph on the superseded workbook, and asides in @sec-model and @sec-tecp) explain what v0.2 got wrong. A reader of v0.4 needs the correct result and one pointer to the erratum. *Fix:* add `# Executive summary` as chapter 1; move the v0.2 material to a short appendix ("Correction to earlier versions") that keeps the proofs and the unit-dependence result, and leave one sentence in the introduction.

**M6. The paper still has no real-country application.** PAPERS.md flags it, and a referee at *Energy Economics* would too: a stylized economy shows the mechanics, not the size of the gap between explicit and effective prices in any real country. This cannot be done credibly in this revision without an energy balance and fuel-price data for a named country (and without using non-public CPAT country runs). *Fix for v0.4:* state it as the main limitation; add a short "How to compute it for a country" subsection listing the inputs (energy balance by fuel and sector, delivered prices including taxes, emission factors, the elasticity ranges of Table 2) and the steps, so that the next version can do it. *Not resolved in v0.4.*

## Minor points

1. Contribution 1 writes $|\eta_s|$ although $\eta_s$ is defined as a positive number; use $\eta_s$ throughout.
2. Proposition 4 calls a binding intensity standard "a feebate at rate λ with benchmark ē_std". Say that, because the standard binds, the average intensity after the policy equals the benchmark, so the implicit feebate is revenue-neutral at the post-policy mix, which links it to the series' definition.
3. Proposition 5(iii): "a lower resource cost" should be "no higher".
4. Half-life of the demand margin at λ = 0.35 is 1.6 years, not "about 2".
5. Calibration table caption: "central values ... follow the brief" is not a reason. State them as the author's central values and say where they sit in each range.
6. The adjustment speed for demand should cite the authors' own short-to-long ratio (Labandeira, Labeaga and López 2016: 0.29–0.34) rather than a second-hand reading of the 2017 article.
7. The darcangelo2022 entry lists "Asa Raj"; the author is Anasuya Raj.
8. The `.bib` has malformed series entries (`CFPB11` to `CFPB13` close before their `note`), unused entries with old `VERIFY` notes, and `StrettonWP11` and `StrettonWP14` still at version 0.3 (both are now v0.4). Proposition numbers cited (1.1 Proposition 6; 1.4 Proposition 4) are still right.
9. The figure of German feed-in tariffs is from the working-paper version; the published version is in *The Energy Journal* (Crossref lists both 36(4) 2015 and 37(1) 2016 records).
10. The first line should link to the brief (`[Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 6.1](brief:)`), and the brief to the working paper's sections.
11. `number-sections: true` is set; three appendix headings use `.appendix .unnumbered`, which is fine. Every main section already has an id.

## The brief

It should be the plain-language form: about 2,000 words, `Key messages` first, bold lead sentences, no equations (v1.0 has three displayed equations and a symbol-heavy "two margins" section), one table and one figure with the working paper's numbers, links into the working paper's sections, "What to do", "Related papers". Fix the two errors (Goulder et al. "roughly doubling"; CBAM Article 9 now in Regulation 2025/2083), the mis-citation of Labandeira and Stern for substitution elasticities, the stale "this section extends" framing of non-price instruments, the `StrettonTECP` key and the page-break raw blocks. Add the band and the one-year number, which the brief lacks.

## The deck

Three slides in an older format. Target ten on the new builder: title; the problem in numbers; why adding prices fails; the definition and the two margins; the weights (figure); the worked example (table); timing and the band; non-price instruments; uses; what to do.

## Disposition

All major points except M6 and all minor points are addressed in working paper v0.4 and brief v1.1 (31 Oct 2026). M6 (a real-country application) is stated in the paper as the main limitation and next step, with a recipe for doing it; it is not resolved. See `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md` for the reference changes.
