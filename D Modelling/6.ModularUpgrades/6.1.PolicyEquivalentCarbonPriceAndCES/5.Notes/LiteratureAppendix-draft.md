## 0. Five findings that matter most for 1.7

1. **The IMF already defines and computes the TECP concept.** Black, Minnett, Parry, Roaf & Zhunussova (2022) define an "economywide carbon price equivalent" (ECPE): "the carbon price that would yield the same emissions reduction" as a policy. They also define a sectoral version (SCPE) and compute both for the G20 using CPAT. Parry, Black & Roaf (2021) already propose weighting fuel taxes "by their relative effectiveness at reducing economy-wide CO2 emissions compared with a comprehensive carbon price". 1.7 cannot claim the *concept* as new. What it can claim is a closed-form, decomposable, linear-in-policy version (the Ω weights) that needs no model re-solve and splits the effect by margin.
2. **The World Bank names the gap 1.7 fills.** Agnolucci et al. (2023/2024), §6.4, "Effectiveness-weighted total carbon pricing", say current metrics (OECD ECR, the World Carbon Pricing Database, their own TCP) are "descriptive" and weight by emissions only. They propose weighting by fuel- and sector-specific "mitigation elasticities" as future work. That is the TECP, almost word for word. This is the best single citation for the motivation.
3. **The calibration table in the paper misattributes its sources.** Stern (2012) covers interfuel substitution (Morishima/shadow elasticities) in *industry, manufacturing and the macro-economy*. It has no road-transport, power or residential estimates. Labandeira et al. (2017) gives *own-price demand* elasticities, not substitution elasticities. So neither source supports the σ column for transport, power or buildings. Labandeira does support the η column, roughly. The sectoral interfuel evidence (Serletis et al. 2010) finds σ < 1 in every sector, with power and transport the *lowest*. That contradicts σ_power = 2–4, which needs a different source: Papageorgiou, Saam & Schulte (2017) on clean-versus-dirty substitution, with σ > 1.
4. **Taxes move demand more than equal price changes do.** Davis & Kilian (2011), Li, Linn & Muehlegger (2014), Andersson (2019) and Rivers & Schaufele (2015) all find this, with tax elasticities about 3× price elasticities in the cleanest studies. Price elasticities are what 1.7 calibrates to, so the TECP will understate explicit taxes relative to "shadow" prices from standards and feebates. This hits instrument-neutrality, the metric's core selling point.
5. **Emissions-equivalence is not cost-equivalence.** Keen, Parry & Roaf (2021/2022) and Black et al. (2022, Annex B) both stress that a regulation at a given shadow price imposes much lower private costs on firms than a tax at the same rate, because there is no tax-transfer rectangle. For BCA/CBAM recognition, which is the use in 1.1, the TECP answers the environmental-equivalence question, not the competitiveness question. The paper must say which one CBAM recognition should rest on.

## 1. Effective carbon rates and carbon-pricing metrics

**`oecd2013ecp`: OECD (2013), *Effective Carbon Prices*.** *Abstract.* Estimates the cost per tonne abated of policies in 15 countries across five sectors: electricity, road transport, pulp and paper, cement, and household energy. Instruments include taxes, trading, feed-in tariffs, capital subsidies and regulations. It finds wide variation in cost per tonne within and across countries and instruments. Market instruments are consistently cheapest; capital subsidies and feed-in tariffs are among the most expensive. This is the original "effective carbon price" in the *implicit-cost* sense: cost divided by abatement. It is the sense Aldy & Pizer (2016) and Mehling et al. (2019) mean. *Use in 1.7:* the closest OECD precursor for non-price instruments. Distinguish TECP (emissions-equivalent marginal price) from OECD-2013 (average cost per tonne).

**`oecd2016ecr`, `oecd2021ecr`, `oecd2023ecr`: OECD Effective Carbon Rates (2016; 2021; 2023, 4th edition).** *Abstract/metadata (OECD iLibrary and oecd.org blocked here).* The ECR is the sum of fuel excise taxes, explicit carbon taxes and ETS permit prices, in EUR/tCO2. It is computed for each fuel-by-sector cell and aggregated by emissions weights. The "net ECR" deducts fossil-fuel subsidies. The 2023 edition covers developments through 2021 with updates to 2023 [VERIFY: country count, ~71–72 countries; headline shares of emissions priced]. The ECR deliberately excludes non-price instruments and does not weight by responsiveness (Parry, Black & Roaf 2021, fn 26, note this explicitly). *Use in 1.7:* the main foil. The TECP's fuel-sector cell structure is the same as the ECR's, which makes it easy to explain: TECP = ECR cell prices × abatement weights Ω instead of × emissions shares.

**`darcangelo2022`: D'Arcangelo, Pisu, Raj & van Dender (2022), OECD Economics WP 1732.** *Abstract.* Estimates the long-run elasticity of emissions with respect to the OECD ECR in a cross-country panel. A EUR 10 rise in the effective carbon rate cuts fossil CO2 by 3.7% on average in the long run. Broadening coverage to unpriced emissions delivers two-thirds of the effect. Even ECRs of about EUR 1,000/t by the late 2030s would not reach net zero. *Use in 1.7:* the only econometric estimate of an emissions semi-elasticity with respect to an aggregate effective carbon price. Use it as an external check on the TECP's Λ: TECP-implied Λ/E should be of order 0.004 per EUR/t. It also shows responses vary with sectoral structure, which supports country-specific Ω.

**`agnolucci2023tcp` / `agnolucci2024wbro`: Agnolucci, Fischer, Heine, Montes de Oca Leon, Pryor, Patroni & Hallegatte (2023, WB PRWP 10486; 2024, *World Bank Research Observer* 39(2)).** *Read (PRWP).* Builds a "total carbon price" (TCP) for 142 countries, 1991–2021. It sums direct pricing (carbon taxes and ETS, weighted by coverage) and indirect pricing (fuel excises, consumption subsidies and VAT deviations, via a price-gap method), per fuel and sector, aggregated by emissions. The global TCP is barely above its 1994 level. Indirect pricing is 87% of the global TCP in 2021. Transport is priced highest, at roughly USD 110/tCO2 in 2021, and coal and gas are barely priced. Net-exporting low- and lower-middle-income countries subsidise at about USD 25/t; importing upper-middle- and high-income countries average about USD 42/t. Compared with the OECD net ECR, the TCP is broadly aligned for OECD countries but often far off, sometimes with the opposite sign, for poorer countries. §6.4 proposes "effectiveness-weighted total carbon pricing", weighting by "mitigation elasticity", and notes salience effects (taxes vs prices). §6.3 distinguishes marginal from average carbon prices and notes that BCA credit is based on *average* prices. *Use in 1.7:* the key motivating citation. Also take the marginal-vs-average point into the CBAM discussion.

**`dolphin2022wcpd`: Dolphin & Xiahou (2022), *Scientific Data* 9.** *Read.* Documents the World Carbon Pricing Database: sectoral (IPCC 2006) coverage and prices of explicit carbon taxes and ETSs, national and subnational, 1990–2020. Explicit instruments only. In 2020, 43 national and 32 subnational jurisdictions had a mechanism in at least one sector. *Use in 1.7:* data source for τ on explicit instruments at IPCC-sector resolution, which maps onto the TECP's s-dimension.

**`parry2021subsidies`: Parry, Black & Vernon (2021), IMF WP 21/236.** *Read.* Efficient fuel prices (supply cost plus environmental costs plus foregone consumption tax) and implied subsidies for 191 countries. Global subsidies were USD 5.9 trillion in 2020 (6.8% of GDP). 8% of that is explicit and 92% implicit. Efficient pricing in 2025 would cut CO2 36% below baseline. The global warming component uses a carbon value of USD 60/t in 2020, rising. Fuel demand uses constant-elasticity curves with long-run price elasticities "typically between about −0.5 and −0.8". *Use in 1.7:* source for subsidies as negative τ cells. Note that the IMF's "implicit subsidy" is a gap to an *efficient* price, not a policy price. The TECP should use only the explicit-subsidy part, as the World Bank TCP does.

**`black2023subsidies`: Black, Liu, Parry & Vernon (2023), IMF WP 23/169.** *Read.* 2023 update. Subsidies were USD 7 trillion in 2022 (7.1% of GDP). Explicit subsidies more than doubled since 2020 but are still only 18% of the total; nearly 60% is undercharging for warming and local air pollution. 80% of global coal use is priced below half its efficient level. Full reform would cut CO2 43% below 2030 baseline. It adds a "smart non-pricing" combination (tradable rate standards or feebates by sector) that captures a large share of pricing's CO2 benefit but forgoes the reduced-driving co-benefits. A ±50% change in fuel price elasticities changes CO2 and efficiency gains by about ±1/3. *Use in 1.7:* the latest IMF numbers. Its non-pricing scenario is a ready-made test case for the TECP: what TECP does a sectoral feebate package reach?

**`coady2019subsidies`: Coady, Parry, Le & Shang (2019), IMF WP 19/89.** *Read.* Subsidies were USD 4.7 trillion (6.3% of GDP) in 2015 and USD 5.2 trillion projected for 2017. China, the US, Russia, the EU and India are the largest. Efficient pricing in 2015 would have cut CO2 28% and air-pollution deaths 46%. *Use in 1.7:* background and series history. Also see `coady2017wd` (*World Development* 91, metadata) for the peer-reviewed version.

**`imf2019fm`: IMF (2019), *Fiscal Monitor*, October 2019, "How to Mitigate Climate Change".** *Metadata only [VERIFY].* Parry, Black & Roaf (2021, fn 26) say it gives "first-pass estimates of effective carbon prices for 135 countries" (pp. 91–93) that *do* account for differences in fuel price responsiveness, unlike the OECD. If so, this is the earliest responsiveness-weighted effective carbon price and must be cited and compared. *Use in 1.7:* priority to obtain (elibrary.imf.org).

**Critiques of price-gap and ECR methods (as reported by Agnolucci et al. 2023, §6.2, citing Kojima & Koplow 2015).** Price gaps confuse market power and distribution costs with taxes. Market price support shows up as a "tax". Reference prices are hard to set in large, heterogeneous countries. The ECR's desk-review method is more accurate but narrower in coverage. Parry, Black & Roaf (2021) add that fuel taxes set for congestion, accidents or revenue should not be relabelled as carbon prices. They propose measuring *changes* from a benchmark year instead. *Use in 1.7:* the TECP inherits all of these on its τ inputs. It improves weighting, not measurement of τ.

## 2. Implicit carbon prices of non-price policies

**`gillingham2018`: Gillingham & Stock (2018), *JEP* 32(4): 53–72.** *Abstract (AEA PDF blocked).* Reviews static cost per tonne of mitigation options and policies: fuel-economy standards, gasoline taxes, renewable portfolio standards, wind and solar subsidies, biofuel mandates, weatherisation and supply-side restrictions. Costs run from negative to many hundreds of dollars per tonne [VERIFY: specific figures in their Table 1]. The central argument is that *static* cost per tonne can mislead: some high-static-cost actions (solar, EVs) have low *dynamic* cost through learning and spillovers, and vice versa. *Use in 1.7:* the standard survey of implicit carbon prices. It also gives the dynamic-cost objection to *any* static equivalence metric: the TECP credits a solar subsidy only for current abatement, not induced cost declines.

**`marcantonini2015`: Marcantonini & Ellerman (2015), *Energy Journal* 36(4).** *Read (EUI WP).* Ex-post net cost of German feed-in tariffs for wind and solar, 2006–2010. Net cost is remuneration minus savings in fuel, carbon, capacity and start-up costs, plus balancing costs. They divide net cost by CO2 abated to get a "carbon surcharge", then add the EUA price to get an "implicit carbon price". Wind: surcharge EUR 45/t, implicit price EUR 57/t (2006–10 average, range 34–77). Solar: surcharge EUR 537/t, implicit price EUR 552/t (range 461–627). Wind abated 22–32 Mt/yr; solar 2–7 Mt/yr. *Use in 1.7:* the canonical average-cost implicit price of a renewable subsidy. A worked contrast: the TECP of a FIT is the uniform tax that would cut as much CO2. For German solar that is far below EUR 552/t, which shows TECP ≠ implicit cost.

**`abrell2019`: Abrell, Kosch & Rausch (2019), *J. Public Economics* 169: 172–202.** *Metadata only (ScienceDirect and SSRN blocked; ZHAW repository has no public file).* Estimates the abatement and cost per tonne of wind and solar subsidies in Germany and Spain from high-frequency power-market data [VERIFY: headline EUR/t figures]. *Use in 1.7:* the econometric (rather than accounting) counterpart to Marcantonini & Ellerman. Priority to obtain.

**`holland2009`: Holland, Hughes & Knittel (2009), *AEJ: Policy* 1(1): 106–146.** *Read (NBER WP 13266).* A low-carbon fuel standard (an intensity standard) taxes high-carbon fuel and *subsidises* low-carbon fuel. It can in principle raise emissions; in simulation it rarely does, but it is costly. For a national LCFS cutting carbon intensity 10%: surplus loss USD 80–760 billion/yr, average cost USD 307–2,272/tCO2. An efficient policy achieving the same reduction costs USD 16–290 billion, or USD 60–868/t. *Use in 1.7:* the paper treats a clean fuel standard as "dual to a feebate", a pure intensive instrument whose extensive component "follows from the average abatement cost". HHK show the implicit output subsidy makes the extensive component *negative*: an intensity standard expands total fuel use relative to a tax. The TECP decomposition must carry the sign through.

**`goulder2012`: Goulder, Jacobsen & van Benthem (2012), *JEEM* 63(2): 187–207.** *Read (NBER WP 15337).* California-led state limits on GHG per mile, nested inside federal CAFE, cause leakage. Automakers sell more high-emission cars in non-adopting states and the used-car market adjusts. 70–80% of the new-car emissions reductions in adopting states are offset. *Use in 1.7:* overlapping non-price instruments do not add. Leakage within the same market can wipe out the abatement a single-country, single-instrument TECP would credit. Relevant to the paper's "special case" claim that overlapping instruments sum.

**`goulder2016`: Goulder, Hafstead & Williams (2016), *AEJ: Policy* 8(2): 186–218.** *Read (NBER WP 19847).* A clean energy standard (CES) is equivalent to a revenue-neutral tax-and-subsidy on generators by technology. In their GE model the CES relies too much on fuel switching and too little on demand reduction. In the long run, demand reduction under cap-and-trade is about 3.5× that under the CES for a 20% cumulative cut and about 1.9× for 40%. But the CES has a smaller tax-interaction cost, so for modest targets it can be *cheaper* than cap-and-trade. Cost-effectiveness depends on crediting gas at the right level (about 0.58 credit). *Use in 1.7:* direct support for the intensive/extensive decomposition. A CES, like a feebate, works mainly on the intensive margin. The welfare result is a caution against the paper's claim that a TECP-equal swap is "first-order welfare-neutral".

**`fischer2008`: Fischer & Newell (2008), *JEEM* 55(2): 142–162.** *Read (RFF DP 04-05).* Ranks policies for US power-sector CO2 by cost for the same reduction: emissions price < emissions performance standard (+41%) < fossil output tax (+61%) < renewable portfolio standard (2.04×) < renewable production subsidy (2.47×) < R&D subsidy. Each instrument decomposes into implicit taxes and subsidies by technology. An RPS is "a nearly 0.1 c/kWh fossil output tax with a 1.2 c/kWh renewables production subsidy", with *no* conservation incentive and a slightly *lower* electricity price. *Use in 1.7:* the cleanest template for mapping a non-price instrument into implicit (τ_int, τ_ext) pairs. An RPS has τ_ext ≤ 0, so its TECP can be well below its shadow price.

**`kalkuhl2013`: Kalkuhl, Edenhofer & Lessmann (2013), *Resource and Energy Economics* 35(3): 217–234.** *Read (FEEM WP).* A global GE model with an intertemporal fossil-resource sector. If carbon pricing is permanently missing, mitigation costs under the optimal second-best renewable subsidy are a *multiple* of first-best. Small deviations from the second-best subsidy raise emissions sharply, a "green paradox" through resource owners. Revenue-neutral instruments (a feed-in-tariff scheme, a carbon trust) do better. *Use in 1.7:* supply-side and intertemporal effects are outside the TECP's demand-side model. A subsidy's TECP can be badly wrong if it changes extraction paths.

**`greenstone2019rps`: Greenstone & Nath, "Do Renewable Portfolio Standards Deliver Cost-Effective Carbon Abatement?"** *Metadata only [VERIFY: final journal/version; SSRN 2019 and 2026 versions exist; the NBER number tried was wrong].* Widely cited for implied abatement costs of US RPS of roughly USD 130–460/tCO2 [VERIFY]. *Use in 1.7:* an ex-post implicit price for mandates.

**`black2023cpat` and `cpat2022technotes`: CPAT documentation (Black, Parry, Mylonas, Vernon & Zhunussova 2023, IMF WP 23/128; World Bank 2022 technical notes).** *Read (the relevant sections).* CPAT models non-price policies "through a shadow price" on the *efficiency* margin only. A fuel tax or carbon price enters both the usage term u_t and the efficiency term h_t; a feebate adds a shadow price "to p_t in the expression for h_t only". Price elasticities are split into an "intensive margin" (usage of existing capital) and an "efficiency and extensive margin" (fuel economy and ownership), calibrated to Labandeira et al. (2017). All are long-run. Rebound with ε_F = ε_U = −0.3 is 15.5%. *Use in 1.7:* CPAT's margin split is the closest existing analogue to the TECP's, but on a different axis. CPAT separates *use* from *efficiency*; the TECP separates *fuel switching* from *conservation*. The paper should say so. Also, CPAT's own elasticity tables (reproduced in §4 below) are the most practical calibration source.

## 3. Policy equivalence and cross-country comparison of mitigation effort

**`aldy2016reep`: Aldy & Pizer (2016), *REEP* 10(1): 3–24.** *Read (Harvard DASH).* Surveys metrics for comparing effort: emissions, emissions intensity, reductions versus BAU, explicit and implicit carbon prices, energy prices and taxes, and costs. No single metric is comprehensive, measurable and universal, so they recommend a dashboard. On implicit prices: they "estimate the average cost of abatement", are model-derived, "are not market signals", and are high both for costly policies and for those with little net abatement. Germany's wind and solar implicit prices are EUR 62 and 537/t (Marcantonini & Ellerman 2013 WP). They call for "a single economy-wide average carbon price that weights implicit and explicit carbon prices" (citing OECD 2013). Energy-price metrics miss non-price regulation. *Use in 1.7:* frames the TECP as an answer to Aldy & Pizer's call. Their criteria (comprehensive, measurable/replicable, universal) are the natural test for the TECP.

**`aldy2017`: Aldy, Pizer & Akimoto (2017), *Climate Policy* 17(4): 501–515.** *Read (RFF DP 15-32).* Template for ex ante and ex post review of pledges. RITE DNE21+ estimates for post-2020 INDCs: US 2025 marginal abatement cost USD 64/tCO2e and cost 0.39% of GDP. EU 2030: USD 166/t and 0.82% of GDP. Russia and China: USD 3–5/t, roughly zero cost. Rankings change with base year and metric. *Use in 1.7:* marginal abatement cost from a full model is the benchmark the TECP approximates cheaply.

**`aldy2016ncc`: Aldy, Pizer, Tavoni et al. (2016), *Nature Climate Change* 6: 1000–1004.** *Read (accepted MS).* Four IAMs assess Paris pledges. Marginal abatement costs vary across countries by two orders of magnitude. Implied global carbon prices are USD 7–28/tCO2e, below the mean SCC (USD 57/t in 2030). Marginal cost rises almost proportionally with income (burden elasticity about 1.1). *Use in 1.7:* model dispersion is large. A one-model TECP needs a sensitivity band.

**`black2022framework`: Black, Minnett, Parry, Roaf & Zhunussova (2022), IMF WP 22/254.** *Read.* Defines the carbon price equivalent (CPE), the economywide CPE (ECPE) and the sectoral CPE (SCPE). Method: (i) build a CPAT BAU, (ii) simulate the policy or sectoral target, (iii) find the economywide or sectoral carbon price giving the same CO2 cut. Results for the G20 in 2030: explicit-pricing ECPEs are mostly modest, but about USD 50/t or more for Canada, France, Germany, Italy and the UK. Power-sector renewables and coal-phaseout targets imply SCPEs above USD 150/t in ten cases. Combined ECPEs exceed USD 150/t in six cases and range from USD 1–136/t elsewhere. Existing fuel taxes imply ECPEs of about USD 5–40/t, with ambiguity over whether to count them. Renewables targets are modelled as a renewable subsidy plus an electricity tax; intensity standards as an output-rebated carbon charge; vehicle CO2/km standards as a shadow price on efficiency. Annex B: mitigation equivalence ≠ cost or competitiveness equivalence. *Use in 1.7:* the single most important precursor. 1.7's contribution relative to it: an analytical, linear Ω-weight formula, an explicit two-margin decomposition, instruments entered by their price wedges, and computation without re-running CPAT. 1.7 should validate the TECP against the ECPEs for at least one G20 country.

**`parry2021icpf`: Parry, Black & Roaf (2021), IMF Staff Climate Note 2021/001.** *Read.* An international carbon price floor among six large emitters (China, US, India, EU, Canada, UK), at USD 50/t or differentiated at 25/50/75. Either version cuts energy CO2 23–24% below 2030 BAU, against 14% if G20 NDCs are met. Design allows "non-pricing approaches with equivalent emissions impacts as the price floor (perhaps subject to third-party verification)". Proposed effective-carbon-price compliance metric: (1) express fuel taxes in CO2 terms; (2) weight fuel taxes and explicit prices "by their relative effectiveness at reducing economy-wide CO2 emissions compared with a comprehensive carbon price"; (3) aggregate. Because fuel taxes are set for other reasons, the floor should bind on the *increase* in effective price over a benchmark year. Fn 26 says OECD estimates "do not account for differences in the price responsiveness of fuels in different sectors". *Use in 1.7:* the use case, and IMF endorsement of the responsiveness-weighting principle. The "change from benchmark" design is worth adopting.

## 4. Energy demand elasticities, interfuel substitution, CES and partial adjustment

**`labandeira2017`: Labandeira, Labeaga & López-Otero (2017), *Energy Policy* 102: 549–568. Cited in 1.7.** *Metadata confirmed via Crossref. Full text not accessible here (ScienceDirect blocked; the open WP at eforenergy.org is unreachable through the proxy).* Meta-analysis of about 2,000 own-price elasticities from about 430 studies, covering seven fuels and four sectors [count as reported by CPAT technical notes]. Long-run means as reproduced in CPAT Table 3-2 (Table A): residential −0.62, industrial −0.51, commercial −0.72, transport −0.44. By good: energy −0.57, electricity −0.51, natural gas −0.57, gasoline −0.53, diesel −0.39, heating oil −0.54. "Adjusted" long-run elasticities: electricity −0.37, gas −0.68, gasoline −0.77, diesel −0.44, heating oil −0.19. Developing countries are about 10% more elastic. Short-run means [VERIFY against the article; from recollection, not seen here]: overall about −0.22, electricity about −0.13, gas about −0.18, gasoline about −0.29, diesel about −0.15, heating oil about −0.02. *Use in 1.7:* correct source for η (own-price) by fuel and sector. **Not** a source for σ.

**`stern2012`: Stern (2012), *Journal of Economic Surveys* 26(2): 307–331. Cited in 1.7.** *Read (MPRA WP 15792, June 2009; the published version may differ in detail).* Meta-analysis of interfuel substitution: 46 primary studies, up to 367 observations, of *shadow (Morishima) elasticities* among coal, oil, gas and electricity in the industrial sector, manufacturing, sub-industries and the macro-economy. No transport, power-generation or residential samples. Mean elasticities (Table 3): unweighted 0.83–1.22 across pairs; sample-size-weighted 0.76–2.03. Fixed-effects panel estimates, which Stern judges best though biased downward: coal-oil 0.71, coal-gas 2.07, coal-electricity 0.42, oil-gas 1.32, oil-electricity 0.63, gas-electricity 1.54. Macro-level estimates are lower: coal-oil 0.32, coal-electricity 0.11, oil-gas 0.37. Manufacturing estimates are 1.2–2.2. Cross-section studies give the largest values and time-series the smallest. No publication bias found. Electricity pairs show the least substitutability. *Use in 1.7:* supports σ_industry ≈ 0.5–1.5 (good) and the general "σ rises with horizon" claim. For a CES nest, every Morishima elasticity equals σ, so Stern's pair-specific spread (0.4–2.1) is itself evidence against a single σ per sector.

**`serletis2010ej` / `serletis2009wp`: Serletis, Timilsina & Vasetsky (2010), *Energy Journal* 31(4): 1–30; WB PRWP 5026 (2009).** *Read (WP); abstract (EJ).* Sector-level interfuel Morishima elasticities using locally flexible normalised quadratic forms on annual IEA data, 1980–2006, for a range of OECD and developing countries. Elasticities are "consistently below unity" in every sector. Industrial and residential substitution exceeds that in power generation and transport. In power, the three-fossil-fuel models (coal, oil, gas, *no renewables*) show σ < 1 except for oil-gas and coal-oil in the US. *Use in 1.7:* the right sectoral source, and it cuts *against* the paper's σ_power = 2–4 and σ_transport = 1–1.5. These are short-run, fossil-only estimates, so the paper can argue that long-run clean-vs-dirty σ is larger. But that requires a different citation (Papageorgiou et al.).

**`papageorgiou2017`: Papageorgiou, Saam & Schulte (2017), *REStat* 99(2): 281–290.** *Abstract.* Nested-CES estimates on sectoral data for 26 countries of the elasticity of substitution between clean and dirty energy inputs. It "significantly exceeds unity" [VERIFY: point estimates, from recollection about 2 in electricity generation and about 3 in non-energy industries]. *Use in 1.7:* the appropriate source for σ_power > 1 in a clean-vs-dirty nest. Its CES framing matches 1.7's.

**`havranek2012`: Havranek, Irsova & Janda (2012), *Energy Economics* 34(1): 201–207.** *Read (WP).* Meta-analysis of gasoline price elasticities with publication-bias correction. Published estimates average about −0.23 in the short run (110 estimates) and −0.69 in the long run (92). After correcting for selective reporting, the true means are −0.09 (short run) and −0.31 (long run), so published averages are exaggerated about twofold. *Use in 1.7:* supports the paper's lower-end η for road transport (−0.3). It warns that meta-analysis means (Labandeira, Espey, Brons) are biased upward in magnitude. TECP weights should carry a bias-corrected sensitivity case.

**`davis2011`: Davis & Kilian (2011), *J. Applied Econometrics* 26(7): 1187–1214.** *Read (NBER WP 14685).* US gasoline demand. OLS price elasticities are small: −0.10 national, −0.19 state panel. IV estimates exploiting gasoline-tax variation are larger: about −0.32, rising to −0.46 to −0.74 in specifications using nominal tax increases. Preferred estimate: a 10¢/gal tax rise cuts vehicle CO2 by about 1.5%. *Use in 1.7:* a tax-response elasticity exceeds the price-response elasticity, and the TECP's τ is a tax.

**`li2014`: Li, Linn & Muehlegger (2014), *AEJ: Policy* 6(4): 302–342.** *Read (NBER WP 17891).* US state data. Gasoline tax increases cut consumption far more than equal tax-exclusive price rises. A 5¢ tax rise cuts consumption 0.86%, three times the effect implied by a pooled price elasticity. Household data show the differential effect works through vehicle choice (MPG), especially for newer vehicles. *Use in 1.7:* evidence for salience or persistence on the *intensive* (capital-switching) margin. Taxes are seen as permanent, so they move capital choice more.

**`andersson2019`: Andersson (2019), *AEJ: Policy* 11(4): 1–30.** *Abstract.* Synthetic control for Sweden's 1991 carbon tax and VAT on transport fuel. Transport CO2 fell almost 11%, mostly due to the carbon tax. The carbon-tax elasticity of gasoline demand is three times the price elasticity, so "using price elasticities to simulate emission reductions may... significantly underestimate" a tax's effect. *Use in 1.7:* the same point, ex post, for a carbon tax specifically.

## 5. Uses: border adjustment recognition, crediting, price floors

**`keen2022bca` (read as Keen, Parry & Roaf 2021, IMF WP 21/239; published *Fiscal Studies* 43(3): 209–234, 2022).** *Read.* BCAs are the most effective domestic instrument against leakage and competitiveness loss, but design is critical. Non-price policies impose only the efficiency-cost triangle on firms, not the tax-transfer rectangle, so they "generally impose markedly lower private costs on firms than carbon pricing (at equivalent shadow prices)". Charging imports the *shadow* price is therefore not the right competitiveness response, and a pricing country "could well choose to apply [a BCA] to imports from a country achieving equivalent emissions reductions through regulations". Exemptions for non-price efforts can be justified on cooperation or leakage grounds but not narrow competitiveness grounds, and are legally contestable under WTO non-discrimination. *Use in 1.7 / 1.1:* the core objection to using a TECP for CBAM credit. The TECP measures environmental effort, while the CBAM adjusts for carbon cost actually paid. 1.1 needs to argue explicitly for recognition on effort grounds.

**`mehling2019`: Mehling, van Asselt, Das, Droege & Verkuijl (2019), *AJIL* 113(3): 433–481.** *Read (relevant parts).* Legal-economic design of BCAs. Under 20% of global emissions face an explicit price, and explicit prices are "significantly lower than the cost of compliance with other non-price carbon constraints". Over time a BCA "could seek to adjust for the differential between effective carbon prices... including both explicit and implicit carbon prices". The effective price is computed from energy taxes by carbon content, average sectoral abatement cost × mandated reductions, or "economic models to identify the carbon price needed to achieve mandated emission reductions in a sector" (citing OECD 2013), with third-party verification and deduction of foreign carbon constraints for GATT Art. III. *Use in 1.7:* legal scholarship already anticipates a model-based "carbon price needed to achieve the same reduction". The TECP is one such model and should cite this.

**`cosbey2019`: Cosbey, Droege, Fischer & Munnings (2019), *REEP* 13(1): 3–22.** *Abstract.* Reviews BCA design choices: scope, carbon-content method, adjustment type and price, revenue use, and compatibility with trade and climate regimes. Agnolucci et al. (2023) cite it for the point that border credit is based on *average*, not marginal, carbon prices. *Use in 1.7:* the TECP is a marginal-equivalence measure. CBAM deductions concern average carbon cost per tonne embodied (free allocation and rebates matter), so a separate average-price step is needed.

**`eu2023cbam`: Regulation (EU) 2023/956 (CBAM), Art. 9.** *Metadata [VERIFY wording].* Deducts only "carbon price effectively paid" in the country of origin. 1.1 already cites it. *Use:* the legal constraint the TECP must work around. As drafted, it does not recognise feebates or standards.

## 6. Synthesis: what is new in the TECP, and what a referee will say

**How the TECP differs from existing metrics.** The OECD Effective Carbon Rate and the World Bank's Total Carbon Price are *price-signal* metrics. Both start from the same fuel-by-sector grid of explicit carbon prices, ETS prices, excise taxes and (for the net ECR and TCP) consumption subsidies. Both aggregate by emissions shares, so a dollar of excise on inelastic diesel counts the same as a dollar on gas with an electric substitute. The IMF has two different objects. Its subsidy estimates (Parry, Black & Vernon 2021; Black et al. 2023) measure gaps to an *efficient* price. Its carbon price equivalents (Black et al. 2022) are *outcome* metrics: the uniform price that delivers the same CO2 cut as a policy, found by re-simulating CPAT. The TECP sits between them. It keeps the ECR's grid and the CPE's definition, but replaces model re-simulation with a closed-form linearisation. The TECP is a weighted sum of cell-level price wedges, with weights Ω set by the economy (emissions, prices, σ, η) and not by the policy. Agnolucci et al. (2023, §6.4) and Parry, Black & Roaf (2021) both ask for exactly this "effectiveness-weighted" aggregation. Neither builds it.

**What is genuinely new.** Four things. (1) The Ω-weight representation: once Ω is computed, any package is evaluated by a SUMPRODUCT, transparently and reproducibly, which addresses Aldy & Pizer's replicability criterion. (2) The decomposition of each instrument into an intensive (relative-price) and an extensive (average-cost) wedge. This explains *why* feebates, clean energy standards and RPSs deliver less abatement per dollar of shadow price than a tax: they have small or negative τ_ext, as Fischer & Newell (2008), Goulder et al. (2016) and Holland et al. (2009) show. (3) A CES microfoundation that disciplines the n×n elasticity matrix with two parameters per sector. (4) A route to credit non-price instruments at the border and in results-based finance (1.1), which the CBAM does not do.

**What is not new, and should be conceded.** The concept of a carbon price equivalent (Black et al. 2022; OECD 2013 in cost terms; Mehling et al. 2019 in legal terms). The idea of weighting by responsiveness (Parry, Black & Roaf 2021; reportedly IMF 2019 Fiscal Monitor [VERIFY]). Representing standards as shadow prices (CPAT; Fischer & Newell 2008). The two-margin split of price elasticities (CPAT uses usage vs efficiency).

**Main referee objections.**

1. *"This is CPAT's ECPE in a spreadsheet."* The paper must benchmark against Black et al. (2022). Show TECP ≈ ECPE for a G20 case, and show where linearisation breaks (SCPEs above USD 150/t are exactly where the paper's own ±20% linearisation bound fails).
2. *Calibration is mis-sourced and fragile.* Stern (2012) has no transport, power or residential σ. Labandeira (2017) has no σ at all. Sectoral evidence puts σ < 1 (Serletis et al. 2010). Meta-analytic η are biased upward in magnitude (Havranek et al. 2012). Because Ω depends on σ and η, rankings of countries can flip with calibration. A sensitivity analysis is mandatory.
3. *Taxes are more effective than price-equivalent shadow prices* (Andersson 2019; Li et al. 2014; Davis & Kilian 2011; Rivers & Schaufele 2015). A price-elasticity-calibrated TECP understates explicit carbon taxes and overstates the equivalence of fuel-price changes. Conversely, standards bind regardless of salience. Instrument-specific effectiveness factors are needed, or the claim of instrument neutrality must be softened.
4. *Additivity fails.* The paper's claim that overlapping instruments sum is only first-order. Nested regulations leak (Goulder et al. 2012: 70–80% offset). Overlapping caps make complementary policies zero-abatement under a binding ETS cap (waterbed). Black et al. (2022) devote effort to avoiding double counting. The TECP needs a rule for policies under a binding cap.
5. *Emissions-equivalent ≠ cost-equivalent ≠ welfare-equivalent.* For CBAM recognition, Keen, Parry & Roaf show non-price policies impose lower private costs at the same shadow price. Goulder et al. (2016) show TECP-equal swaps differ in welfare cost through tax interactions. The claim that "replacing a feebate with a carbon tax of equivalent TECP is a first-order welfare-neutral move" should be removed.
6. *Static versus dynamic.* Gillingham & Stock (2018) and Kalkuhl et al. (2013): learning, spillovers and fossil-supply responses are excluded, so the TECP will understate solar and EV support and can misstate subsidies.
7. *Inputs inherit price-gap problems.* Measuring τ for subsidies and taxes (Agnolucci et al. 2023, §6.2; Kojima & Koplow 2015), and the question of whether pre-existing fuel taxes "count" (Parry, Black & Roaf 2021 recommend changes from a benchmark year).
8. *Internal consistency.* The qmd and the short LaTeX variant normalise τ_int differently. In the qmd, the (e_f − ē_s) term appears in both the semi-elasticity (A.7) and the Λ bracket (eq-lambda), which looks like double-counting. The split-speed text labels the fast conservation speed λ_int. The headline claims should be checked against one consistent derivation before submission.
