# Peer review of 6.2, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "The CBAM Policy Tool: Measuring Exporters' Exposure to the EU Carbon Border Adjustment and What Domestic Policy Can Do About It" (working paper v0.1, 29 Oct 2026; about 5,450 words of body; three-slide deck in the old format; no policy brief), written from a full reading before any change to the master. Every number in the paper was recomputed: the Türkiye example from `2.Source/cbam_example.py` and the Commission's default-value workbook; the Egypt application from `2.Source/egypt_example.py`, the tool's workbook `Egypt_CBAM_EF_v1.2.xlsx` (sheets EF, EgyptVolumes, Quality_Assurance) and the Commission's workbook. The legal rules the tool implements were checked against the texts themselves (Regulation (EU) 2023/956 and 2025/2083; Implementing Regulations (EU) 2025/2547, 2025/2620 and 2025/2621; Directive (EU) 2023/959; the draft implementing regulation Ares(2026)4841230 and its annex), all retrieved on 31 Oct 2026. Trade volumes were re-queried from Eurostat Comext. The reference check is in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`.

## Summary judgement

The paper does something useful and rare: it turns the EU's border rules into a product-by-product, year-by-year calculation that a finance ministry can audit, and it shows the calculation with public data. The method section is clear, the five equations are right, and the Türkiye example reproduces exactly (every cell of Table 3 and the €660 million, €507 million and €127 million aggregates were recomputed and match; the Eurostat volumes match the live database to the tonne). Its central qualitative point, that for goods far above the benchmark most of the charge arrives in 2026, is correct and worth making.

The Egypt application, which carries the paper's most quoted numbers (€595 million and €177 million a year), is wrong in three ways that compound. The tool deducts the CO₂ bound in urea, which the CBAM rules count as emitted; it counts only half the ammonia in ammonium nitrate; and its EU export volumes are its own estimates, which differ by up to a factor of two from Eurostat's record. Corrected, the charge on the seven products at full phase-in is about €793 million a year at default values and €480 million with verified data at 2025 volumes (€982 million and €445 million at 2024 volumes). The policy story changes with it: verification is still the decisive lever for steel, but urea, now the largest exposure by far, gains little from data, so for Egypt's biggest CBAM export the levers are abatement and a domestic carbon price. The paper also misdescribes the July 2026 proposal on free allocation, overstates how unsettled the conversion of a foreign carbon price is, and does not connect its own steel numbers to paper 1.4's finding on routing. It has no executive summary, no brief and only a three-slide deck.

## Major points

**M1. The urea factor deducts CO₂ that CBAM counts as emitted.** The tool's urea line carries a process term of −0.733 tCO₂ per tonne, the stoichiometric CO₂ bound into urea, so that its CBAM-relevant intensity, with the ammonia feedstock integrated, is 0.49 tCO₂e/t. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2547, Annex II point B.8.2, lets CO₂ used in a product count as not emitted only where its carbon is "permanently chemically bound so that it does not enter the atmosphere under normal use". Urea does not qualify: when applied to soil it releases the CO₂ "that was fixed in the industrial production process" (IPCC 2006, Vol. 4, ch. 11.4). The Commission's own default for Egyptian urea confirms the reading: 1.32 tCO₂/t direct, which is about 0.567 t of ammonia per tonne of urea times Egypt's ammonia default of 1.96, plus the urea plant's own fuel; with the deduction it would be near 0.6. *Fix:* add back 0.733; the tool's urea estimate becomes 1.22 tCO₂e/t against a default of 1.39, and the saving from verified data falls from €73 to €15 per tonne.

**M2. The ammonium nitrate factor counts half its ammonia.** Both nitrogen atoms in ammonium nitrate come from ammonia: one directly, one through nitric acid, itself a CBAM precursor made from ammonia. A tonne of ammonium nitrate therefore embeds 34/80 = 0.425 t of ammonia, not the 17/80 = 0.213 t the tool integrates (its guide gives the molar ratio "17/80"). *Fix:* the estimate rises from 1.53 to 1.93 tCO₂e/t (default 2.39). The money is small (EU imports of 9 kt in 2025), but the error is in the method the paper says "transfers to any exporter".

**M3. The Egypt volumes are the tool's estimates, not the recorded trade.** Table 4 uses the tool's 2024 EU export volumes and the text attributes them to "Eurostat and UN Comtrade". Eurostat's record of EU imports from Egypt in 2024 differs widely: hot-rolled flat steel 696 kt (tool 364), long products 441 kt (325), clinker 1,041 kt (560), ammonia 320 kt (110), urea 2,557 kt (1,600), ammonium nitrate 7 kt (44); only aluminum agrees (137 kt). The tool's volume sheet builds EU exports from production and export shares, which is reasonable for projections but not for a published table. *Fix:* use Eurostat, the source the Türkiye example uses, for 2025 (the latest full year) and report 2024 as a check. Hot-rolled flat steel imports fell from 696 kt in 2024 to 169 kt in 2025, so the year matters for steel.

**M4. With M1–M3 corrected, the Egypt headline numbers and their policy message change.** Recomputed at €80/tCO₂ and full phase-in, with 2025 volumes (5.4 Mt): €793 million a year at default values and €480 million with verified data where it pays, a saving of €313 million, of which €178 million on steel, €84 million on clinker and €48 million on urea. With 2024 volumes: €982 million and €445 million, a saving of €537 million, €430 million of it on steel. Urea carries €369 million of the default charge and €321 million of the verified one. The paper's "verification first, domestic price second" is right for steel and clinker, but for urea a domestic carbon price credited under Article 9, or abatement, is the only material lever. The abstract, introduction, Egypt section, policy section, conclusion and deck all need the new numbers and the sharper message.

**M5. The steel estimate needs a range, and the tool's own numbers disagree with each other.** The gas-based steel factor fell from 1.63 to 0.73 tCO₂/t between workbook versions 1.1 and 1.2 when natural gas use was set to one plant's reported figure. Three checks: Iran, a large gas-based DRI producer with an EU country default, has a default of 1.94 tCO₂/t for CN 7208; the same workbook's factor for merchant gas-based DRI is 1.38 tCO₂ per tonne of DRI, which cannot be squared with 0.73 per tonne of steel made mostly from DRI; and the factor is per tonne of crude steel, while CBAM charges per tonne of the rolled product, which adds the reheating furnace and yield losses. The 0.73 is plausible for an efficient gas-based shaft furnace, and it sits above the EU's own DRI-route benchmark of 0.481 for CN 7208 (Implementing Regulation 2025/2620, Annex point 5.3), but it is the low end. *Fix:* show the steel result at 0.73, 1.63 and 1.94. The charge on Egyptian steel with verified data is then €16–36 million (2025 volumes) against €193 million at the default, so the conclusion that verification pays survives; the size does not, as the paper already says.

**M6. The July 2026 proposal on free allocation is misdescribed.** Table 1's note says COM(2026) 616 "would keep 15 percent of the phased-out free allocation from 2028 and end it in 2038". The proposal sets a slower CBAM factor from 2028 (91.5 percent in 2028, 59 percent in 2030, 27 percent in 2033), keeps 15 percent of free allocation in 2034–2037 and ends it in 2038. *Fix:* state the schedule correctly and compute its effect: the charge on Turkish hot-rolled steel at the default would be €188 instead of €196 per tonne in 2030 and €236 instead of €252 in 2034–2037, reaching €252 only in 2038. Show it in the figure.

**M7. The free-allocation adjustment and the credit rule need their assumptions stated, and one statement is out of date.** (a) Implementing Regulation 2025/2620 (Annex points 3–4) multiplies the benchmark by the CBAM factor *and* the cross-sectoral correction factor; the tool and the example omit the latter (it is set to 1). (b) For declarants using actual data the adjustment is built from process benchmarks (Column A) and the free allocation embedded in precursors, not from the default benchmark (Column B); the "actual data" columns of Table 3 use Column B as an approximation. Both affect only 2026–2033. (c) The paper says how the Commission will convert a price paid into certificates, "and how that conversion interacts with the free-allocation adjustment before 2034", is unsettled. The draft implementing regulation, Article 6, already gives the formula: the reduction is the effective carbon price per tonne of good divided by the certificate reference price, times the quantity, with no adjustment for free allocation. That is exactly the deduction $\tau^d e_i$ in the paper's charge equation. The draft is not adopted (in the Commission's adoption workflow at 31 Oct 2026), so the paper should say the example follows the draft. The floor at zero in @eq-charge is the paper's assumption (the draft has no explicit floor); it binds only for aluminum in 2026.

**M8. The paper does not connect its steel numbers to routing, which paper 1.4 analyzes.** Egypt's steelmakers run both gas-based DRI and scrap-based electric furnaces. Under plant-level measurement a firm can send scrap-based steel (0.16 tCO₂/t in the tool) to the EU and the rest elsewhere, so the verified intensity of Egypt's EU exports can fall well below its production average with no change in emissions. Paper 1.4 shows that measuring by exporting country and sector removes the gain from routing, and that a feebate, the domestic instrument most likely to survive exporters' politics, earns no credit under the draft rules (Art. 8(1)(b)(ii)). A tool that recommends verification first should say that part of what verification "saves" may be routing, and that the export-weighted estimate is the right measure of a country's position. *Fix:* add this to the Egypt section and the policy section, and link to 1.4.

**M9. The contribution is not stated against the closest prior work.** The closest prior work is not the trade-model literature but calculations of CBAM liabilities by country from default values, notably Sandbag (2024), which computes CBAM fees on imports from China and others at full phase-in, including a resource-shuffling scenario; and paper 1.4, which calibrates charges across thirteen exporters from the same default values. The tool adds three things: bottom-up estimates of what verified data would show, set against the defaults; the year-by-year path with the free-allocation adjustment and both phase-out schedules; and the split of a domestic price's effect into abatement and credit. Say so in the introduction.

**M10. The strongest objection is not stated: bottom-up technology averages are not verified plant data.** A verified declaration follows the monitoring rules of Implementing Regulation 2025/2547 for each installation, with its own precursors, boundaries and measurement; a technology average with engineering ratios can miss in either direction (M5). The "saving from verification" is therefore the saving for a plant at the estimated technology average, and the default's mark-up (30 percent) is the margin for error before verification stops paying. *Fix:* state the objection in the limits section and answer it: the tool reports ranges and confidence flags, the conclusions that survive the ranges are stated as such, and the first use is to decide which plants to pilot verification at.

**M11. The aluminum row is summed at the wrong value.** The tool's aluminum estimate (2.51 tCO₂e/t) is above the marked-up default (2.43), so a producer near the estimate would declare the default. The €177 million total adds the higher charge. *Fix:* sum the lower of the two for each product ("best declaration"). The difference is about €1 million.

## Minor points

1. Indirect emissions for cement and fertilizers are cited to the default-value regulation; the rule is Regulation 2023/956, Annex II (goods for which only direct emissions count).
2. The default values are to be "revised in 2027 at the latest" (2025/2621, Art. 2), not "review due by 2027". Use 1.4's wording.
3. The 50-tonne threshold (Regulation 2025/2083, Annex VII) is missing from the rules table; importers below it owe nothing.
4. "In draft on 29 October 2026": update the status to 31 Oct 2026 (adoption workflow, not adopted), as in 1.4.
5. The process-emission semi-elasticities "fitted to published abatement-cost data" have no source. State them as the tool's assumptions.
6. "In most exporters the larger sum" (revenue on other sales versus the CBAM credit) is unsourced. True for Türkiye and Egypt, where EU sales are a minority of output; say that, or cut.
7. Egypt's steel routes ("no blast furnaces since 2021") need a source; the integrated plant at Helwan was put into liquidation in January 2021.
8. Old numbering: the bib header says "paper 4.5", both scripts say "Paper 4.5", and the deck's series line says "CPAT Companion Paper No. 6.2". The README still describes v0.1.
9. The bib has duplicate entries (CFPB61, CFPB14) and a dozen uncited ones; StrettonWP11 and StrettonWP14 cite version 0.3 (both are now v0.4). Key names differ from 1.4 for the same acts (EUETSDirective, EU2026ETSProposal); use 1.4's verified entry for Directive 2023/959.
10. "Table 4 ... seven products, which account for 3.1 of the 3.6 million tonnes": tool volumes; replace with Eurostat (M3).
11. @eq-split's example (−32.5 percent, 22.5 points from the credit) is right; say it holds at full phase-in only.
12. The paper refers to "paper 1.3" for carbon-price recognition in the README; it is 1.4.
13. The first italic line does not link to a brief, and there is no executive summary.

## What the brief needs

There is no brief. Write Climate Fiscal Policy Brief No. 6.2 for finance and trade ministries in exporting countries and the development-bank teams that advise them: how large the charge is and when it arrives (Türkiye, public data); the three levers (verified data, abatement, a domestic carbon price) and what each is worth; the Egypt application with corrected numbers, showing that the right lever differs by product; why a rebated domestic price or feebate earns no credit today (1.4); what to do. One table (Egypt) and the Türkiye charge-path figure, with the working paper's numbers. No equations.

## What the deck needs

Three slides in the old format. Rebuild at about ten on `make_deck.py`: title; the charge is large and arrives early; what the tool computes; the charge path for Turkish steel; what each lever is worth; Egypt: the gap to defaults by product; Egypt: steel range; why a rebated price earns no credit; limits; what to do.

## Disposition

All major and minor points are addressed in working paper v0.2, the new brief v1.0 and the ten-slide deck (31 Oct 2026). The Egypt table now uses Eurostat volumes and the tool's factors corrected to the CBAM rules (M1–M4, M11); the steel range is shown (M5); the proposal, the credit formula and the free-allocation assumptions are stated correctly (M6, M7); routing and 1.4's country-sector finding are in the Egypt and policy sections (M8); the contribution and the strongest objection are stated (M9, M10). The tool's workbook itself was not changed (it is outside this folder's scope): the corrections are applied in `2.Source/egypt_example.py` and should be carried back into the tool (`Egypt_CBAM_EF` v1.3). The cross-sectoral correction factor is set to 1 by assumption, stated in the paper.
