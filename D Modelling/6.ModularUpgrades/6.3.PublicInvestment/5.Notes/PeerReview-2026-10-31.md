# Peer review of 6.3, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of "Public Investment in CPAT: Investment Needs and the Efficiency of Public Spending" (v0.1, 29 Oct 2026, about 6,000 words of body, drafted by an agent and not reviewed before), written from a full reading and before any change to the master. Every number in Tables 1 and 2 and in the text of the worked example was recomputed by running `2.Source/investment_lenses.py` and `2.Source/sids_worked_example.py` (the script's self-checks pass and its CSV output is unchanged). Every derivation in Appendix A was redone by hand. CPAT's cost-of-capital setting and levelized-cost convention were checked in a fresh clone of the public repository `cpmodelai/cpat_ai` (commit 108f976): `excel_ai/mitigation/docs/methods/Power.md` sections 1.4, 1.5 and 6, and `docs/CPAT_full_documentation_qmd/03_Mitigation.qmd`. The country runs of Section 6 (Table 3 and the Uganda SISEPUEDE figures) come from two private repositories that are not available in this session, so they could only be checked for internal consistency, not recomputed. The paper had no policy brief; this review also says what the brief and the deck need.

## Summary judgement

The paper asks a good question and mostly answers it. Its two extensions are coherent: an investment-needs layer that prices the same physical capital at a market rate, a social rate and a social rate with co-benefits, and a public-spending test (the marginal value of public funds against the marginal cost of public funds) that puts capital, operating and contract routes on one fiscal ledger. The worked example is correct in every number I recomputed, and its main lesson, that the island's problem is liquidity, debt statistics and hidden contract liabilities rather than value, is right and useful. The problems are of four kinds. (i) The headline link to CPAT rests on a wrong statement of CPAT's default cost of capital. (ii) Two headline sentences overclaim what the worked example shows: the plant does not "fail the budget" on the seven-year loan, and the guaranteed power purchase agreement does not "rescue" it. (iii) Proposition 4 is stated without a condition its own proof needs. (iv) Several numbers are attributed to sources that do not contain them. The paper also lacks an executive summary, a brief and a ten-slide deck.

## Major points

**M1. CPAT's default cost of capital is stated wrongly, and the paper's headline depends on it.** The abstract, introduction, Section 2, the commentary on Proposition 1, Section 6 and the conclusion all say that CPAT assumes a cost of capital of 7.5, 10, 12.5 and 15 percent by income group, and that the break-even rate of 12.6 percent is "the rate the tool already assumes for lower-middle-income countries". The 2023 documentation does say the WACC is "by default income-dependent" (`03_Mitigation.qmd`, power chapter). But the model's setting (`D_WACCSource` = "Tech", `methods/Power.md` §1.4 and §2.5) is a country real cost of capital for 2022, from a calculator built on a real risk-free rate, country risk premiums and default spreads (7.5 percent where a country is missing), plus a technology premium by income group; for lower-middle-income countries the premium is about 2.5 points for fossil plants and 2.9 points for wind and solar (§6). The income-group rates are an option ("Income\*"). This was also found by the 5.1 review (31 Oct 2026, point M3). *Fix:* state both settings and which one is the default. Re-state the link: the stylized asset's break-even of 12.6 percent coincides with the optional income-group rate for lower-middle-income countries (12.5 percent); under the default setting, a lower-middle-income country's solar asset crosses the break-even when its country rate exceeds about 9.7 percent (12.6 less the 2.9-point premium; recomputed). Uganda's paragraph must say "CPAT's income-group option" rather than "the default".

**M2. The worked example's headline overclaims.** The abstract and Table 2's title say the plant "fails the budget" on the seven-year loan and that a concessional loan or a guaranteed PPA "rescues it". Table 2 says otherwise. On the seven-year loan the plant passes the public-money test with room to spare: its MVPF is 4.18, its net benefit at a cost of public funds of 1.2 is \$93 million, and even with a liquidity premium ($\lambda_t = 2$ in years 1–7) it is \$28 million. What fails is cash: the budget runs \$14.6 million a year short for seven years (0.7 percent of GDP a year), and headline debt rises by 6 percent of GDP. The guaranteed PPA does not close the cash gap (it is still −\$4.0 million a year) and has a lower net benefit than the short loan at constant $\lambda$ (\$69 million against \$93 million); it keeps the debt off the headline at the price of a committed liability of 7.6 percent of GDP. Only concessional finance puts cash into the budget from year one. *Fix:* re-word the abstract, the table title and the text: the plant is worth building on every route; the seven-year loan opens a cash gap the budget cannot carry; concessional finance closes it; a guaranteed PPA narrows it and moves the debt off the books into a contract liability of similar size.

**M3. Proposition 4 needs a condition.** It states that, if the producer's WACC $w$ exceeds the government's rate $i_g$ under take-or-pay, $L^{PPA} > K + PV_{i_g}(OM) + PV_{i_g}(\mathrm{rep})$. The proof in Appendix A shows this only for the capital term, and then concedes that the replacement term, discounted at $w$ inside the tariff and at $i_g$ on the right-hand side, goes the other way, so the inequality holds "whenever the capital term's markup exceeds that difference". As stated, the proposition is false in general (a plant with a large late replacement and a WACC barely above $i_g$ is a counterexample). *Fix:* state it for a plant without mid-life replacements, where it holds exactly, and add the condition for the general case; note that it holds in the worked example, by about \$31 million unguaranteed and only about \$5 million guaranteed (recomputed from Table 2: committed payments 1.48 and 1.27 times capital against \$147 million of capital, operation and replacement valued at 8 percent).

**M4. Several numbers are not in the cited sources.** Checked against the sources (details in `BibCheck-2026-10-31.md`):
- "roughly 6.5 percent of their GDP" for developing countries other than China is not in the expert group's third report [@IHLEG2024]; the report gives \$2.3–2.5 trillion a year by 2030 for these countries but no share of GDP. Cut.
- "The cost of capital in emerging and developing economies has been two to three times that in advanced economies" [@IEA2022CoC]. The IEA's 2024 report, cited by papers 3.1 and 3.2, says the cost of capital for utility-scale solar in these economies is "well over twice" that in advanced economies. Use that wording and source, so that section 3 and section 6 agree.
- Discount rates of published estimates: "6 percent [@RozenbergFay2019]" is not confirmed; "7 percent [@Kalra2023]" is (SISEPUEDE discounts at a fixed 7 percent, 2019 dollars). Keep 7 percent; cut the 6 percent attribution.
- SISEPUEDE's results: the paper says co-benefits are "about as large as the fuel savings" and "roughly triple the net benefit again". The report gives fuel savings of \$900 billion, avoided pollution \$500 billion and other health, safety and productivity gains \$1 trillion, against \$1.3 trillion of investment, for a net benefit of \$2.7 trillion; counting only financial costs and benefits, the net benefit is \$700 billion. So co-benefits are larger than fuel savings, and counting them nearly quadruples the net benefit. Re-word with these numbers.
- @AuriolWarlters2012: the averages of 1.2 (all instruments) and 1.1 (cheapest instrument, often the VAT) are in the abstract; "the informal sector's size, not national income, predicts the cost" is not. Cut that clause.
- "Frontier studies put developing countries' health and education output at roughly 0.8 to 0.9 of best practice" [@HerreraPang2005; @GuptaVerhoeven2001]: neither abstract gives such a range. Re-word to what they show.
- Recurrent-cost coefficients (1–2 percent for solar, 10–20 percent for clinics and schools) have no source. State them as the author's illustrative values, and add the sourced aggregate: operations and maintenance add 2.7 percent of GDP a year to the 4.5 percent capital need in @RozenbergFay2019.

**M5. Lens (c) is defined two ways.** Equation (6) defines lens (c) as the social rate plus local co-benefits and carbon at the shadow price. Table 3's lens (c) counts local air-pollution co-benefits only. And Section 7 says the country allocations "still use [the stylized 13.3 cents] for buildings and industry", which cannot be checked without the workbook and is hard to square with Table 3: at 6 percent, Kenya's incremental investment of about \$1.7 billion has a capital charge of about \$134 million against a net cost of \$68 million, which implies savings of about 4 cents a year per dollar, not 13.3. *Fix:* label Table 3's lens (c) as "local co-benefits, no carbon"; cut the claim about the tool's savings rate and say instead that the tool's savings and co-benefit rates per dollar are calibrated, not observed.

**M6. The capital-recovery convention moves the break-even by two points.** The README flags this as open. CPAT's levelized cost divides by a discounted lifetime summed from year 0 (an annuity due, `methods/Power.md` §1.5), which is equivalent to a capital recovery factor of $\mathrm{CRF}(r,n)/(1+r)$; the paper uses the end-of-year factor. Under CPAT's convention the break-even rate for the stylized asset is 14.8 percent, not 12.6 (recomputed). *Fix:* say which convention the paper uses and why (payments start a year after commissioning, as in project finance), report the alternative, and recommend that the extension adopt one convention with CPAT.

**M7. The contribution and the strongest objection need stating.** The paper names three literatures but does not say what is new against the closest work. The closest prior work is (a) investment-needs studies that already vary the discount rate [@RozenbergFay2019; @Kalra2023]; (b) integrated-model studies of the cost of capital [@Ameli2021; @Calcaterra2024]; and (c) the MVPF framework [@HendrenSprungKeyser2020]. What is new is combining them inside a fiscal model ministries use: the cost of capital by country and technology as an input to investment needs, the three lenses reported side by side, and a public-money test that puts financing routes, delivery haircuts and contract liabilities on one ledger. The strongest objection is that the lenses only restate a discount-rate sensitivity, and that Table 1, with one savings rate for every group, is Proposition 1 in disguise. The answer is that the lenses separate two jobs the discount rate is asked to do (pricing finance and valuing time), and their gap measures what de-risking is worth; and that Table 1 is an illustration of scale, not an estimate. *Fix:* add both to the introduction and to the limits section; label Table 1 as an illustration.

**M8. Proposition 3's statement is garbled.** "A rule with one constant $\lambda$ therefore ranks a short loan at the social rate as cheaper than a long one whenever the loan rate exceeds the social rate" mixes the loan and the discount rate. The result (proved correctly in Appendix A) is: when budget costs are discounted at a social rate below the loan rate, a constant $\lambda$ ranks the shorter loan as cheaper, because the spread is paid for fewer years. *Fix:* re-word.

**M9. The executive summary, the brief and the deck are missing.** *Fix:* add an executive summary as chapter 1 (no equations; numbers from Tables 1–3); write a brief for finance ministries and the development-bank teams who run CPAT (what the cost of capital does to the case for clean capital, why liquidity rather than value is the island's problem, what to ask before signing a PPA); build a deck of about ten slides on the new builder.

## Minor points

1. "A dollar of such capital nets \$43 a year per \$1,000" mixes units. Say "each \$1,000 of capital nets \$43 a year".
2. "The same asset is a good investment in Germany and a bad one in Zambia" rests on the income-group option (M1) and on Zambia's income classification, which has changed; cut.
3. Pooling and refinancing: paper 3.2 refinances the senior debt at a blended 5.65 percent and cuts the cost of power by 19 percent; the paper says "a blended rate near 5.7 percent instead of 10". Use 3.2's numbers and say that the rate applies to the refinanced senior debt.
4. The shadow price of carbon in the worked example (\$40 a ton in 2020 rising 2.25 percent a year) is the low end of the High-Level Commission's range (\$40–80 by 2020, \$50–100 by 2030), which the World Bank's 2024 note follows. Say so.
5. Appendix B says the debt framework adds "35 percent of public-private partnership capital above 3 percent of GDP". The framework adds 35 percent of the whole PPP capital stock once the stock exceeds 3 percent of GDP (the script computes 2.1 percent of GDP, 35 percent of 6). Re-word.
6. The IMF's efficiency gaps (40, 27 and 13 percent) are gaps in output relative to an efficient frontier, not "value lost". Re-word.
7. "Observed renewable WACCs cover fewer than a hundred countries" is unsourced. @Steffen2020 covers 46 countries. Re-word.
8. Equations carry hard-coded numbers "(1)"–"(12)" and tables are numbered in captions. Use `{#eq-}` and `{#tbl-}` labels, and refer to sections by name, so the cross-reference filter numbers them.
9. The first italic line should link to the brief; the disclaimer should follow 5.1 and 1.1.
10. Bibliography: CPATDocs2025 is a re-labelled copy of the CPAT documentation; use the series' verified entries for the documentation (CPATTeam2024) and the repository (CPATAI2026). Black et al. (2023): the fourth author is listed by Crossref as Vernon-Lin. The IRENA URL points to June; the report is in the July 2025 folder.
11. `2.Source/country_runs.py` calls itself "Paper 4.6", an old number. Fix the docstring.
12. Acronyms: WACC, PPA, MVPF, ENPV, NDC, PIMA, MIGA. Define once; spell out those used fewer than five times (MIGA, PIMA, NDC).

## Closest prior work and the contribution

Stated in M7. The contribution is real but modest in theory and practical in use: the propositions are short results (two of them standard), and their value is that they put known facts (the cost of capital decides the case for clean capital; PPPs do not free public funds, @EngelFischerGaletovic2013; tenor eases liquidity, not solvency) into a ministry's model with numbers attached. The paper should say this plainly and not claim more.
