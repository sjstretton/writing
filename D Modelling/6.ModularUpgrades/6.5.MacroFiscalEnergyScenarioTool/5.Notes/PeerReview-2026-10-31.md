# Peer review of 6.5, working paper v0.1 (31 Oct 2026)

A referee-style review of *The Macro-Fiscal-Energy Scenario Tool: Linking Energy Investment and Carbon Pricing to Growth, Revenue and Debt in One Workbook*, working paper v0.1 (29 Oct 2026, about 6,300 words of body), written before the v0.2 revision and the first policy brief (v1.0).

What was checked, and how. Every equation was re-derived or checked against its published source. The pricing screen (equations 2 and 3, Table 4) was recomputed from the formulas and the stylized parameters in `2.Source/stylized_run.py`: every number in Table 4 reproduces. The stylized macro results (Table 3) were checked against `2.Source/stylized_run_output.txt` (the private tools repository that holds the lite models is not reachable from this session, so the macro runs could not be re-run; the derived statements in the text were recomputed from the table). Every Kenya number was checked against the tool's own output (`2.Source/kenya_run_output.txt`) and, where a public counterpart exists, against public sources: the joint IMF-World Bank debt sustainability analysis of December 2023 (IMF Country Report No. 24/13), the 2018 LIC-DSF Guidance Note and its 2024 Supplement, the IMF World Economic Outlook database, World Bank International Debt Statistics and World Development Indicators, Ember's yearly electricity data and Kenya's updated NDC of December 2020. The README's clearance was followed: only the Kenya run is used, as illustrative tool results; no other country's results, no fiscal-risk dashboard inputs, no internal code names, no Public Finance Review, not-for-citation or draft-for-discussion material.

## Summary judgement

The paper has a clear question (can the investment need, feasible carbon pricing, the growth effect and the debt test be run as one consistent, inspectable scenario for any country?) and a clear answer (yes, at the cost of using simplified "lite" versions of the parent models; and the scenario says that financing terms and investment efficiency matter more than the program's size, while the binding political limit decides whether pricing can pay). The description of the tool is careful and honest about what the lite models drop. The pricing screen is correct as implemented, and the Kenya run is internally consistent: every derived number (direct need 0.38 + 0.3 × 0.73 = 0.60 percent of GDP; 0.60/(1 − 0.265) = 0.82; financing gap 0.49 − 0.05 = 0.44; debt change 74.6 − 67.3 = 7.2 points) reproduces.

Five things keep it below the standard. (i) The Kenya debt comparison is easy to misread: the "67.3 percent without the program" in 2050 is Kenya's 2024 debt ratio held flat by construction, while the IMF projects Kenya's debt at 71 to 74 percent of GDP through 2030 with no climate program at all. (ii) The paper never sets the tool's Kenya debt results against the published IMF-World Bank DSA, which is the obvious check and mostly confirms them (high risk, medium capacity, PV of public debt above 55 percent) but contradicts the tool on the Composite Indicator (3.01 against the tool's 2.73) and shows the main thing the lite debt test cannot see: exchange-rate depreciation drove the latest upward revision of Kenya's debt metrics. (iii) The source of the gap between the two macro families is explained two different ways in two sections. (iv) The contribution is stated only against the parent models, not against the closest integrated work (the World Bank's climate-extended macro-structural model, the IMF's 2023 Fiscal Monitor on the debt cost of net zero policy mixes) or the empirical evidence on public investment that the calibration rests on. (v) The paper has no executive summary, no brief, and its results are asserted rather than stated with their conditions.

## Major points

**M1. The Kenya "without the program" debt path is the 2024 ratio held flat; say so, and set it against the IMF's projection.** Table 5 reports public debt in 2050 of 74.6 percent of GDP with the program and 67.3 without. The tool's output gives `debt_2024_pct_gdp: 67.34` and `debt_change_2050_pp: 7.24`, so the no-program path is the 2024 ratio, consistent with the baseline described in the tool section ("holds the country's 2024 fiscal position"). A reader will take 67.3 as a projection. The IMF World Economic Outlook database (accessed 31 Oct 2026) puts Kenya's general government gross debt at 67.3 percent of GDP in 2024 (matching the tool's starting point) but projects 71.3 in 2025 and about 74 percent in 2027–2030. *Fix:* label the comparison as "against debt held at its 2024 level", state the IMF projection, and say that the 7.2 points are the program's addition to whatever path debt follows, not a forecast of Kenya's debt.

**M2. Check the tool's debt test against the published DSA.** The joint IMF-World Bank DSA of December 2023 (IMF Country Report No. 24/13) rates Kenya's risk of external and overall debt distress as high, debt carrying capacity as medium with a Composite Indicator of 3.01 (2.98 and 3.02 in the two previous vintages; the cut-off for strong is 3.05), and finds the PV of public debt above the 55 percent benchmark through 2029, peaking at 68 percent of GDP in 2023. The tool agrees on the rating (High before and with the program), on capacity (medium) and on the level of the PV of public debt (66.4 percent at its 2025–2034 peak). It disagrees on the Composite Indicator (2.73), because the build has no trade data and assumes imports of 40 percent of GDP; Kenya's imports of goods and services were 22–24 percent of GDP in 2022–2025 (World Development Indicators). The paper's own sensitivity (imports near 20 percent put the indicator at about 3.07, "just into the strong band") then overshoots the published 3.01. *Fix:* report the DSA's numbers beside the tool's, say the published Composite Indicator is the one to use, and treat the tool's indicator as a check that needs country trade data.

**M3. The debt equation needs its exchange-rate term, and the strongest objection needs stating.** The debt dynamics equation in the debt-test section, $d_t = d_{t-1}(1+i)/((1+g)(1+\pi)) - pb_t + \text{other flows}$, is right for debt in domestic currency [Escolano 2010, equations 1–5, checked]. For foreign-currency debt, which is most of Kenya's external debt, the ratio also moves with the exchange rate. The December 2023 DSA attributes the upward revision of Kenya's debt metrics to "projected REER depreciation in 2023–2024", higher interest costs and more program financing. DIGNAD-lite has no real exchange rate; whether the lite debt test carries a valuation term cannot be seen in the paper. *Fix:* write the equation with a foreign-currency share and a depreciation term; state that the lite models hold the real exchange rate fixed; make this the strongest objection to the Kenya run (the tool misses the shock that actually moved Kenya's debt) and say what would answer it (a depreciation stress on the lite debt path, as the DSA's standard stress tests do).

**M4. The source of the gap between the two macro families is explained inconsistently.** The integration section says the gap comes from labor (GIMF-lite lets employment respond; with labor nearly fixed the two "give similar GDP gains, so the remaining difference is a model choice"). The Kenya section says the GIMF-lite cross-check is higher (4.6 against 0.3 percent of GDP in 2050) "because it credits energy savings of 1.7 percent of GDP by 2050 and the private investment that comes with the program", and then refers back to the integration section for the explanation. Both channels are real, but the reader is told two stories. *Fix:* state once, in the integration section, that the gap has three sources (energy savings and private investment, which only GIMF-lite credits, and the labor response), and in the Kenya section say which dominates there (energy savings: 1.7 percent of GDP of the 4.6).

**M5. The contribution needs stating against the closest prior work and the evidence.**

- *Integrated climate-macro-fiscal tools.* The World Bank has extended its macro-structural model (MFMod) with climate policies and impacts for use by ministry staff, starting with Pakistan [Burns, Jooste and Schwerhoff 2021]. The IMF's October 2023 Fiscal Monitor estimates the debt cost of net zero policy mixes: relying mostly on spending could raise public debt by 45–50 percent of GDP by 2050 for a representative large-emitting country, while a mix with carbon pricing holds the rise to 10–15 percent of GDP in an indicative advanced-economy scenario. The tool's result for Kenya (7 points of GDP for a small program, little pricing revenue) is a low-income counterpart, and its stylized earmarking result agrees in sign with the Fiscal Monitor. *Fix:* add both; state the contribution as integration at country level, for every country, with the pricing screen and the payer split inside the chain, in an open workbook.
- *Evidence on the parameters that drive the results.* The stylized run says efficiency and the return on public capital matter most. The meta-analysis of Bom and Ligthart (2014; 578 estimates from 68 studies) puts the long-run output elasticity of core public capital at about 0.12 (0.083 short run); the IMF (2015) finds average public investment inefficiency of around 30 percent; Furceri and Li (2017) estimate a short-run public investment multiplier of about 0.2 in developing economies, larger where investment efficiency is higher; Abiad, Furceri and Topalova (2016) find public investment more effective where efficiency is higher and when debt financed. These support the paper's ranking and bound its calibration (GIMF-lite's first-year multiplier is 0.32). *Fix:* cite them where the calibration and the efficiency result are discussed.
- *CPAT.* Cite the CPAT documentation and Black et al. (2023) with the same wording as papers 5.1–5.4 ("more than 180 countries"); the v0.1 entry `CPATdocs` points to an old URL with a different title.

**M6. Results are asserted, not stated with their conditions.** The paper standard asks for propositions with conditions. For a tool paper, the right form is a small number of numbered results, each with the conditions under which the run shows it. *Fix:* state three results in the stylized run: (1) financing changes the debt path more than the growth path (conditions: GIMF-lite or DIGNAD-lite at default calibration; a one-off program); (2) investment efficiency scales the growth gain roughly in proportion and leaves debt unchanged (efficiency enters only the capital accumulation equation); (3) the binding feasibility limit decides whether pricing raises revenue (power sector, static screen).

**M7. The concentrated-loser metric rests on an assumption that should be stated, and it matters.** In equation (3) a high-emitting generator recovers the share $\phi$ of its own carbon cost ($\tau e_{hi}\phi$), while the residential tariff rises by $\phi e_{avg}\tau$, the pass-through applied at grid-average intensity. These are consistent only if the generator is compensated for a share of its own cost (for example under a cost-plus contract), not if all generators receive the same tariff increase. If high emitters recover only $\phi e_{avg}\tau$ per MWh, their unrecovered cost is $\tau(e_{hi} - \phi e_{avg})$, and in the stylized sector the $F_3$ limit binds at a tax of USD 22 per tonne rather than 35, with 18 rather than 25 percent abatement, and the best mix becomes a pure feebate (USD 38, 24.5 percent) (recomputed: `2.Source/pricing_screen.py`). The feebate is unaffected. *Fix:* state the assumption, report the alternative, and note that it strengthens the paper's conclusion that the concentrated-loser limit decides the feasible price.

**M8. The lite models are validated against theory and against each other, not against their parents.** The checks in the integration section (Ricardian limit, Walras's law, horizon independence, Excel against Python) show that the lite models are solved correctly. They do not show that GIMF-lite reproduces GIMF's published simulation properties [Anderson et al. 2013] or that DIGNAD-lite reproduces the DIG scale-up results [Buffie et al. 2012]. *Fix:* say so plainly in the limits section and name the comparison that would answer it (the parents' published public-investment simulations, at the parents' calibrations).

**M9. No executive summary, no brief, and the Kenya section's recommendations outrun the run.** "Closing the efficiency gap is worth about 0.2 percent of GDP a year" is right arithmetic (0.82 − 0.60) but is a saving on the budget need, not a GDP gain; say so. The recommendation that Kenya's land-use and non-CO₂ needs be financed by grants and concessional loans is consistent with Kenya's own NDC, which asks for international support for 87 percent of its USD 62 billion mitigation and adaptation cost (updated NDC, December 2020); cite it. *Fix:* add the executive summary as chapter 1 and write the brief.

## Minor points

1. Section cross-references by number ("Section 5 explains", "Section 8") should be by name, so that the brief's links and renumbering survive.
2. The roadmap paragraph in the introduction is a full paragraph; cut it to one sentence or drop it.
3. Table 1 calls the investment module "the national mitigation investment model" and the text calls it "the investment model" and "the investment module": use one term ("the investment model").
4. LIC-DSF-lite: "five indicators compared with thresholds" — in the framework four external indicators have thresholds and the PV of total public debt has a benchmark (35/55/70 percent of GDP for weak, medium and strong capacity; checked in the 2018 Guidance Note, Table 7). The external thresholds are 30/40/55 (PV of external debt to GDP), 140/180/240 (to exports), 10/15/21 (debt service to exports) and 14/18/23 (to revenue) (Table 6); the Composite Indicator cut-offs are 2.69 and 3.05 (Table 5). All checked; give the full set once.
5. The guidance has a 2024 Supplement (IMF Policy Paper 2024/039) that leaves the 2018 note in force except where modified; cite both.
6. Kenya's efficiency gap: v0.1 says "Kenya's PEFA score on public investment management, 2.5 out of 4". The PEFA report could not be read (the PEFA site refused access from this session), so the score is the tool's input, not a verified fact. Report the 26.5 percent gap as the tool's input, derived from PEFA scoring, and compare it with the IMF's average of around 30 percent.
7. Kenya's emissions: the tool's current-trends path (121 MtCO₂e in 2030) is below the NDC's business-as-usual of 143 MtCO₂e, and its net zero path (89 MtCO₂e) is below the NDC's 2030 target (32 percent below 143, about 97). Say so, since it shows the program is more ambitious than the NDC.
8. Kenya's power: "already mostly renewable" — Ember: 91.5 percent of generation in 2024 (90.0 percent in 2025), fossil (oil) generation 1.13 TWh with 0.74 MtCO₂ in 2024. At USD 88 per tonne that base yields about 0.05 percent of GDP, which confirms the tool's revenue. Give the numbers; note Kenya has no coal plants.
9. Kenya's multilateral share (56 percent of external public debt) checks against International Debt Statistics: USD 20.0 billion of USD 35.6 billion in 2024 (51.8 percent in 2023). Say which year.
10. "Pricing revenue ... about 5 percent of the need to 2050": in 2030 the revenue covers about a tenth of that year's need (0.05 of 0.49); the 5 percent is the cumulative share to 2050. Give both.
11. "If instead a recent tariff protest makes the government cap the first-year tariff rise at 2 percent": an invented story; state the case as a tighter limit on the visible price.
12. The stylized earmark (0.5 percent of GDP a year) is ten times Kenya's feasible pricing revenue; say so where the two are compared.
13. Paper 6.3 reports larger investment totals for Kenya from a different method (a global estimate shared among countries); say once that the two answer different questions, so readers do not compare them.
14. `2.Source/kenya_run.py` and `stylized_run.py` describe themselves as "Paper 5.2" (the paper's old number): fix.
15. The abstract (about 210 words) runs over; the brief's abstract should be about 100 words.
16. The deck (v0.1) has four slides on the old builder; it needs about ten, each title a full sentence, with sources.

## What the brief and the deck need

The brief (new, v1.0) should speak to finance ministries and development-bank teams deciding how to finance a climate investment program: what the tool can tell them, the three lessons of the stylized run, what Kenya shows (with the DSA check), and what to do (use it to compare packages, replace defaults in order, treat model disagreement as a finding, do not use it in place of a DSA or a CPAT run). One table: the Kenya chain from investment need to debt risk, with the public DSA beside the tool's numbers. The deck should follow the same line in about ten slides: the problem, the chain, the pricing screen, the stylized run (figure), the three results, Kenya's need and pricing, Kenya's debt with the DSA check, the limits, and what to do.

## Disposition (31 Oct 2026)

All major points were addressed in working paper v0.2 and brief v1.0. M1: the comparison is now "against debt held at its 2024 level", with the IMF projection beside it. M2: Table 5 sets the tool against the December 2023 DSA, and the text says the published Composite Indicator is the one to use. M3: the debt equation now has a foreign-currency share and a depreciation term; the fixed real exchange rate is the strongest objection, stated and answered in the Kenya and limits sections. M4: one explanation of the cross-check gap, in the integration section, with energy savings named as the main source for Kenya. M5: the closest prior work and the evidence on public investment added, all verified (BibCheck-2026-10-31.md). M6: three numbered results with conditions. M7: the pass-through assumption stated and the alternative reported (a tax of USD 22 rather than 35, and a pure feebate as the best mix), with a new script, `2.Source/pricing_screen.py`, that reproduces Table 4 without the private repository. M8: stated in the limits section. M9: executive summary added; the efficiency saving re-worded; the NDC cited. Minor points 1–16 fixed. Not resolved: the macro runs could not be re-run (the private tools repository is not reachable from this session), so Table 3 rests on the saved output of 29 Oct 2026; Kenya's PEFA score on public investment management could not be read from the source and is reported only as the tool's input.
