# NORMS.md

The overarching norms for all writing in this repo. Every agent applies these to every piece of work. Detail lives in `H Writing Instructions/Writing Standards/`; this file is the short version that always applies.

## 1. Write for a busy reader

The reader is a busy, intelligent non-specialist with a firehose of other things to read. They are not Stephen. Write more simply and more concisely than feels natural. If a sentence makes the reader work, rewrite it.

## 2. The first sentence of every paragraph carries the argument

A reader who reads only the first sentence of each paragraph should get the whole argument. That sentence states the claim. The rest of the paragraph supports it with evidence, elaboration, an example or a qualification. This is World Bank house practice, and it is the rule for all policy and working-paper prose. (Popular essays may relax it; see `writing-types.md`.)

## 3. Every piece is self-contained

A piece must make sense to someone who has never seen the prompt, the conversation or the other papers. So it must:

- say what the topic is, in plain words, near the start;
- say why it matters: the problem, who has it and what it costs;
- say what the piece adds, and what it concludes.

Never write as if answering a question the reader cannot see. No "as requested", "here is", "this revised version", "as discussed above" (unless it is above), or answers that begin mid-thought.

## 4. Sound like a person, not a model

Writing should be specific, opinionated where the evidence allows, and recognizably Stephen's. Avoid the tells of AI prose listed in `H Writing Instructions/Writing Standards/ai-tells.md`. The worst are: generic openings, empty triads, "it's not X, it's Y" constructions on repeat, hedges that say nothing, summary-paragraph endings that restate the piece, and bullet lists where an argument should be.

## 5. Concrete beats abstract

One real number, with a unit and a year, beats three adjectives. One named country, instrument or institution beats "various contexts". Every abstraction should have an example within a paragraph.

## 6. Use one word for one thing

Define a term once and then use exactly that term every time. Don't switch to synonyms for variety; the reader will think you mean something different. Keep terms and numbers consistent across papers.

## 7. Claims must be true and traceable

- Never invent a citation, number, quote or result.
- Every factual claim headed outward has a source a reader can find.
- Mark anything unverified as `[VERIFY: what and why]`. A gap is better than a confident invention.
- Distinguish what is shown, what is argued and what is assumed.

## 8. Structure follows the paper standard

Working papers follow `H Writing Instructions/Writing Standards/paper-standard.md`, modeled on the World Bank Policy Research Working Paper. Other writing types follow `writing-types.md`. Decks run to about ten slides (8–12, title slide included), each slide's title a full-sentence message; quality matters more than count. Existing shorter decks move to this length when they are next revised.

## 9. Spelling and style

- US English for World Bank and policy work (per `voice.md`). Oxford applications may use UK English. One spelling system per document. *(Open question for Stephen: switch the existing UK-spelled papers to US English? See `TODO.md`.)*
- Active voice with named agents: "the ministry raised the levy", not "the levy was raised".
- Chicago author-date citations.
- Numbers with units and years: "$75/tCO₂ (2030 prices)".

## 10. Respect confidentiality

Nothing marked confidential or World Bank internal goes outward. Anything published carries the standard disclaimer that the views are the author's own.

## 11. Paginate for the reader

PDFs and Word files of papers and briefs follow these page rules. The working-paper format (`Writing Standards/templates/_extensions/working-paper/`) applies them automatically; check them by eye before publishing.

- The title, abstract and disclaimer have the first page to themselves.
- The executive summary (or key messages) starts on a new page, ideally fitting on one page.
- The first section after the executive summary starts on a new page.
- The references (bibliography) and the appendices each start on a new page.
- No orphaned headings: if only about a paragraph of space (roughly a fifth of a page) is left when a new section starts, start it on the next page. Never leave a heading, or a single line of a paragraph, at the foot of a page.

## 12. Less, but finished

A short, finished, excellent paper beats a long, ambitious, unfinished one. Cut scope before cutting quality.
