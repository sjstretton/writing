# Writing Types

Stephen writes in several forms, and each has a different reader and a different test. Decide the type before drafting. The principles in `writing-principles.md` apply to all of them; what changes is the reader, the length, the structure and how much technical apparatus is allowed.

## Summary

| Type | Reader | Length | Structure | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Working paper (World Bank form) | Economists and policy staff in ministries and development banks | 6,000–12,000 words | `paper-standard.md` | Skim test: title, abstract, headings and first sentences give the whole argument |
| Journal article | Referees and academics in the field | Journal limit (often 5,000–8,000) | Paper standard, adapted to the journal | Would a referee know the contribution from the introduction alone? |
| Introductory paper | A newcomer to the area: a new colleague, a student, an assessor | 3,000–5,000 words | Problem → main results → how they fit → where to read more | Could a smart non-specialist explain the area's main results after one read? |
| Concept paper | Bank operational staff | 1,500–2,500 words | Name → one-sentence definition → why it matters → worked example → what changes in practice | Could a reader use the term correctly in a meeting the next day? |
| Position paper | Anyone who wants to know where Stephen stands | 3,000–6,000 words | Position in the title → the case → strongest objection → answer → implications | Does it state the best case against, and answer it? |
| Policy brief | A senior official with five minutes | 2–4 pages | Key messages box → problem → proposal → costs and risks → next steps | Could the official act on the key messages box alone? |
| Slide deck (paper or section) | A meeting, or a reader flicking through before reading the brief | About 8 slides (6–10), title slide included | Title → the problem → the proposal → the main results, one per slide → costs and risks → what to do | Do the slide titles alone, read in order, tell the argument? |
| One-page pitch | A manager or funder | 1 page | Problem, idea, ask | Is the ask in the first three lines? |
| Research proposal | Admissions assessors and prospective supervisors | ~2,500 words (Oxford) | Question → why it matters → gap → approach and data → feasibility → contribution | Is the question answerable in three years, with named data? |
| Popular philosophy essay | An educated general reader | 2,000–6,000 words | Narrative arc: a tension, a turn, a resolution | Does the last paragraph say something the first couldn't? Would a curious friend read to the end? |
| Blog or explainer | Interested public, colleagues on social media | 800–1,500 words | Hook → one idea → example → so what | Could someone retell it in two sentences? |

## Notes by type

### Working papers and journal articles

These are the default for serious work. The World Bank form is the house standard (see `paper-standard.md`): abstract that states the finding, introduction that gives the answer early, and paragraphs that lead with their claim. Use the "Dan" persona in `voice.md` for clarity and the "Clarisa" persona for the introduction's argument.

### Introductory, concept and position papers

These are new forms for this repo (see `H Writing Instructions/PAPERS.md`). They exist to make Stephen's ideas easy to pick up and repeat. Keep the technical apparatus to a minimum and point to the full working paper for proofs and data. A concept paper succeeds when the term spreads; give it a name people will want to say.

### Research proposals

Assessors read many proposals quickly. Lead with the question. Name the data and the method. Show the project fits in three years. Cut anything that signals an unfocused applicant: parked ideas, "contradictory strands", tool or planning language.

### Popular philosophy

Here the goal is accessibility, and the rules relax. A narrative opening is allowed, and the first-sentence rule gives way to an arc. Personal voice and vivid metaphor are welcome if they carry real argument. Still: define every technical term, give concrete examples, and never assume the reader has read the philosophers you cite. Model: "The Vocative Case" (7.3), Stephen's own reference piece.

## Choosing the persona

`H Writing Instructions/Claude + Scripts/Setup Claude/_admin/voice.md` defines two writing personas.

- **Dan** (short words, concise sentences, concrete examples) for briefs, concept papers, explainers and the body of working papers.
- **Clarisa** (*The Economist* at its best; narrative arc, explicit reasoning, confident conclusion) for essays, position papers and introductions.
