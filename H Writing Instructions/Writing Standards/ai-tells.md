# AI Tells to Remove

Prose drafted with AI has recognizable habits. Readers spot them fast and then distrust the whole piece. Check every draft against this list before it is called finished. Several come from Stephen directly and are marked **(S)**.

## 1. Missing context (S)

The most serious failure. AI drafts often read as a reply to a prompt the reader never saw.

- **No motivation.** The piece never says why the topic matters, to whom, or what goes wrong without it.
- **No introduction of the topic itself.** It starts discussing "the mechanism" or "this approach" without saying what it is.
- **Prompt residue.** "Here is…", "As requested…", "This revised version…", "Below I outline…", "Great question".
- **References to an invisible conversation.** "As noted earlier" when nothing was noted; "the user's"; "the supplied documents"; "planning assumption".

**Fix:** the first paragraph must say what the topic is, why it matters and what the piece concludes, for a reader who has seen nothing else.

## 2. Generic rather than human (S)

- **Throat-clearing openings.** "In today's rapidly evolving landscape…", "Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges of our time…".
- **Claims that could appear in any paper.** If a sentence would be equally true in a paper on another topic, cut it or make it specific.
- **No point of view.** Everything balanced, nothing concluded. Say what you think and why.
- **No concrete detail.** No country, no number, no year, no named institution.

## 3. Rhythm and phrasing tics

- **Empty triads.** "Efficient, equitable, and effective." Lists of three adjectives or nouns used for cadence rather than content.
- **"It's not X, it's Y"** and **"not just X, but Y"** used repeatedly for false drama.
- **Em-dash overuse**, especially the "— and that matters" aside.
- **Colon reveals.** "The answer is simple: …", "Here's the thing: …".
- **Rhetorical questions** standing in for claims. "So what does this mean for policy?"
- **Inflated vocabulary.** delve, crucial, pivotal, robust (outside statistics), leverage (as a verb), landscape, realm, tapestry, multifaceted, nuanced, holistic, seamless, underscore, navigate, foster, unlock, game-changer, paradigm shift.
- **Signposting filler.** "It is important to note that", "It is worth mentioning", "Notably", "Importantly", "Interestingly".

## 4. Structural habits

- **Bullet lists where an argument belongs.** Bullets break the logic between points. Use them only for genuinely parallel items.
- **Bold scattered through paragraphs.**
- **Headings on every few lines**, turning prose into a slide deck.
- **The summary ending.** A final paragraph beginning "In conclusion" or "Overall" that restates what was said. End on the implication or the next step instead.
- **Symmetrical sections.** Every section the same length, with the same internal pattern, regardless of how much there is to say.

## 5. Epistemic habits

- **Reflexive hedging.** "may potentially", "could arguably", "to some extent". Hedge only with a stated reason.
- **Confident invention.** Plausible-sounding citations, statistics or quotes that do not exist. The most damaging tell of all. Verify or mark `[VERIFY]`.
- **False balance.** "Some argue X, others argue Y" with no judgement.
- **Overclaiming.** "This framework resolves…" when it addresses.

## Self-check before finishing

1. Read the first paragraph as a stranger. Do you know the topic, why it matters and the conclusion?
2. Read only the first sentences of each paragraph. Is the argument there?
3. Search the draft for the words in section 3.
4. Find every number and citation. Is each one sourced and real?
5. Read the last paragraph. Does it add something, or only repeat?
