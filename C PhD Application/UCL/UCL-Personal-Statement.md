I want to find out how multilateral development banks can use their balance sheets to make ambitious climate policy a government's own best choice. Solar and wind plants are almost all upfront cost, so where capital is dear, clean power is dear. Development banks can cut that cost with guarantees and credit enhancement, but they cannot set a country's power-market rules or carbon price. My research asks how de-risking can reward credible national policy rather than replace it, and at what expected cost to the bank. I will test whether bank support cuts financing costs more where governments already offer long-term power contracts, and whether cheaper capital changes a government's choice only above a threshold set by the political cost of reform.

I have spent my career building the models that such decisions rest on. I am an economist at the World Bank, where I work on climate and fiscal policy tools for finance ministries, including the Climate Policy Assessment Tool (CPAT). Before that I built credit-risk and capital models for investment banks, including Barclays Capital and Credit Suisse, and modelled climate mitigation as a research associate at Cambridge. My working papers include a payment intermediary that guarantees clean-power contracts rather than debt, and a measure of carbon pricing by the emissions it cuts. What I need now is doctoral training in estimating the cost of capital and in real-options methods.

UCL ISR is where this question is already being asked. Professor Ameli and Professor Grubb, with colleagues, showed in *Nature Communications* (2021) that higher financing costs trap developing economies in too little clean investment. My proposal starts from that result and asks what development banks can do about it. Professor Ameli's work on climate finance would anchor the financial side. Professor Grubb's *Planetary Economics* treats pricing, strategic investment and innovation as complements, the frame I use, and his work on UK electricity market reform bears on the contracts for difference at the centre of my first hypothesis.
