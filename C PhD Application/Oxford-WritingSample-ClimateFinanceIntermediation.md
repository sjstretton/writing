# Guarantee the Contract, Not the Debt: A Payment Intermediary for Clean Power

Stephen J. Stretton[^1]

*Word count (main text, excluding bibliography and footnotes): 1,983*

## 1. Introduction

Clean power in developing countries costs too much because its finance costs too much. A solar plant has no fuel bill, so the price of its electricity is mostly the repayment of the capital that built it: a stylized plant that needs 3.9 US cents per kilowatt-hour at a 5 percent real cost of capital needs 6.5 cents at 12 percent. In 2021 the cost of capital for utility-scale solar in key emerging economies was two to three times that in advanced economies, and financing made up about half of the levelized cost of solar in emerging and developing economies (International Energy Agency 2022).

A large part of the premium is the risk that the buyer of the power will not pay. In most developing countries that buyer, the offtaker, is a state-owned distribution company that sells below cost and collects poorly. Of 39 Sub-Saharan African countries studied by the World Bank, only Seychelles and Uganda had financially viable electricity sectors (Trimble et al. 2016). When such a utility runs short of cash, its generators wait. Lenders and investors price that wait into every project that sells to it, however good the project.

This essay asks how a developing country can remove the payment risk that sets the cost of capital for clean power, at the lowest contingent cost to the state.

The answer is to put a creditworthy intermediary between generators and distribution companies. A *payment intermediary facility* buys power from generators under one standard power purchase agreement and pays generators in full and on time whatever the distribution companies do. It recovers arrears itself, backed by a *guarantee waterfall* contracted in advance: the offtaker, its subnational owner, the national government, a multilateral agency and a donor contingency account. Guaranteeing the contract beats guaranteeing project debt, because it keeps the claim on the distribution company alive and costs the guarantor only the arrears it fails to recover. India's Solar Energy Corporation shows that the design can lower tariffs; its distribution companies' arrears show that it fails without utility reform.

Where the de-risking literature classifies public instruments and estimates their effect on the cost of capital (Waissbein et al. 2013; Schmidt 2014), this essay models one institution, compares two forms of guarantee, and shows how one form turns an implicit fiscal risk into a smaller, explicit one (Irwin 2007; Polackova 1998).

## 2. A model of offtaker risk

The offtaker premium depends on how long arrears last and how much is recovered. Suppose the offtaker falls into *default episodes* at intensity $\lambda$ per year. In an episode it withholds all payments for $D$ years, then pays a fraction $R$ of what it withheld. Investors discount at a rate $r_0$ that prices every other risk, and price episodes at an intensity $m\lambda$, where $m \ge 1$ is the market price of default risk (Driessen 2005). The generator then expects to collect a fraction $1-h$ of each contracted dollar, where the *offtaker haircut* $h$ is the share of time in arrears times the share of each withheld dollar lost in present value. Its break-even tariff is the levelized cost at $r_0$ divided by $1-h$: a generator who expects to collect 90 cents of each dollar must charge 11 percent more. An intermediary changes each term. Its liquidity shortens the episode the generator experiences to zero, its powers to intercept transfers raise $R$, and as a repeat creditor it lowers $\lambda$ itself, because the offtaker cannot play one generator off against another.

A contract guarantee removes the whole premium; a debt guarantee removes only the part that falls on lenders. A *contract guarantee* pays the generator during every episode and recovers from the offtaker later. A *debt guarantee* pays lenders when the project fails to service its debt, from any cause. If the equity cushion absorbs every episode, lenders were never at risk and the tariff does not fall, while construction and operating failures add to the guarantor's cost. The contract guarantor's exposure in any episode is bounded by the arrears; the debt guarantor's only by the outstanding debt.

The contract guarantee is the more efficient whenever its guarantor recovers at least as much, in present value, as a debt guarantor would. That holds when the contract guarantor keeps an enforceable claim on the offtaker, while the debt guarantor's claim runs against a project company whose only asset is an unpaid contract. Part of the gain is real: a repeat creditor with collection powers recovers more than a dispersed set of generators. The rest is the market price of risk that investors no longer charge, which is a gain to society only if the state bears the risk better than investors do. It does not when the offtaker's default is correlated with the state's own fiscal distress, as it often is.

## 3. A stylized calibration

In a stylized calibration the offtaker adds about 11 percent to the solar tariff. The central case assumes a real cost of capital of 8 percent for every risk except the offtaker's, one default episode every six to seven years, episodes lasting a year, recovery of 60 percent and a price of risk of 2: a weak but not failed utility that is regularly late and eventually pays most of what it owes. The break-even tariff rises from 4.96 to 5.50 cents per kilowatt-hour, equivalent to a cost of capital of 9.4 percent instead of 8. Recovery matters as much as frequency: a utility with 25 episodes per 100 years that recovers 30 percent adds 4 percentage points to the cost of capital; one that is late as often but recovers 90 percent adds 0.6 points. These figures illustrate a mechanism; they are not estimates.

For a 100-megawatt plant, a contract guarantee buys more tariff reduction for less risk. Suppose the facility recovers 95 percent of what it pays a year after each episode, a debt guarantor recovers 50 percent three years later, debt is \$60 million of the \$80 million cost, and lenders bear 30 percent of the offtaker's losses. The contract guarantee cuts the tariff by 0.54 cents, the whole premium, saving the offtaker \$0.94 million a year at an expected cost of \$0.18 million. The debt guarantee cuts the tariff by 0.17 cents at an expected cost of \$0.40 million, nearly half of it for risks unrelated to the offtaker. Per dollar of expected cost, the contract guarantee saves \$5.3 and the debt guarantee \$0.8.

Each layer of the waterfall pays only when the layers above it have failed, so a reliable national layer makes the layers below it cheap. Take a regional facility buying 2 gigawatts of solar output from ten offtakers for \$174 million a year. If the national government pays four times in five when called, a funded donor account at the bottom expects to pay only \$0.1 million a year. Liquidity, not loss, is what can break the facility, because a common shock such as a devaluation puts many offtakers into arrears at once (Vasicek 2002). If defaults share a common factor with correlation 0.3, 65 percent of offtakers are in arrears in a 1-in-200 year, against 14 percent on average, and a facility that must wait six months for the national layer needs a buffer of \$57 million, a third of a year's payments.

## 4. Evidence from India and Nigeria

The Solar Energy Corporation of India is the clearest working example of a payment intermediary facility. A central government company, it buys from developers only after signing back-to-back sale agreements with state distribution companies, and by July 2025 it had sale agreements for about 60 gigawatts at a weighted average tariff of Rs 2.93 per kilowatt-hour, well below the distribution companies' average power purchase cost (ICRA 2025). Its payment security is layered: letters of credit, mandatory since 2019 (Power Line 2019); a payment security fund; and, since 2016, the protection of a tripartite agreement under which the central government can have the Reserve Bank of India debit a defaulting state's account (ICRA 2025; Kenning 2017). ICRA rates the corporation AAA. Because a defaulting state loses central transfers, the corporation is paid ahead of generators that sell to the state directly.

The failure is upstream. Most state distribution companies remain financially weak, and their letter-of-credit coverage is only 68 percent (ICRA 2025). The central government has had to act twice: a Rs 90,000 crore liquidity scheme in May 2020 so that distribution companies could pay generators (CRISIL Ratings 2020), and rules in June 2022 letting them pay accumulated arrears in up to 48 monthly installments (Power Line 2022). India therefore confirms both halves of the argument: the intermediary moved risk away from generators and lowered tariffs, but the risk moved to the center rather than disappearing. A waterfall without utility reform turns into a standing line of credit.

Nigeria shows a single buyer with nothing behind it. Its bulk electricity trader buys from generators and sells to distribution companies (World Bank Group 2024), but it had neither revenue from its buyers nor a contracted layer behind it. In 2017, when the trader expected to collect about a quarter of its invoices, the government arranged a central bank loan of up to N701 billion so that it could pay generators and gas suppliers (Okafor 2017). An intermediary that collects a quarter of its bills is a conduit for subsidy, not a credit enhancement. For the Azura-Edo gas plant, the government gave no sovereign guarantee but signed a put-and-call option: if the power purchase agreement ends early, the project company can sell the plant to the government for at least the outstanding debt. A World Bank Group case study presents this as having no negative impact on the government's balance sheet (World Bank Group 2024). It is a debt guarantee with a delay: the exposure of a debt guarantee without the recovery of a contract guarantee, since once the government owns the plant there is no one left to recover from.

## 5. Implications for the state

The facility does not make the state's exposure disappear; it makes it smaller, contingent and contracted. It is smaller because the facility recovers more and utility reform lowers the frequency of default, contingent because the state pays only when its own utility fails to, and contracted because the waterfall fixes in advance who pays, in what order and up to what cap. The state already bears its utilities' arrears implicitly: India's liquidity scheme of 2020 and Nigeria's central bank loan of 2017 were implicit contingent liabilities falling due (Polackova 1998). The facility converts part of that liability into an explicit one with a cap and a price.

The layers should be recorded at expected cost and charged for. The IMF's statistical standard records expected calls under standardized guarantee schemes as a liability (International Monetary Fund 2014). A facility with one standard contract is such a scheme, so the national government should provision for its expected calls net of recovery. A fee passed to the utilities that cause the calls makes the cost visible (Irwin 2007).

The design fails in four cases: if reform does not come, so that the waterfall becomes a channel for permanent subsidy and the honest instrument is an explicit budget transfer; if the national layer will not pay, as in a regional facility without an intercept; if a common shock finds the facility underfunded; and if favored developers capture it.

The strongest objection is that the facility only moves risk to the center, as India shows. That is true, and it is the point: the center already pays for the risk in crises, and the alternative is to pay the offtaker premium on every project, every year, for the life of every contract. The next step is empirical: estimating the frequency, length and recovery of default episodes for named utilities from payment data such as India's PRAAPTI portal, and comparing tariffs in intermediated and direct auctions to measure the premium directly.
