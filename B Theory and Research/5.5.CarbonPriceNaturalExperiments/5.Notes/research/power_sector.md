## Q1. UK: Carbon Price Support (CPS) rates, EU ETS price, total carbon price, and the implied increase in fuel costs

### Takeaway
GB generators paid the CPS on top of the EU ETS price. The CPS was about £5/t in 2013-14, £9.55/t in 2014-15 and £18.08/t in 2015-16, and has been frozen at £18/t since 2016-17. In euros, the total carbon price rose from about €7/t in 2012 to €32/t in 2015, roughly four times the EU ETS price alone. At 2015 fuel prices the carbon cost (EUA + CPS) roughly doubled to 2.4× the pre-tax cost of coal per MWh of heat (+141%), but raised the cost of gas by only about 30%. That gap is what drove the switch from coal to gas. The government announced in April 2026 that the CPS will be removed from April 2028.

### Cited Findings

**CPS rates (£/tCO2; fiscal years run April to March)**
- The 2013-14 rate was **£4.94/tCO2**. It was set by subtracting the forecast ETS price for 2013-14 (£14.21/tCO2) from the target floor: "Taking this rate and subtracting the forecast Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) rate for 2013-14 (£14.21/tCO2), the support rate for 2013-14 will be £4.94/tCO2." — [HMRC, Carbon price floor tax information note](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/179259/carbon_price_floor.pdf.pdf) [VERIFY: quote seen in a search snippet; I did not open the host document to confirm it is this file]
  - **Conflicting figure:** Leroutier's Table 1 gives **4.96** for April 2013–March 2014, citing Ares and Delebarre (2016) — [Leroutier WP 2021, p.7, Table 1](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). The official HMRC figure is £4.94. Use £4.94.
- 2014-15: **£9.55/t** ("The so-called carbon price support was set at 9.55 pounds ($15.13) a metric ton for 2014, according to a budget document") — [Bloomberg, 21 Mar 2012](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2012-03-21/u-k-almost-doubles-carbon-surcharge-on-utilities-from-2014-1-). This also matches [Leroutier Table 1](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf).
- 2015-16: **£18.08/t**; 2016-17, 2017-18 and 2018-19: **£18/t** — [Leroutier WP 2021, Table 1, p.7](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). Leroutier: "in 2014 the Government decided to freeze the CPS rate to £18/tCO2e (€22 in 2016) until 2019/2020, after business representatives expressed concerns over the competitiveness of energy-intensive industries" (p.6).
- The freeze has been extended repeatedly. The 2014 Budget capped the rate at £18 from 2016/17. Later Budgets extended the freeze to 2020-21, then to 2023-24 — [ICAEW, Autumn Budget 2021](https://www.icaew.com/technical/tax/budgets-and-legislation/tax-faculty-analysis-of-uk-budgets/autumn-budget-2021/environment-taxes); [International Tax Review](https://www.internationaltaxreview.com/article/b1f9jxmm44pd99/uk-to-freeze-carbon-price-support-rate). The Autumn Budget 2024 maintained "carbon price support (CPS) rates in Great Britain at a level equivalent to £18 per tonne of CO2 in 2026-27" — [Deloitte Taxscape, Autumn Budget 2024](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/measures-autumn-budget-2024/climate-change-levy-and-carbon-price-support.aspx).
- **Fuel-specific HMRC rates for 1 April 2016 to 31 March 2028:** gas £0.00331/kWh; LPG £0.05280/kg; "coal and other solid fossil fuels" £1.54790/GJ (gross calorific value) — [GOV.UK, Climate Change Levy rates](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/climate-change-levy-rates).
  - My own calculation: these rates imply 0.184 tCO2/MWh thermal for gas (£3.31/MWh ÷ £18) and 0.31 tCO2/MWh thermal for coal on a gross calorific value basis (£1.5479/GJ × 3.6 = £5.57/MWh ÷ £18). The coal rate per unit of energy is 1.68× the gas rate. This matches Leroutier: "The CPS rate on coal is about 70% higher than the tax on natural gas" (p.7).
- **Removal:** On 16 April 2026 HM Treasury announced that the CPS will be removed from April 2028, leaving carbon priced only by the UK ETS. The rate is currently £18/tCO2 — [Carbon Credits news](https://www.ccarbon.info/news/uk-government-confirms-carbon-price-support-will-be-removed-from-april-2028/); [IFS response](https://ifs.org.uk/articles/response-scrapping-carbon-price-support) [VERIFY: the IFS page returned 403; its content is known only from a search snippet. That snippet says CPS revenue "was already forecast to dwindle to £200 million by 2028–29"]. Removal was widely expected in the November 2025 Autumn Budget but did not happen there — [techUK](https://www.techuk.org/insights/news/autumn-budget-2025-energy-transport-sustainability) [VERIFY: snippet].
- Coverage: the CPS applies to almost all power generators in Great Britain. Northern Ireland is excluded because it is in the all-island Single Electricity Market. The only exemptions are stand-by generators and units under 2 MWth. CHP plants pay only on fuel used to generate electricity for the grid — [Leroutier WP 2021, pp.6–7 and footnotes 4 and 6](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). CPS revenue goes to the general budget (ibid.).
- Original intent: "a total carbon price target of £30 (around €35) by 2020" — [Leroutier, p.6](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). Abrell et al.: "In 2013, the CPF was announced to increase up to 34.5 (69) €/tCO2 in 2020 (2030). At the end of 2015, however, the UK government fixed the CPS rate to 21.6 €/tCO2 until 2021" — [Abrell et al. ZEW DP 21-039, fn 8, p.10](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp21039.pdf).

### Inferences
- The CPS raised coal's carbon cost per MWh of electricity about 2.4 times as much as gas's. In 2015 a coal-to-gas fuel-price ratio already moving toward gas (0.69 in 2015, 0.88 in 2016, inclusive of carbon, from Abrell Table 1) amplified this. That interaction is why Abrell et al. find the effect "mainly driven by the variation in the relative carbon tax-exclusive prices for coal and natural gas and only to a limited extent by the carbon tax rate itself" (p.4).
- For the paper's summary table, a clean "carbon price faced" series is: total €10.6/t (2013-14), €18.4/t (2014-15), €32.0/t (2015-16), €26.9/t (2016-17), from Abrell Table 1. For 2017–2019 the total is £18 CPS plus the EUA price. The EUA price rose sharply in 2018, roughly €15–25/t [VERIFY: EUA annual averages 2017–2019 not collected].

### Gaps
- **US$ per tonne:** I collected no exchange rates. As a rough guide, £18/t is about US$23–24/t at the 2017–2019 rate of roughly 1.28–1.34 US$/£ [VERIFY: exchange rates from memory; use Bank of England or FRED annual averages]. Bloomberg converts £9.55 to US$15.13 at the March 2012 rate.
- EUA annual averages for 2017–2019 and the total GB carbon price for those years were not collected from a primary source. Ember/Sandbag and ICE are the likely sources.
- I did not open HMRC's fuel-specific CPS rates for 2013-14, 2014-15 and 2015-16 (per kWh of gas and per GJ of coal).

## Q2. UK: effects on wholesale and retail electricity prices, and coal's share of generation

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed ex post estimate of how much the CPS raised GB wholesale or retail electricity prices. Abrell et al. report the tax paid instead: €5.2bn from April 2013 to December 2016. In April 2026 one market participant estimated that removing the CPS in 2028 would lower GB power prices by about £4–5/MWh. Coal's share of UK generation fell from 39% in 2012 to 22% in 2015, 9% in 2016, about 7% in 2017 and 2.1% in 2019. The official statistics explicitly attribute the 2016 collapse to the carbon price.

### Cited Findings
- **Coal's share of UK electricity generation:**
  - 2012: 39% (143 TWh) — [DECC Energy Trends special article, "Coal in 2012"](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7a5d5940f0b66a2fc01d17/et_article_coal_in_2012.pdf). On a different basis (share of electricity supplied) the figure was 42.8% (search snippet).
  - 2015: 22%. 2016: 9%. "In 2016, coal-fired generation fell below half of its 2015 level, its share falling from 22 per cent to 9 per cent as the carbon price made coal generation more expensive than gas. Consequently, gas increased its share to 42 per cent of generation." — [BEIS, DUKES 2017 press notice](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a8218eb40f0b6230269ad23/DUKES_2017_Press_Notice.pdf).
  - 2012 to 2017: "the share of coal in electricity generation decreased from 40% to 7% and power sector greenhouse gas emissions decreased by 57%" — [Leroutier WP 2021, p.1](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf).
  - 2019: 2.1%. Coal generation fell 59% to "a record low share of just 2.1 per cent" — [BEIS Energy Trends press notice, March 2020](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/877047/Press_Notice_March_2020.pdf).
  - 2018: [VERIFY: not collected; about 5% from memory. Check DUKES 2019, Table 5.6.]
- **Fuel switching attributed to the CPS:** the CPS caused coal-plant output to fall by **46.3 TWh** and gas-plant output to rise by **45.6 TWh**, April 2013 to December 2016. This is "a fuel switch from coal to natural gas in the order of 15 percent" — [Abrell et al., Table 4, p.27](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp21039.pdf). By year (TWh change in coal output, with % of counterfactual): 2013 −4.17 (−3.7%); 2014 −9.26 (−9.8%); 2015 −21.92 (−27.0%); 2016 −10.94 (−43.6%) (ibid.).
- **Tax paid, not price pass-through:** "Since its introduction and until the end of 2016, the British government received around €5.2 billion in tax revenue from the CPS policy … the highest tax revenues (around €2 billion) accrued in 2015" — [Abrell et al., fn 19](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp21039.pdf). Tax payments by year in €m (Table 5): 725.7; 1,309.6; 2,129.4; 1,372.8. Total 5,194.3.
- **Price effect of removal (industry estimate, not ex post):** Greencoat UK Wind estimated that "electricity prices used in the Company's NAV could fall by approximately £4 - 5/MWh from April 2028 to the early 2030s" after the CPS is removed — [Greencoat UK Wind RNS, 17 Apr 2026](https://www.ticker.app/lse/UKW/rns/2026-04-17/Removal-of-Carbon-Price-Support/8429A) [VERIFY: quote from a search snippet]. This is low because gas now sets the price and gas pays about £6.7/MWh_e of CPS (my Q1 calculation).
- **Compensation:** electro-intensive industries received compensation for the indirect cost of the CPS — "around £100 million for the period April …" — [Leroutier WP, footnote, p.~49](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf) [VERIFY: the sentence is truncated in my extract]. The CPS was frozen "after business representatives expressed concerns over the competitiveness of energy-intensive industries because of generators passing on the tax costs" (Leroutier, p.6). That is qualitative evidence of pass-through.
- **Interconnector and leakage effects:** Leroutier estimates that CPS-induced emissions abroad over 2013–2017 were 6.4 MtCO2e in the Netherlands and 0.8 MtCO2e in Ireland. If coal is assumed to be the marginal fuel, the figures are 13.2 and 1.7 MtCO2e — [Leroutier, pp.~33–34](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). Gugler et al. (2023) say the tax "likely created emissions abroad via imports and the waterbed effect within the EU Emission Trading System" — [Gugler et al. 2023, abstract](https://research.wu.ac.at/en/publications/carbon-pricing-and-emissions-causal-effects-of-britains-carbon-ta/).

### Inferences
- With the CPS at £18/t and a gas plant at the margin (about 0.37 t/MWh_e), the CPS adds roughly £6–7/MWh to the wholesale price. With coal at the margin (2013–2015) it adds about £16/MWh. These are engineering upper bounds assuming full pass-through, not estimates. On a typical GB wholesale price of about £40–45/MWh in 2015–2016 [VERIFY: Ofgem wholesale indicators not collected], that is roughly a 15–40% wholesale increase. The paper should present this as an illustration, not a measured effect.

### Gaps
- **No ex post econometric estimate of CPS pass-through to GB wholesale or retail prices was found.** Leads: Chyong, Guo & Newbery on the GB–France interconnector and CPS (Energy Journal), Ofgem wholesale market indicators, and the House of Commons Library briefing SN05927. That briefing returned 403, so I did not read it.
- Retail price effect: I found no source. Ofgem and DESNZ retail price indices for 2013–2016 were not collected.

## Q3. UK: ex post estimates of the emissions reduction caused by the CPS

### Takeaway
Four peer-reviewed ex post studies agree on the direction but differ by an order of magnitude on size, because they measure different things.

- **Abrell et al. (machine learning; short-run coal-to-gas switching at existing plants):** −6.2% (abstract) or −6.4% (Table 5); 26.1 Mt over 2013–2016; about €18/t.
- **Gugler et al. (2023, regression discontinuity in time):** −26%, 38.6 Mt over 2013–2015.
- **Leroutier (synthetic control; includes plant closures):** −20.5% to −26% per year, 143–191 MtCO2e over 2013–2017.
- **Gugler et al. (2021):** carbon pricing about €30/t delivers large, cheap abatement in GB compared with German renewable subsidies.

### Cited Findings

**Leroutier (2022).** Marion Leroutier, "Carbon pricing and power sector decarbonization: Evidence from the UK," *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 111, 102580 (2022). Citation from the [MISUM Academic Insight](https://www.hhs.se/contentassets/a6206121d9eb477384fb4a0fceb1e34e/misum-academic-insight---carbon-pricing-and-power-sector-decarbonization-in-the-uk.pdf). DOI: 10.1016/j.jeem.2021.102580 [VERIFY: DOI inferred from the Elsevier pattern, not seen]. I read the full text of the [MISUM WP 2021-03, 24 Oct 2021](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). Peer-reviewed.
- Abstract (WP): "Relative to a synthetic control unit built from other European countries, I find that emissions from the UK power sector declined by 20 to 26 percent per year on average between 2013 and 2017."
- Abatement "of between 143 and 191 million tons of equivalent carbon dioxide … over the 2013-2017 period, representing a decrease of 20.5% to 26% per year on average" (p.2).
- **Upper bound** (all of the gap attributed to the CPS): "On an average year, emissions decrease by 26 percent, with an associated semi-elasticity of -1.65% of emissions per Euro of the tax on average … total cumulative abatement of 191 million tCO2e … Emissions abatement was the most pronounced in 2017, when UK emissions became 50% lower than synthetic UK emissions" (p.27).
- **Lower bound:** removing biomass conversions (Drax, Lynemouth) gives "emissions decrease by 22.5 percent, with an associated semi-elasticity of -1.41% per Euro of the tax. The total cumulative abatement amounts to 164.3 million of tCO2e." Subtracting an upper bound for the CfD and capacity market (about 3.6 MtCO2e) leaves about 161 MtCO2e (p.~30). Subtracting import spillovers and the ETS waterbed effect as well gives 143 MtCO2e, or 20.5% (pp.44–45).
- **Inference:** "Having only one treated unit is insufficient for building confidence intervals." In the permutation test the UK has the highest post/pre-MSPE ratio of 14 countries, so "the associated probability is 1/14 = 7.1%, the lowest possible probability with this sample size" (p.~32). In-time placebo tests are also run. **No conventional confidence interval is reported.**
- **Donor weights:** Ireland 0.492; Slovakia 0.256; Netherlands 0.137; Finland 0.058; Czech Republic 0.057; all others 0 (Table 3, p.~26).
- **Mechanisms, 2013–17, from lower-bound decomposition:** different behaviour of plants that opted out of the Large Combustion Plant Directive (LCPD), about 64 MtCO2e; intensive margin excluding opt-out plants, about 45 MtCO2e; extensive margin (closures and fewer new entrants) excluding opt-out plants, about 50 MtCO2e (pp.43–44). That is "roughly a third" each (p.2).
- **Sub-periods for comparison:** 2013–2016, 100 to 120 MtCO2e; 2013–2015, 36 to 51 MtCO2e. Intensive margin only, 2013–2016: 14.5 MtCO2e (pp.45–46).
- Cumulative abatement equals 60% (lower bound) to 81% (upper bound) of the abatement needed for the UK's second carbon budget (p.46).
- Leroutier cites three conditions for external validity: high potential to switch from coal to gas, the UK's relative isolation from other electricity markets, and … (p.46, truncated in my extract).
- **Version warning:** an earlier 2019 FAERE working paper version framed the policy as the "Carbon Price Floor" and reported a reduction of "between 41% and 49%" of total power-sector emissions by 2017 — [FAERE WP 2019.12, via search summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/faewpaper/2019.12.htm) [VERIFY: snippet]. **Cite only the 2022 JEEM numbers.**

**Gugler, Haxhimusa & Liebensteiner (2021).** "Effectiveness of climate policies: Carbon pricing vs. subsidizing renewables," *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 106, 102405. DOI 10.1016/j.jeem.2020.102405 — [FAU CRIS record](https://cris.fau.de/publications/246591019/). I saw the abstract only. Peer-reviewed.
- Abstract, as summarised: Britain's power-sector emissions "dropped by 55% within five years" after the unilateral carbon tax. Germany relied on wind and solar subsidies and saw little decline. "Even a modest carbon price (~30 EUR/tCO2) can induce significant abatement" when gas can replace coal. ## Q5. Australia: ex post emissions reduction in the NEM, and the rebound after repeal

### Takeaway
The only dedicated ex post attribution (O'Gorman & Jotzo 2014, not peer-reviewed) estimates that the carbon price cut NEM emissions by **5–8 Mt (3.2–5.0%) in 2012-13** and **6–9 Mt (3.5–5.6%) in 2013-14**, **11–17 Mt cumulatively**. That comes from lower demand (1.3–2.3%) and lower emissions intensity (1.8–3.3%). Observed NEM emissions fell 8.2% over the two years against the previous two. After repeal, emissions rose 3.1% (4.6 Mt) in the year to April 2015, with brown coal's share rebounding. The rebound figures come from a consultancy index, not a peer-reviewed paper.

### Cited Findings
- **O'Gorman, Marianna and Frank Jotzo (2014), "Impact of the carbon price on Australia's electricity demand, supply and emissions," CCEP Working Paper 1411, Centre for Climate Economics & Policy, Crawford School, ANU (July 2014).** DOI 10.22004/ag.econ.249493 — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/ancewp/249493.html); [PDF on AgEcon Search](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/249493/files/ccep1411.pdf) (blocked to me). **Not peer-reviewed.** I saw the full abstract only. Abstract numbers, verbatim:
  - Observed: "Over this period, electricity demand in the NEM declined by 3.8 per cent, the emissions intensity of electricity supply by 4.6 per cent, and overall emissions by 8.2 per cent, compared to the two-year period before the carbon price."
  - Demand: "We estimate the demand reduction attributable to the carbon price at 2.5 to 4.2 TWh per year, about 1.3 to 2.3 per cent of total electricity demand in the NEM."
  - Supply: "Emissions-intensive brown coal and black coal generators reduced output and 4GW of emissions-intensive generation capacity was taken offline. We estimate that these shifts in the supply mix resulted in a 16 to 28kg CO2/MWh reduction in the emissions intensity of power supply in the NEM, a reduction between 1.8 and 3.3 per cent."
  - Total: "The combined impact attributable to the carbon price is estimated as a reduction of between 5 and 8 million tonnes of CO2 emissions (3.2 to 5 per cent) in 2012/13 and between 6 and 9 million tonnes (3.5 to 5.6 per cent) in 2013/14, and between 11 and 17 million tonnes cumulatively."
  - Caveat: "There are fundamental difficulties in attributing observed changes in demand and supply to specific causes, especially over the short term, and in this light we use conservative parameters." Also: "its effect on investment in power generation assets has probably been limited, because of policy uncertainty about the continuation of the carbon pricing mechanism."
- Press coverage: "as much as 17 million tonnes" — [RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/despite-imminent-demise-carbon-price-cut-emissions-35520/amp/); [Eco-Business](https://www.eco-business.com/id/news/carbon-price-helped-curb-emissions-anu-study-finds/).
- **Peer-reviewed follow-ups:**
  - Nazifi et al. (2021) report that NEM "emission intensities fell only slightly" during the carbon price period — [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v96y2021ics0140988321000839.html). That is qualitatively consistent with O'Gorman & Jotzo's 1.8–3.3%.
  - **I found no peer-reviewed paper with a causal Mt estimate of the NEM emissions reduction.** Brief leads "Nelson et al." and "Apergis": nothing found.
- **Rebound after repeal (Pitt & Sherry CEDEX index; consultancy, not peer-reviewed):**
  - NEM electricity emissions in the year to April 2015 were 3.1% (4.6 Mt) higher than in the 12 months to June 2014.
  - June 2014 was the low point, "at the time when the carbon tax had its greatest effect prior to its repeal".
  - Brown coal rose to 24% of generation, its highest share since the year to September 2012 — [The Echo, May 2015](https://www.echo.net.au/2015/05/coal-on-rise-in-electricity-supply-report/); [RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/australia-sees-biggest-jump-in-electricity-emissions-since-2001/).

### Inferences
- At the midpoint, about 7 Mt a year on roughly 165–180 Mt of NEM emissions [VERIFY: base not collected; implied by 5 Mt = 3.2%, about 156 Mt], the Australian effect (about 4–5% a year) is similar in size to Abrell's short-run UK estimate (about 6%) at a similar price (about €17–19/t vs €10–32/t). ## Q6. RGGI (alternate case): allowance prices and attributable emissions reduction

### Takeaway
RGGI allowances cost about US$3 per short ton in 2008–09 and fell to the US$1.86 reserve price from September 2010 to December 2012. That is about US$2.0–3.9 per metric tonne. That is about half the post-2009 decline, with the rest due to the recession, cheap gas and complementary policies.

## Q7. Caveats: concurrent policies and confounders

### Takeaway
UK estimates must net out the LCPD, biomass conversions (Drax, Lynemouth), renewables support (Renewables Obligation and CfD), the capacity market and falling gas prices. Leroutier's bounds remove up to about 25% of the gross effect (191 Mt down to 143 Mt). Australian estimates are confounded by falling demand, plant closures, Hydro Tasmania's surge, the Renewable Energy Target and policy uncertainty over repeal. RGGI coincided with the recession and the shale-gas price collapse.

### Cited Findings
- **UK, other policies:**
  - The CPF was part of Electricity Market Reform, alongside a capacity market, CfDs for low-carbon generation and an emissions performance standard banning new unabated coal — [Leroutier, p.6](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf).
  - Bounding these (biomass conversion about 27 Mt; CfD and capacity market about 3.6 Mt; spillovers and waterbed about 18 Mt) reduces 191 Mt to 143 Mt — [Leroutier, pp.30–45](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). The renewables and capacity market effects were "several orders of magnitude smaller than the CPS during the 2013-2017 period … due to the time lag" (p.2).
- **UK, LCPD:** about a third of Leroutier's lower bound (about 64 Mt) comes from UK LCPD opt-out plants behaving differently, with closures accelerated by the carbon price — [Leroutier, pp.43–44](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). Abrell et al. note that the CPS "coincides with the closure of several coal-fired" plants. Didcot A, Cockenzie and others closed in March 2013 — [Abrell et al., Table 2 and p.~23](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp21039.pdf).
- **UK, gas prices:** "Between 2013-2016, natural gas prices declined by nearly 50 percent while coal prices remained largely constant … This suggests that even without the introduction of the CPS" some switching would have occurred — [Abrell et al., p.~12](https://ftp.zew.de/pub/zew-docs/dp/dp21039.pdf). Abrell's method conditions on fuel prices.
- **UK, external validity conditions:** a high potential for coal-to-gas switching, limited interconnection, and … — [Leroutier, p.46](https://swopec.hhs.se/hamisu/papers/hamisu2021_003.1.pdf). Spare gas capacity and supply chains were preconditions — [Wilson & Staffell 2018](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/127309).
- **UK, policy mix:** Stechemesser et al. (2024) classify the UK electricity success as a policy mix (carbon price floor, renewable subsidies, coal phase-out) and find "successes only arise in tandem with tax or price incentives" — [AFP via yen.com.gh](https://yen.com.gh/business-economy/264976-combining-climate-measures-key-slashing-emissions-study/) [VERIFY].
- **Australia:**
  - NEM demand was already falling; it declined 3.8% over the two years — [O'Gorman & Jotzo abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/ancewp/249493.html).
  - 2012-13 price rises reflected plant closures as well as carbon — [NSW Parliamentary Research Service](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/researchpapers/Pages/electricity-prices-demand-and-supply-in-nsw.aspx).
  - Policy uncertainty limited investment effects — [O'Gorman & Jotzo](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/ancewp/249493.html).
  - Hydro Tasmania sharply increased output during the carbon-price years, drawing down storage, and then cut back; drought and low inflows later compounded this [VERIFY: from memory; no source collected].
  - The Renewable Energy Target and rooftop solar also reduced demand for coal generation [VERIFY].
- **RGGI:** the attribution separates the recession, complementary programmes and lower gas prices — [Duke Scholars abstract](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1061149).

### Inferences
- For the paper's caveats column: the UK is the cleanest case, with a large unilateral price wedge, a synthetic control and explicit bounds. Australia is short (two years), price expectations were contaminated by the expected repeal, and the evidence is a working paper. RGGI's price was too low to identify a pure price effect.

### Gaps
- Quantified hydro and drought contributions in the NEM for 2012–2015 are missing.
- I did not collect UK Renewables Obligation volumes for 2013–2017.

### Inferences
- Across the three cases, effect size rises with how big the price wedge is relative to the coal–gas cost gap, and with whether the price was expected to last. The UK is large and credible, Australia moderate and expected to be repealed, RGGI low-priced.
