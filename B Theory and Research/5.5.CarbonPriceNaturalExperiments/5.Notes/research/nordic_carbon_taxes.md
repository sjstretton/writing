## 1. Policy: what was taxed, from when, which fuels and sectors, exemptions, and what else changed at the same time

### Takeaway
Sweden (1991) and Norway (1991) taxed transport and heating fuels at high rates but exempted or discounted much of industry; Sweden's transport case is clean on coverage but is confounded by a 25% VAT on motor fuel added in March 1990 and by energy-tax cuts in 1991 (heating fuels) and 2001–2005 (petrol). Sweden's residential-heating case is the cleanest "dose" experiment: the 2001–2004 tripling of the rate was not offset by energy-tax cuts for households. Finland (1990) started with a very small tax; Norway's tax covered only about 64% of CO2 because process industry, fishing and shipping were exempt.

### Cited Findings

**Sweden, general design and the 1990–91 reform**
- Energy taxes predate the carbon tax: petrol taxed since 1924, diesel since 1937, heating oil, coal and electricity since the 1950s; a CO2 tax and a sulphur tax were added in 1991 — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, p. 3–4](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)
- At introduction in 1991 the CO2 tax was "levied on all major fossil fuels at rates equivalent to SEK 0.25 per kg CO2"; "At the same time the energy tax rates were reduced by 50 %", so the total tax on heating gas oil "was almost unaffected" while coal's tax rose sharply — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, p. 7](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)
- The carbon tax was part of the 1990–91 tax reform: labour taxes cut (about –US$6 billion), VAT on energy (+US$1.8 billion), and the carbon tax "introduced at a low levels combined with approx. 50% cuts in energy tax rates" (+US$0.4 billion) — [Ministry of Finance 2023 slides, slide 5](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/230323-carbon-tax-sweden---general-info.pdf)
- Biofuels (wood, logging residues) bear zero CO2 tax; commercial aviation and shipping fuels are untaxed; fuels for electricity generation are untaxed (electricity is taxed at consumption) — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, Table 2 note g, footnote 4, Figure 3](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)
- Industry rate: from 1 Jan 1993 industry paid 25% of the general rate (horticulture 15% before that; energy-intensive firms had case-by-case relief in 1991–92); industry's share was 50% in 1997–2000, then cut to 35% (2001), 30% (2002), 25% (2003), 21% (2004–2007) to offset general-rate rises; from 2008 a lower rate applied inside the EU ETS; from 2011 industry inside the EU ETS paid no CO2 tax and industry outside paid 30% — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, Table 1 and notes](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)
- Industry and CHP outside the EU ETS were raised step by step to 100% of the carbon tax over 2011–2018; CHP inside the EU ETS paid 11% (2018), 91% (2019), then no carbon tax from 2023 — [Ministry of Finance 2023 slides, slide 17](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/230323-carbon-tax-sweden---general-info.pdf)
- About 95% of Swedish fossil CO2 is covered by the carbon tax or the EU ETS — [Ministry of Finance 2023 slides, slide 9](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/230323-carbon-tax-sweden---general-info.pdf); the carbon tax itself covers about 40% of Sweden's greenhouse gases — [Tax Foundation 2020](https://taxfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Looking-Back-on-30-Years-of-Carbon-Taxes-in-Sweden-re-1.pdf); the Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al. meta-analysis also codes Sweden's emission coverage as 40% (sectors: transport, buildings) — [Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al. 2024, Table 1](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC11099057)
- Since the 1994 Act the per-unit CO2 and energy tax amounts are indexed annually to the CPI — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, Table 2 note h](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)

**Sweden, transport (core case)**
- "In the year prior, in March of 1990, Sweden also added VAT of 25% to the price of gasoline and diesel"; the VAT applies to all price components including excise taxes — [Andersson 2017 working paper, p. 3](https://www.cccep.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Working-paper-212-Andersson_update_March2017.pdf); VAT on petrol introduced March 1990, previously exempt — [Ekonomifakta 2006](https://www.ekonomifakta.se/sakomraden/skatt/skatt/staten-vinnare-pa-hojda-bensinpriser_1213148.html)
- Transport is "fully covered" except domestic aviation (exempt from energy tax, carbon tax and VAT; 3.5% of transport CO2); transport was close to 40% of annual CO2 in 1990–2005 — [Andersson 2017 WP, p. 3 and fn. 3](https://www.cccep.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Working-paper-212-Andersson_update_March2017.pdf)
- The real energy tax on gasoline was "fairly constant from 1960 to 2000, before decreasing in the years 2001-2005", offsetting the carbon-tax rise; "The total tax rate increased by 39% in 1990 and more than 82% between 1989 and 2005 – from 4 SEK to over 7 SEK per litre" — [Andersson 2017 WP, p. 8](https://www.cccep.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Working-paper-212-Andersson_update_March2017.pdf)
- "the total tax on unleaded petrol was between 2000 and 2004 raised from 4.47 to 4.79 SEK per litre", with the CO2 share of the total tax rising "from 19 to 44 %"; this offsetting energy-tax cut applied to motor fuels, not to household heating fuels — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, Table 1 note A](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)
- From 1 July 2018 a biofuel "reduction obligation" (reduktionsplikt) applies to fuel suppliers; it is not a tax and its cost sits in the pre-tax price — [Ekonomifakta 2026](https://www.ekonomifakta.se/fakta/energi/styrmedel/konsumtionsskatter-pa-bensin); the government takes "biofuel share into account when setting carbon tax rates for petrol and diesel" — [Ministry of Finance 2023 slides, slide 15](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/230323-carbon-tax-sweden---general-info.pdf)
- Petrol energy tax was cut repeatedly in the 2020s: 4.18 SEK/l (Jan 2022) → 3.44 (2023) → 2.57 (2024) → 1.84 (H1 2025) → 1.52 (from Jul 2025), with a temporary 0.70 SEK/l in May–Sep 2026; and the CO2 tax on petrol and diesel was temporarily cut by SEK 2.40/l (diesel SEK 2,400/m3) from 1 July to 30 November 2026 — [Skatteverket, Skatt på bränsle (rate tables)](https://www.skatteverket.se/4.15532c7b1442f256bae5e56.html); [Skatteverket news, 25 Jun 2026](https://www.skatteverket.se/foretag/skatterochavdrag/punktskatter/nyheterinompunktskatter/2026/nyheterinompunktskatter/tillfalligtsanktkoldioxidskattpabensinochdiesel.5.3129d65419ef1497c06893.html)
- About 95% of current carbon-tax revenue comes from motor fuels — [Ministry of Finance 2023 slides, slide 15](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/230323-carbon-tax-sweden---general-info.pdf)

**Sweden, residential and service-sector heating**
- Households and services pay 100% of the carbon tax; "the sharp CO2 tax increase was not combined with energy tax cuts on heating fuels used by households and services" (2000–2004) — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, Table 1 note A](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf); "Energy consumers in the residential sector are subject to 100% of the tax" — [Runst and Thonipara 2019 WP, p. 3](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/201853/1/1671064631.pdf)
- District heating: inputs quadrupled over 1970–2009; supplies over 75% of apartments; biofuel substitution was helped by "Government investment aid schemes promoting biofueled combined heat and power plants" already in place in 1991 — [Hammar and Åkerfeldt 2011, p. 9–10](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/hammar-and-akerfeldt-2011-co2-taxation-in-sweden.-20-years-of-experience-and-looking-ahead.pdf)
- "Fossil heating fuel use has since 1990 dropped by more than 90 %", replaced by district heating, pellet burners and heat pumps, with "Temporary aid schemes for conversion to renewable heating" — [Ministry of Finance 2023 slides, slide 14](https://www.government.se/contentassets/419eb2cafa93423c891c09cb9914801b/230323-carbon-tax-sweden---general-info.pdf)

**Finland**
- Finland introduced the first carbon tax in 1990 — [Mideksa 2021 CESifo WP 9172, abstract](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp9172.pdf); Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al. code its sectors as industry, transport and buildings, emission coverage 36% — [Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al. 2024, Table 1](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC11099057)
- Vehmas (2005) describes repeated ad hoc changes to the CO2/energy tax scheme, a growing number of exemptions, and CO2 tax increases serving mostly fiscal purposes; it says no systematic ex post analysis had been done (abstract-level only) — [Vehmas 2005 record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/2742618?lang=en_US)

**Norway**
- Carbon taxes introduced in 1991 as the main climate instrument; in 1999 rates ranged from US$51/t (gasoline, the maximum) to zero for exempt sectors (ferro-alloys, carbide, aluminium process coal/coke; land-based gas; cement and leca; air transport; foreign carriage, fishing, domestic sea transport); pulp and paper and herring flour paid about half; North Sea petroleum paid US$49/t (gas) and US$43/t (oil); average across all sources US$21/t (1 USD ≅ 7.8 NOK) — [Bruvoll and Larsen 2002 DP 337, Table 2 and p. 10–11](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp337.pdf)
- "the carbon taxes cover about 64 percent of total CO2 emissions in Norway" (1999) — [Bruvoll and Larsen 2002, p. 11](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp337.pdf); Yu, citing other sources, says only 60% are covered — [Yu 2023 WP, §5.1](https://repec.canterbury.ac.nz/cbt/econwp/2309.pdf)
- In the 1990s, 25–30% of Norway's CO2 came from the petroleum sector — [Bruvoll and Larsen 2002, p. 11](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp337.pdf)

**Denmark**
- Denmark introduced a carbon tax in 1992 at a low level (US$24 in 2015) and "the transport sector is exempted" — [Andersson 2017 WP, fn. 9](https://www.cccep.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Working-paper-212-Andersson_update_March2017.pdf); Yu describes it as "a carbon tax on energy products in 1992, though it exempted the transport industry" — [Yu 2023 WP, p. 9](https://repec.canterbury.ac.nz/cbt/econwp/2309.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a paper that reports "% increase in retail price attributable to the carbon tax", Sweden transport needs two caveats: the 1990 VAT raised the whole petrol price by 25% one year earlier, and in 2001–2005 the energy-tax cut largely cancelled the carbon-tax rise at the pump (Andersson calls the combined real tax "flat" over 2001–2005). The carbon tax's share of the tax wedge changed far more than the pump price did.
- The residential-heating case avoids the offsetting problem after 2000 (no energy-tax cut for households) but not in 1991 (the 50% energy-tax cut made heating oil's total tax "almost unaffected"). So the residential "treatment" really starts in 2001, which is how Runst and Thonipara define it.
- Norway's average carbon price (US$21/t in 1999) was well below its headline gasoline rate (US$51/t), and the exempt sectors were the most price-responsive ones; this design difference is likely central to the small Norwegian effects.

## 2. Carbon price path over time (local currency per tonne CO2, and US$/€)

### Takeaway
Sweden's general rate went from SEK 250/t (1991) to SEK 370 (1996–2000), jumped to SEK 910 by 2004, reached SEK 1,050 (2009–2011), about SEK 1,190–1,200 around 2020, and about SEK 1,520 in 2026 (≈ €140 or US$137 at 2022 exchange rates). Converted figures for the same year differ a lot across sources (1991: US$22 to US$30) because each source uses a different exchange-rate year. Finland's tax rose from about US$1.75 (1990) to US$23.39 (2005); Norway's petrol rate was about NOK 259/t (≈US$40) in 1991 and US$51/t in 1999.

## 3. Per-unit tax on each fuel and the % increase in the retail price attributable to the carbon tax

### Takeaway
In Sweden the carbon tax on petrol was SEK 0.58/l in 1991 (SEK 0.73/l with VAT), SEK 0.86/l in 2000, SEK 2.11/l in 2004–05 and SEK 3.27/l in 2025–26 (temporarily SEK 0.87/l in July–Nov 2026). At the two dates where I found a sourced pump price, the carbon tax plus the VAT on it was about 21% of the retail price, meaning it raised the price by about 26% over a no-carbon-tax counterfactual (April 2006 and May 2024; derived). No study reports this share for 1991, and I found no sourced 1991 retail price. Norway's carbon tax was 13% of the petrol purchaser price in 1999 (source-stated). I found no retail heating-oil prices for either country.

### Cited Findings

**Sweden: per-unit carbon tax**

## 4. Ex post estimates: method, data, period, counterfactual, headline reduction, significance, elasticities

### Takeaway
Sweden transport (Andersson 2019, synthetic control): transport CO2 per capita was 10.9% lower on average over 1990–2005 (–0.29 t per capita; 2.5 Mt/yr; 40.5 Mt cumulative), 12.5% lower in 2005, with placebo-based p = 0.067. About 6.3% (1.5 Mt/yr) is attributed to the carbon tax alone, using a carbon-tax semi-elasticity about three times the price semi-elasticity (elasticities –1.57 vs –0.51). Sweden residential (Runst and Thonipara 2020, synthetic control and difference-in-differences): 200–800 kg CO2 per capita per year lower after the 2001–2004 rise. Finland (Mideksa 2024, synthetic control on transport): 16% lower by 1995 rising to about 30% by 2004–05, but a 2026 replication shows this depends on Luxembourg as a donor. Older Finland and Norway evidence (Lin and Li 2011, difference-in-differences on total CO2; Bruvoll and Larsen 2004, decomposition plus a model-based counterfactual) finds small effects: about 1.7% slower growth (Finland) and about 2.3% lower CO2 (Norway, 1999).

## 5. Caveats, robustness checks, replications and critiques

### Takeaway
Andersson's Sweden transport result has been reproduced exactly and survives 14 alternative specifications (median –0.28 t vs –0.29 t) and dropping Denmark (combined effect 12.9%, carbon tax 7.7%). The weak links are the price-elasticity split (a more efficient estimator roughly halves the coefficients), the early-1990s recession, and the fact that it says little about other countries: the same method finds about 2.4% for Norway and is uninformative for Sweden's total emissions. Mideksa's large Finland effect is fragile to one donor (Luxembourg). The residential study has a contaminated pre-period and partly treated donors. Reviews split: Green (2021) reads the ex post literature as 0–2% a year; Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al. (2024) find 5–21% across 17 schemes.

**Andersson (2019): own robustness checks**
- Leave-one-out: average reduction from 13.0% (dropping Denmark) to 8.8% (dropping Switzerland); mean of six runs 10.4%; in-time placebos (1970, 1980) show no effect — [Andersson 2017 WP, §4.3](https://www.cccep.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Working-paper-212-Andersson_update_March2017.pdf)

**Critiques of the Finland evidence**
- Fernando (2026, I4R DP 317) reproduces Mideksa but finds the effect "entirely driven" by Luxembourg (a structural outlier with positive weight); without it the effect "falls to roughly a third of the original estimate" and is indistinguishable from placebos (abstract only) — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:zbw:i4rdps:317)
- Mideksa himself notes that donors' own fuel taxes would bias his estimate downward, and that restricting donors to countries without big fuel-tax reforms (Austria, Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Spain) gives larger effects "based on a some-what ad-hoc criterion" — [Mideksa 2021 WP, §3.4](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp9172.pdf)
- Finland's 1990–93 recession was "to some it was the Finnish great depression"; Mideksa's only test is that industrial CO2 shows no trend break around 1990 — [Mideksa 2021 WP, §3.3](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp9172.pdf)

**Critiques of the residential and Norway evidence**
- Runst and Thonipara: no clean pre-period (the tax existed from 1991); the parallel-trends assumption is "not completely fulfilled"; the treatment is binary because "there currently exists no information on carbon taxation rates for multiple countries over time" — [Runst and Thonipara 2019 WP, §2, §3.1, §5](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/201853/1/1671064631.pdf)
- Bruvoll and Larsen attribute the small Norwegian effect to "extensive tax exemptions and relatively inelastic demand in the sectors in which the tax is actually implemented"; they also note that general-equilibrium models "might overlook some important mechanisms ... which would generally yield larger effect" — [Bruvoll and Larsen 2002, abstract and §1](https://www.ssb.no/publikasjoner/DP/pdf/dp337.pdf)
- Andersson criticises Lin and Li's controls (Austria, Luxembourg, Ireland), their use of total CO2 including exempt sectors, and their "bad controls" (urbanisation, industry structure, energy price) — [Andersson 2017 WP, §5.2](https://www.cccep.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Working-paper-212-Andersson_update_March2017.pdf)

**How the reviews summarise these cases**
- Green (2021): "aggregate reductions from carbon pricing on emissions are limited—generally between 0% and 2% per year". ### Inferences
- Andersson's combined synthetic-control estimate is robust in reproduction terms (range –0.17 to –0.39 t across all reanalyses I found, i.e. roughly 6–15%). The "carbon tax alone" and "tax elasticity three times the price elasticity" claims are less secure: they rest on 42 annual observations, and their size depends on the estimator. Yu's AR(1) correction halves both coefficients while the ratio stays about 2.3 (–0.107 / –0.046, derived).
- The donor-pool problem runs both ways in this literature. Including partly treated donors (Denmark for Sweden; Norway and Denmark in Runst and Thonipara's samples 1 and 3) biases effects toward zero; including structural outliers (Luxembourg for Finland) can inflate them.
- For "ten natural experiments", Sweden transport and Sweden residential are the strongest Nordic cases. Finland is usable only with the Fernando (2026) caveat. Norway works best as a contrasting "high headline rate, broad exemptions, small effect" case (about 2% by both a model-based decomposition and a synthetic control).
