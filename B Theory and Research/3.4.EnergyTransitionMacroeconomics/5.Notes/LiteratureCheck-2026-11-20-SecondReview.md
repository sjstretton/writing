# Literature check, 20 November 2026: works named by the second referee ([A] = abstract or text read; [C] = citation metadata only)

Sources: citation metadata from the Crossref REST API unless stated; abstracts and text from Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, IDEAS/RePEc, the World Bank Documents and Reports site, NBER, the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, Cambridge Core, the Belfer Center and two repository copies, as named in each item. Quotations are copied from those records; footnote markers and line-break hyphens are removed. "Claim" is the bracketed claim on the referee list. Nothing below is filled from memory; gaps are marked. ScienceDirect, Springer, SSRN, the University of Chicago Press site and JSTOR refused automated access, and OpenAlex ran out of its free daily budget part-way through, so several Elsevier abstracts come from RePEc or a working-paper version, as stated.

1. **Roberts, M.J. and Spence, M. (1976)**, "Effluent charges and licenses under uncertainty", *Journal of Public Economics* 5(3–4): 193–208. DOI 10.1016/0047-2727(76)90014-1. **[C]**
   - Finding: no abstract could be read. Crossref, OpenAlex, IDEAS/RePEc and CORE hold none; Semantic Scholar says the abstract is withheld by the publisher; ScienceDirect refused access. The only description found is second-hand, in the abstract of Pizer's 1997 RFF working paper (item 2): "A hybrid policy, suggested by Roberts and Spence (1976), is also explored. Such a policy uses an initial distribution of tradeable permits to set a target emission level, but then allows additional permits to be purchased at a fixed "trigger" price."
   - Claim: not checked against the paper. The second-hand description confirms a licence-plus-price hybrid and a price ceiling; it does not mention the floor (subsidy) or the result that the mix beats both pure instruments.

2. **Pizer, W.A. (2002)**, "Combining price and quantity controls to mitigate global climate change", *Journal of Public Economics* 85(3): 409–434. DOI 10.1016/S0047-2727(01)00118-9. **[A]** (journal abstract as reproduced in a university library record, Shanghai University of Finance and Economics, marked "(C) 2002 Elsevier Science B.V."; not on Crossref, OpenAlex or RePEc. Working-paper abstract from IDEAS/RePEc.)
   - Finding (journal abstract): "Although most of the debate on global climate change policy has focused on quantity controls due to their political appeal, this paper argues that price controls are more efficient. Simulations based on a stochastic computable general equilibrium model indicate that the expected welfare gain from the optimal price policy is five times higher than the expected gain from the optimal quantity policy." And: "Even sub-optimal hybrid policies offer dramatic efficiency improvements over otherwise standard quantity controls. For example, a $50 trigger price per ton of carbon converts the $3 trillion expected loss associated with a simple 1990 emission target to a $150 billion gain. These results suggest that a hybrid policy is an attractive alternative to either a pure price or quantity system."
   - Finding (working-paper version, different title: "Prices vs. Quantities Revisited: The Case of Climate Change", RFF Working Paper, RePEc handle rff/dpaper/dp-98-02, dated October 1997): "The results indicate that an optimal tax policy generates gains which are five times higher than the optimal permit policy a $337 billion dollar gain versus $69 billion at the global level." And: "The optimal hybrid policy leads to welfare benefits only slightly higher than the optimal tax policy."
   - Claim: supported, with one qualification. Prices beat quantities (five times the expected welfare gain). The hybrid is "an attractive alternative to either"; the working paper says the optimal hybrid is only "slightly" better than the optimal tax, so "outperforms either pure instrument" is right but the margin over a pure price is small. The $337 billion and $69 billion figures are from the working paper, not the journal abstract.

3. **Harstad, B. (2020)**, "Technology and Time Inconsistency", *Journal of Political Economy* 128(7): 2653–2689. DOI 10.1086/707024. **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and IDEAS/RePEc).
   - Finding: "Policy makers have time-inconsistent preferences if they fear losing power or are endowed with hyperbolic discount factors. Politicians may thus seek to influence future policy choices, for example, by investing in green technologies that motivate later politicians to act sustainably. I show that optimal investment subsidies are larger for technologies that are strategic complements to future investments, that are further upstream in the supply chain, or that are characterized by longer maturity. Time inconsistency can rationalize subsidies at similar levels as market failures such as externalities can."
   - Claim: supported. The abstract does not use the words "commitment device"; it says investment is a way "to influence future policy choices". The source of the problem is time-inconsistent preferences (fear of losing power, or hyperbolic discounting).

4. **Acemoglu, D. and Rafey, W. (2023)**, "Mirage on the horizon: Geoengineering and carbon taxation without commitment", *Journal of Public Economics* 219: 104802. DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2022.104802. Working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 24411 (March 2018), DOI 10.3386/w24411. **[A]** (journal abstract, IDEAS/RePEc).
   - Finding: "A benevolent social planner sets carbon taxes without commitment. Higher future carbon taxes both reduce emissions given technology and encourage energy producers to switch to cleaner technology. Geoengineering advances, which reduce the negative environmental effects of the existing stock of carbon, decrease future carbon taxes and thus discourage private investments in conventional clean technology. We characterize the conditions under which these advances diminish—rather than improve—environmental quality and welfare, and show that given current estimates of costs and environmental damages, these conditions are likely to be satisfied in our model."
   - Claim: supported. Note the paper's own point is narrower: a geoengineering breakthrough lowers expected future carbon taxes and so can reduce clean investment and welfare.

5. **Arrow, K.J. and Lind, R.C. (1970)**, "Uncertainty and the Evaluation of Public Investment Decisions", *American Economic Review* 60(3): 364–378. No Crossref DOI for the 1970 article; volume, issue and pages from IDEAS/RePEc (handle aea/aecrev/v60y1970i3p364-78). Reprints with DOIs: in Layard and Glaister (eds), *Cost-Benefit Analysis*, Cambridge University Press, 1994, pp. 160–178, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511521942.005; *Journal of Natural Resources Policy Research* 6(1): 29–44 (2014), DOI 10.1080/19390459.2014.867640. **[A]** (opening paragraphs only, from the Cambridge Core summary of the 1994 reprint; the result itself was not read).
   - Finding: "The issue is whether it is appropriate to discount public investments in the same way as private investments." And: "A second position is that the government can better cope with uncertainty than private investors and, therefore, government investments should not be evaluated by the same criterion used in private markets."
   - Claim: not confirmed from the text read. The opening states the question and the competing positions, not the paper's result. The risk-spreading result (cost of risk-bearing going to zero as risk is spread over many taxpayers) and "close to the risk-free rate" were not seen in the paper's own words; JSTOR and the reprints are closed. A web-search summary attributes to the paper the phrase "when the risks associated with a public investment are publicly borne, the total cost of risk-bearing is insignificant", quoted in a later article; that is second-hand and unverified.

6. **Bovenberg, A.L. and de Mooij, R.A. (1994)**, "Environmental Levies and Distortionary Taxation", *American Economic Review* 84(4): 1085–1089. No Crossref DOI for the 1994 article; volume, issue and pages from IDEAS/RePEc (handle aea/aecrev/v84y1994i4p1085-89). The Tilburg University research portal record gives the same pages but volume "94", which looks like a typing error. Reprint: in *Environmental Policy Making in Economies with Prior Tax Distortions*, Edward Elgar, 2002, pp. 154–158, DOI 10.4337/9781035304639.00014. **[C]**
   - Finding: no abstract exists on IDEAS/RePEc, Crossref or Semantic Scholar. The Tilburg repository PDF returned "403 Forbidden" and a third-party copy of the JSTOR offprint was on a host this session could not reach (blog.rchss.sinica.edu.tw).
   - Claim: not checked against the paper. Indirect support only: the abstract of Goulder's reader's guide (item 7) says that "the conditions under which the strong double dividend claim is rejected (upheld) are closely related to the conditions under which the second-best optimal environmental tax is less than (greater than) the marginal environmental damages."

7. **Goulder, L.H. (1995)**, "Environmental taxation and the double dividend: A reader's guide", *International Tax and Public Finance* 2(2): 157–183. DOI 10.1007/BF00877495. Working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 4896 (October 1994), DOI 10.3386/w4896. **[A]** (abstract of the NBER working paper, OpenAlex; the journal abstract was not reachable: Springer refused access and RePEc holds none).
   - Finding: "A weak double dividend claim is that returning tax revenues through cuts in distortionary taxes leads to cost savings relative to the case where revenues are returned lump sum. This claim is easily defended on theoretical grounds and (thankfully) receives wide support from numerical simulations. The stronger versions contend that revenue-neutral swaps of environmental taxes for ordinary distortionary taxes involve zero or negative gross costs. Theoretical analyses and numerical results tend to cast doubt on the strong double dividend claim. At the same time, the theoretical case against the strong form is not air-tight, and the numerical evidence is mixed."
   - Claim: supported, from the working-paper abstract. "Usually fails" should be read with the abstract's own hedge: the case against the strong form "is not air-tight".

8. **Barrage, L. (2020)**, "Optimal Dynamic Carbon Taxes in a Climate–Economy Model with Distortionary Fiscal Policy", *The Review of Economic Studies* 87(1): 1–39. DOI 10.1093/restud/rdz055. Online 15 October 2019. (Crossref gives no volume or pages; these are from IDEAS/RePEc.) **[A]** (abstract, Crossref).
   - Finding: "The central quantitative finding is that optimal carbon tax schedules are 8–24% lower when there are distortionary taxes, compared to the setting with lump-sum taxes considered in the literature."
   - Claim: supported. The difference is 8–24% lower.

9. **Bento, A.M., Jacobsen, M.R. and Liu, A.A. (2018)**, "Environmental policy in the presence of an informal sector", *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 90: 61–77. DOI 10.1016/j.jeem.2018.03.011. **[A]** (abstract, IDEAS/RePEc and Semantic Scholar).
   - Finding: "First, energy taxes indirectly tax the informal sector because even informal firms must buy formal sector sources of energy such as electricity." And: "Even though the U.S. informal sector is relatively small, we find that distortionary costs of energy taxes are reduced by more than half. In developing countries, where the informal sector is typically much larger, the effect we identify here become even more relevant: we find negative gross costs in many cases."
   - Claim: supported. The abstract also names two countervailing effects (informal fuels; informal production substituting for energy-intensive goods) and finds that "welfare enhancing effects typically dominate".

10. **Dorband, I.I., Jakob, M., Kalkuhl, M. and Steckel, J.C. (2019)**, "Poverty and distributional effects of carbon pricing in low- and middle-income countries – A global comparative analysis", *World Development* 115: 246–257. DOI 10.1016/j.worlddev.2018.11.015. **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and IDEAS/RePEc).
    - Finding: "we assess the expected incidence of moderate carbon price increases for different income groups in 87 mostly low- and middle-income countries. Building on a consistent dataset and method, we find that for countries with per capita incomes of below USD 15,000 per year (at PPP-adjusted 2011 USD) carbon pricing has, on average, progressive distributional effects." And: "We argue that an inverse U-shape relationship between energy expenditure shares and income explains why carbon pricing tends to be regressive in countries with relatively higher income."
    - Claim: supported. The threshold is USD 15,000 per person per year (2011 PPP), and the result holds "on average".

11. **Hirth, L. (2013)**, "The market value of variable renewables", *Energy Economics* 38: 218–236. DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2013.02.004. (Crossref and RePEc list the title without the subtitle given by the referee.) **[A]** (abstract, IDEAS/RePEc).
    - Finding: "Because the drop is larger with more installed capacity, the market value of VRE falls with higher penetration rate." And: "We find the value of wind power to fall from 110% of the average power price to 50–80% as wind penetration increases from zero to 30% of total electricity consumption. For solar power, similarly low value levels are reached already at 15% penetration."
    - Claim: supported. The evidence is for the European electricity market (the EMMA model, a literature review and market data).

12. **Goulder, L.H. and Mathai, K. (2000)**, "Optimal CO2 Abatement in the Presence of Induced Technological Change", *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 39(1): 1–38. DOI 10.1006/jeem.1999.1089. Working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 6494 (April 1998), DOI 10.3386/w6494. **[A]** (journal abstract, OpenAlex).
    - Finding: "We show analytically in a cost-effectiveness setting that the presence of induced technological change (ITC) always implies a lower time profile of optimal carbon taxes. The same is true in a benefit–cost setting as long as damages are convex in the atmospheric CO2 concentration. The impact of ITC on the optimal abatement path varies. When knowledge is gained through R&D investments, the presence of ITC justifies shifting some abatement from the present to the future. However, when knowledge is accumulated via learning-by-doing the impact on the timing of abatement is analytically ambiguous." And: "The impact of ITC on the timing of abatement is very slight, but the effect (applicable in the benefit–cost case) on cumulative abatement over time can be large, especially when knowledge is generated through learning-by doing."
    - Claim: supported. Induced technological change lowers the optimal carbon tax path; with R&D it shifts some abatement later; with learning by doing the timing effect is ambiguous. The working-paper abstract differs on the last point: it says the effect on total abatement "is generally small as well, especially when knowledge is accumulated via R&D". Use the journal wording.

13. **Nordhaus, W.D. (2014)**, "The Perils of the Learning Model for Modeling Endogenous Technological Change", *The Energy Journal* 35(1): 1–14. DOI 10.5547/01956574.35.1.1. Working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 14638 (January 2009), DOI 10.3386/w14638. **[A]** (abstract, Crossref).
    - Finding: "First, it shows that there is a fundamental statistical identification problem in trying to separate learning from exogenous technological change and that the estimated learning coefficient will generally be biased upwards. Second, we present two empirical tests that illustrate the potential bias in practice and show that learning parameters are not robust to alternative specifications. Finally, we show that an overestimate of the learning coefficient will provide incorrect estimates of the total marginal cost of output and will therefore bias optimization models to tilt toward technologies that are incorrectly specified as having high learning coefficients."
    - Claim: supported. The abstract's wording on the consequence is that models "tilt toward technologies" wrongly given high learning coefficients; it does not use the words "gains from deployment".

14. **Nemet, G.F. (2006)**, "Beyond the learning curve: factors influencing cost reductions in photovoltaics", *Energy Policy* 34(17): 3218–3232. DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2005.06.020. **[A]** (abstract of what appears to be the preliminary version, under a different title: "Technical Change in Photovoltaics and the Applicability of the Learning Curve Model", IIASA Interim Report IR-05-029, April 2005; the journal abstract is on no open source).
    - Finding (working-paper version): "Which changes were most important in accounting for the cost reductions that have occurred over the past three decades? Are these results consistent with the widely held learning-by-doing theory of technological change? We gather empirical data and populate a simple model to identify the most important factors affecting the cost of PV. We find that the learning curve theory only weakly explains change in the most important factors—plant size, module efficiency, and the cost of silicon."
    - Claim: supported in substance, from the working-paper version. The factors named are plant size, module efficiency and the cost of silicon. The abstract says learning "only weakly explains" them; it gives no share, so "a small part" is a paraphrase.

15. **Murphy, K.M., Shleifer, A. and Vishny, R.W. (1989)**, "Industrialization and the Big Push", *Journal of Political Economy* 97(5): 1003–1026. DOI 10.1086/261641. Working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 2708 (September 1988), DOI 10.3386/w2708. **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and IDEAS/RePEc).
    - Finding: "This paper explores Rosenstein-Rodan's idea that simultaneous industrialization of many sectors of the economy can be profitable for them all even when no sector can break even industrializing alone. We analyze this idea in the context of an imperfectly competitive economy with aggregate demand spillovers and interpret the big push into industrialization as a move from a bad to a good equilibrium."
    - Claim: supported. The abstract's mechanism is "aggregate demand spillovers"; the working-paper abstract adds shared infrastructure ("a railroad") as one of three channels.

16. **Li, S., Tong, L., Xing, J. and Zhou, Y. (2017)**, "The Market for Electric Vehicles: Indirect Network Effects and Policy Design", *Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists* 4(1): 89–133. DOI 10.1086/689702. **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and IDEAS/RePEc).
    - Finding: "Based on quarterly EV sales and charging station deployment in 353 metro areas from 2011 to 2013, our empirical analysis finds indirect network effects on both sides of the market, with those on the EV demand side being stronger. The federal income tax credit of up to $7,500 for EV buyers contributed to about 40% of EV sales during 2011–13, with feedback loops explaining 40% of that increase. A policy of equal-sized spending but subsidizing charging station deployment could have been more than twice as effective in promoting EV adoption."
    - Claim: supported. United States data, 2011–13.

17. **Böhringer, C., Balistreri, E.J. and Rutherford, T.F. (2012)**, "The role of border carbon adjustment in unilateral climate policy: Overview of an Energy Modeling Forum study (EMF 29)", *Energy Economics* 34 (Supplement 2): S97–S110. DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2012.10.003. **[A]** (journal abstract, IDEAS/RePEc; numbers from the Belfer Center summary page of the discussion-paper version).
    - Finding (journal abstract): "We find that border carbon adjustment can effectively reduce leakage and ameliorate adverse impacts on energy-intensive and trade-exposed industries of unilaterally abating countries. However, the scope for global cost savings is small. The main effect of border carbon adjustment is to shift the economic burden of emission reduction to non-abating countries through implicit changes in international prices."

In the simulations with BCA in place, leakage rates range between 2% and 12%, with a mean value of 8%." And: "Cost savings from implementing BCA range from 0% to 18%, with a mean of 7.7%."

18. **Daniel, K.D., Litterman, R.B. and Wagner, G. (2019)**, "Declining CO2 price paths", *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 116(42): 20886–20891. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1817444116. **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar).
    - Finding: "In contrast to most modeled CO2 price paths, EZ-Climate suggests a high price today that is expected to decline over time as the "insurance" value of mitigation declines and technological change makes emissions cuts cheaper. Second, higher risk aversion increases both the CO2 price and the risk premium relative to expected damages. Lastly, our model suggests large costs associated with delays in pricing CO2 emissions. In our base case, delaying implementation by 1 y leads to annual consumption losses of over 2%, a cost that roughly increases with the square of time per additional year of delay."
    - Claim: supported. The abstract gives two reasons for the decline: the falling "insurance" value of mitigation and cheaper abatement through technological change.

19. **Inderst, G. and Stewart, F. (2014)**, *Institutional Investment in Infrastructure in Emerging Markets and Developing Economies*. Washington, DC: Public-Private Infrastructure Advisory Facility (PPIAF), World Bank Group, March 2014. World Bank report number 91307 (public Documents and Reports site). No DOI for the PPIAF report; Crossref lists an SSRN copy as DOI 10.2139/ssrn.2494261. It is not a Policy Research Working Paper: Policy Research Working Paper 6780 (February 2014, DOI 10.1596/1813-9450-6780) is a different paper by the same authors, "Institutional Investment in Infrastructure in Developing Countries: Introduction to Potential Models". **[A]** (abstract and full text, World Bank Documents and Reports).
    - Finding (abstract): "Investment volumes are currently still low but, nonetheless, interesting practical examples can be found in different countries and projects."
    - Finding (text, section 5.1): "Pension assets in developing countries are currently about USD 2.5 trillion, with the potential to reach USD 17 to 25 trillion by 2050 (World Bank estimates based on OECD data)." And: "Pension assets in OECD countries are 78 percent of GDP on a weighted average (see Figure 18 in Appendix 2). Among other countries, only South Africa, Namibia and Chile have a pension assets per GDP figure above or near that level. About a dozen developing countries—primarily in Latin America—figures between 10 percent and 30 percent, whereas pension assets are still very small relative to GDP in all other developing countries, if not nil (see Figure 19 in Appendix 2)." And: "In absolute terms, the biggest pension assets are in China (USD 1,214 billion), Brazil (USD 308 billion), Mexico (USD 148 billion), Chile (USD 145 billion), Malaysia (USD 106 billion), India (USD 129 billion), South Africa (USD 84 billion), Egypt (USD 54 billion) and Russia (USD 78 billion) (OECD, 2013b, and Wright et al., 2011)."
    - Finding (text, allocation): "The asset allocation to infrastructure of the 10 EMDE pension funds is 5.7 percent on average, which is higher than the global average of 3.9 percent. However, this is driven by relatively high weightings in listed equities (2.5 percent vs. 0.5 percent) and debt (2.0 percent vs. 0.4 percent) that are often not reported by pension funds in developed markets. Compared to the assets of the full universe of pension funds in the survey (including those not reporting infrastructure investments), the asset allocation in developing countries is 0.4 percent, against a global average of 0.8 percent." The 5.7 percent figure covers only the 10 EMDE funds that report infrastructure holdings in the OECD survey ("about USD 400 billion, covering one sixth of the estimated overall EMDE pensions assets of USD 2.5 trillion"); across all surveyed funds the developing-country allocation is 0.4 percent. The data are for about 2011–2012.

20. **Barr, N. and Diamond, P. (2006)**, "The Economics of Pensions", *Oxford Review of Economic Policy* 22(1): 15–39. DOI 10.1093/oxrep/grj002. (Crossref lists only "Barr, N." as author; IDEAS/RePEc and the article's first page give Nicholas Barr and Peter Diamond.) **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and IDEAS/RePEc; text from a copy of the published article hosted by the South African National Treasury, with the journal header and page numbers).
    - Finding (abstract): "After introductory discussion, successive sections consider the effects of different pension arrangements on labour markets, on national savings and growth, and on the distribution of burdens and benefits." The abstract states no result.
    - Finding (text): "To that end, future output is central. PAYG and funding are simply financial mechanisms for organizing claims on that future output." On saving: "On the first, the impact of an increase in funding on national savings can be anything from negative to large positive depending on the reaction of private savers and of the rest of the government budget." On demography: "Though the point was shown to be flawed many years ago, the argument that funding necessarily assists pension finance significantly in the face of demographic change still reappears." And: "As noted earlier, PAYG and funding are both mechanisms for organizing claims on future output; since demographic change generally affects that output, it generally causes problems for pension schemes, however they are organized." And: "In sum, the argument is not that funding is bad policy, nor that it cannot help with population aging, but that its helpfulness is contingent on beneficial effects on growth and on country-specific factors".
    - Claim: supported, from the text (not the abstract). The paper's wording is conditional: funding may raise saving and may help with ageing, but not necessarily.

21. **Nordhaus, W. (2015)**, "Climate Clubs: Overcoming Free-riding in International Climate Policy", *American Economic Review* 105(4): 1339–1370. DOI 10.1257/aer.15000001. **[A]** (abstract, Crossref).

23. **Fried, S., Novan, K. and Peterman, W.B. (2022)**, "Climate policy transition risk and the macroeconomy", *European Economic Review* 147: 104174. DOI 10.1016/j.euroecorev.2022.104174. Working-paper version: Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Working Paper 2021-06 (version of June 2022), DOI 10.24148/wp2021-06. **[A]** (journal abstract, IDEAS/RePEc; numbers from the text of the working paper).
    - Finding (journal abstract): "We find that climate policy transition risk decreases carbon emissions today by causing investment to become relatively cleaner and output to fall. This result counters the Green Paradox, which argues that climate policy risk raises emissions today by increasing incentives to extract fossil fuel, expanding its supply. Even allowing for the supply-side response, we find the demand-side response dominates, and the net effect of climate policy transition risk is still to reduce emissions today."
    - Finding (working-paper version, June 2022): the tax is "$51 (in 2020 dollars) per ton of CO2"; "In our main specification, we assume that firms believe there is a 75 percent chance the government will introduce this tax within the next ten years." Results: "climate policy transition risk reduces emissions by 2.12 percent. For comparison, emissions fall by 20.34 percent in the policy steady state." "This reduction in emissions from climate policy transition risk is equivalent to the reduction that would be achieved in a steady state with no transition risk and a carbon tax of $4.91 per ton." "First, climate policy transition risk decreases the expected return to investments in fossil capital relative to clean capital, leading to an 8.86 percent decrease in the ratio of fossil to clean capital. This composition effect is responsible for 84 percent of the decline in emissions caused by climate policy transition risk." "Second, climate policy transition risk reduces output by 0.34 percent."
    - Claim: supported. All numbers are from the working-paper version, for the United States; the journal abstract has none. Note that output falls as well: the emissions cut is not free.

24. **Kavlak, G., McNerney, J. and Trancik, J.E. (2018)**, "Evaluating the causes of cost reduction in photovoltaic modules", *Energy Policy* 123: 700–710. DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2018.08.015. **[A]** (abstract, IDEAS/RePEc and Semantic Scholar).
    - Finding: "We find that increased module efficiency was the leading low-level cause of cost reduction in 1980–2012, contributing almost 25% of the decline. Government-funded and private R&D was the most important high-level mechanism over this period. After 2001, however, scale economies became a more significant cause of cost reduction, approaching R&D in importance. Policies that stimulate market growth have played a key role in enabling PV's cost reduction, through privately-funded R&D and scale economies, and to a lesser extent learning-by-doing."
    - Claim: partly supported. The abstract gives the ranking (R&D first, scale economies close behind after 2001, learning by doing least) but not the percentage shares for the three; the only share in the abstract is "almost 25%" for module efficiency. The shares would need the full text (closed).

25. **Steffen, B. (2020)**, "Estimating the cost of capital for renewable energy projects", *Energy Economics* 88: 104783. DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2020.104783. **[A]** (abstract, OpenAlex and IDEAS/RePEc).
    - Finding: "We then evaluate the empirical evidence from 46 countries for the period 2009–2017. We find a globally consistent rank order among technologies, with the cost of capital increasing from solar PV to onshore wind to offshore wind power.
