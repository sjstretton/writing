# Peer review of 3.4, note v0.1 (20 Nov 2026)

A referee-style review of *The Energy Transition Swaps a Fuel Bill for a Capital Bill* (Theory and Research Note No. 3.4, v0.1; master saved 13:16 UTC, 24-page PDF).

What was checked, and how. I read the master, the PDF (text and page images), the bibliography notes, the four evidence files, the script and its output, and the five sibling briefs (3.2 and 4.2 in part). A scratch copy of `egypt_estimates.py` reproduces the output file of 12:56 UTC line for line (macro models not re-run), and every Egypt number I traced in the note matches that output to rounding. Results from the copy at other rates are marked "my re-run". **During the review the script, output, CSVs and figures in `2.Source` were regenerated (13:26–13:27 UTC) with a new central case; the note was not** (M11).

## Summary judgement

The note has a clear claim, a title that states it, and headings and bold lead sentences that pass the skim test. The page budget is met: the executive summary runs 1.3 pages (pp. 2–3) and the body exactly twelve (pp. 4–15). The sector accounting is sound: electricity transfers net out, and I found no double counting inside Table 2. The Egypt run makes the title literal (fuel saved of $21.0 billion against a capital charge of $21.0 billion at 6 percent), which the note does not say. Numbers quoted from the sibling briefs match, with one exception (M4).

Six things keep it below the standard: the two costs of capital are not comparable (M1); the headline gap covers capital no product reaches (M2); the levels rest on one assumed industry cost (M3); "guarantees are cheap" divides by the wrong thing (M4); the returns contrast and the cost of waiting rest on a functional form and a tautology (M5, M6); and the macroeconomic implications are the thinnest part (M7). The central claim is argued for a plant and asserted for an economy.

Three readers. A sceptical macroeconomist will say the premium is real risk that a guarantee moves and does not remove, that senior claims raise the cost of Egypt's other debt, and that the rates mix real and nominal: not answered. A World Bank treasury official will say capital, not expected loss, is the constraint, and that preferred status does not travel to a utility's contract at this scale: half answered. An Egyptian finance official will say Egypt already buys at 2.4 cents, targets a faster path, has raised prices since 2024 and is cutting guarantees under its IMF program: not answered.

## Major points

**M1. The two rates are not comparable, and "10.3 percent" is mis-described.** The note calls 10.3 percent "the rate financiers reported for solar power in Egypt". Its bibliography note records 9.71 percent (IRENA survey: nominal, after tax, 2020). The 10.343 is the tool's average of that figure and a benchmark real rate of 8.8 percent inflated at 2 percent. The 6 percent is presented as an IBRD loan rate (nominal, floating, November 2026); the tool's method note defines it as a real social discount rate and warns that "Market WACC is nominal and 6% is real". The model runs in constant prices. A sovereign loan rate is also not a project's cost of capital: brief 3.1's stylized guarantee removes 1.4 points, not 4.3. With both rates deflated by 2 percent (8.2 and 3.9), my re-run gives a 2035 net cost of 1.0 percent of GDP at the local rate and a saving of 0.4 percent de-risked (gap 1.4). If 6 percent is real, the gap is 0.8. *Fix:* state both rates in real terms, with dates; call 10.3 percent "the author's blend of two IRENA figures"; show which product removes which part of the gap.

**M2. The headline gap is not what the products can close.** The $9.4 billion is the charge on all $266 billion of capital at one rate less the other. Power is $4.5 billion of it (48 percent); industry is $3.3 billion, buildings $1.0 billion and vehicles $0.6 billion, and nothing in Table 3 is aimed at a factory, a boiler or a car. "Would close most of that gap" has no support. In power the counterfactual is wrong: Kom Ombo (2.47 cents) and the Red Sea wind farm (2.4 cents) sell below the model's de-risked cost of solar ($29) and wind ($34), and the path's 38 percent in 2030 is below the government's 42 percent by 2028. The model's clock starts in 2025, so "2035" means year eleven. "Because the state carries their risk in dollars" joins the prices of two projects to the terms of a third (Obelisk), whose tariff is undisclosed. *Fix:* claim the power share only ("up to $4.5 billion a year, 0.75 percent of GDP"); state the gain over today's contracts as less sovereign exposure per gigawatt, and estimate it; count years from the program's start.

**M3. The levels are fragile and mostly one assumption.** Industry supplies $9.3 billion of the $10.7 billion net cost, on an assumed $150 per gigajoule. Across Table 5 the 2035 net cost at the local rate runs from −$9.6 billion to +$21.5 billion. Power "pays for itself" by $0.6 billion on a charge of $13.5 billion, costs $1.9 billion at 14.8 percent, and breaks even only because 107 of its 202 terawatt-hours are sold at cost to electrified end uses, 78 to industry. Table 2 omits the running-cost column, so no row adds up (13.5 − 7.0 is not −0.6), and the text invites the reader to take 30.4 − 21.0 = 9.4 as the net cost. Nothing is set against the World Bank's published country report (2022: $96 billion of extra power investment over 2030–50), which is in the evidence file. *Fix:* lead with power and transport; report industry as an assumed upper bound; give ranges; add the column and a benchmark paragraph.

**M4. "Guarantees are cheap" divides by the wrong thing.** The note gives three ratios (80, 2.7 and 2) and does not reconcile them. The 80 is brief 4.1's figure at a three-point saving on sovereign loans; the note pairs it with "some four points" (about 105) and omits 4.1's own 27 (net gain) and 13 (prudent buffer). Product 1 guarantees a state utility, and for public non-sovereign borrowers the same statistics give default of 2.61 percent and recovery of 85.8 percent: an expected loss of 0.37 percent, ten times higher, and a ratio near 10. Expected loss is also not the scarce resource. The note says a guarantee uses capital as a loan does (on a 2014 source "not checked" for 2026), so "guarantees priced at expected loss" asks the Bank to give capital away. Brief 4.1's point that senior claims raise the cost of other debt is missing. Scale is unchecked: the World Bank Group's guarantee target is $20 billion a year worldwide by 2030; the path's power investment in Egypt is $13.5 billion a year. *Fix:* one ratio per product, from the right loss record; add saving per dollar of capital used; size the buffer fund for Egypt.

**M5. The returns contrast is drawn, not shown.** Figure 1's two panels plot the same curve two ways. Fuel use under a constant elasticity and cost under a constant learning rate are both power laws: price steps cut 6.7, 5.5, 4.6 points; doublings cut 20, 16, 12.8 points, and each doubling needs twice the deployment. The price half has no source: the evidence file says "the rising-marginal-cost half: NOT FOUND", and working paper 5.5 observes $20 to $125 only. A carbon price is a transfer that raises revenue, so points per $50 is not a return on resources. For Egypt the learning is someone else's: 7.5 gigawatts a year cannot move a 670-gigawatt market. *Fix:* define each term in a sentence ("with technology fixed, each further tonne costs more; with deployment, each further unit costs less"); redraw on common axes, cost per tonne against cumulative abatement; say that the increasing return accrues to the world.

**M6. The cost of waiting restates the sign of the net cost.** The +$9 billion is +$20 billion in power and transport less $11 billion saved by postponing the rest. The present values discount at the financing rate, as if a guarantee changed Egypt's time preference. At a de-risked rate of 7.5 percent the cost of waiting is zero; in real terms (3.9 percent) it is $29 billion (my re-runs). Fact 12 also argues for waiting: brief 4.1 says "falling technology costs reward waiting". *Fix:* report power and transport only, at one stated social discount rate; add the extra fuel bill ($178 billion) and emissions; answer the technology objection.

**M7. The macroeconomic implications are thin, and the fiscal ones out of date.** Section 2 restates facts; the implications sit in a column of Table 1. (a) The J-curve is a trade balance. Interest at 6 percent on foreign finance for the imported half (about $133 billion by 2035) is $8 billion a year, which turns +0.8 percent of GDP in 2035 into about −0.6 (my arithmetic). (b) The macro range is not a result: the higher model has GDP 30 percent above baseline by 2050, and the note omits both models' debt paths (94 percent of GDP in 2030). (c) "The budget's share" (2.0 percent of GDP) exceeds the bill it is a share of (0.2): one is a financing need for the whole path, the other an annualized net cost. De-risking moves it 0.2 points and tariffs 1.1, which sits badly with the central claim. (d) "Tariffs as today" are 2024 data. The IMF's seventh review records retail fuel at cost recovery since December 2025; electricity tariffs rose three times in 2026; and guarantees to the state oil company are due to fall by a quarter, where the note proposes more. *Fix:* for each implication say what the finance ministry, central bank and debt office do differently; add interest to Figure 3; report debt; update the baseline.

**M8. Several facts are stronger than their sources, and four are the note's own model.**

- Facts 25, 26, 29 and 30 are "Supported" by the author's unpublished run, fact 11 by an assumed elasticity, fact 21 by nothing. The introduction promises "published evidence".
- "Behavior change supplies about 4 percent": the evidence file records 8 percent. "China produces over 75 percent of the world's batteries": the record says "NOT FOUND".
- "About half" (Prest) is from a working paper not read, is the low end of 50 to 75, concerns an oil supply cut, and is applied to gas.
- "$12 trillion" holds at a 1.4 percent discount rate; the source gives $5 trillion at 5 percent.
- The ADB source says "up to $5"; IBRD's "never lost" omits "two outstanding cases excepted"; the Wood Mackenzie figure is for the United States.
- "About half of it is imported" is fact in the executive summary and assumption in the body. "No developing country can supply the increase": China and India do.
- `[VERIFY]` markers remain on Batini (2021) and Xinhua (2026).

**M9. Coverage of the brief.** (a) The products are seven with the buffer fund and tenders, and only 1 to 3 survive one reading. Product 5 holds two ideas that pull apart (finance in the supplier's currency; contracts in the buyer's), and its evidence is yen bonds. Product 3 is a bond guarantee in brief 3.2. Product 4 swaps 4.1's emissions balance for tariff conditions, close to the Bank's policy-based guarantee. The note never says how the five differ from what the Bank sells now. (b) "How can we quantify the grid?" goes unanswered, though the output has the numbers ($24 against $15 per megawatt-hour). (c) Pensions: do the check or drop the fact. (d) Fiscal results are not by sector, and GDP results are neither by sector nor at two rates. (e) The overall implication, that a transition that pays may come too late, appears only in the last section. (f) Missing facts: fuel-tax bases erode; the gas fleet's fixed costs outlive its use. *Fix:* three products and a buffer; a five-row facts table in the body, the thirty merged into Appendix A (Table 1 takes 2.3 of twelve pages).

**M10. Confidentiality and tone.** The README says the Egypt estimates are "not yet cleared", yet note, script and output sit in a public repository. The subtitle says "Five Simple World Bank Products"; the model sources are titled "... in CPAT" and the tool's method note describes inputs as CPAT data; tables credit "Mitigation Investment Needs Model (2026)" as if a third party. The note sets Egypt's IBRD debt against a limit the evidence file says Egypt is "not near". "Does not try to" and the stale 45 percent will read badly in Cairo. *Fix:* hold the Egypt section until cleared, or present a stylized gas importer; "products the World Bank could offer"; "Source: author's calculations"; name the public file behind each input; cut the limit; credit the 2025–26 reforms.

**M11. The note and its source folder are out of step.** The 13:26 output values gas at $12. Net cost in 2035 is now 1.3 percent of GDP and −0.25 (was 1.8 and 0.2); waiting −4 and +20; the gap, unchanged at $9.4 billion, now exceeds the net cost, so "nine-tenths" fails. The figure files show the new case, so a re-render sets Figures 2 and 3 against Table 2. *Fix:* update every Egypt number in one pass.

## Minor points

1. Layout: page 20 is blank; headings 3 and 4 sit at the foot of pages 10 and 13; a third of page 11 is empty; Table 1 breaks over three pages; the "Evidence" and "Product" columns are too narrow.
2. Figure 2 repeats two columns of Table 2; the spare unit-cost figure makes the central claim better. Figure 3 does not draw the J. Both have labels for titles.
3. One thing, two names: "payment guarantee" (3.1: payment intermediary facility), "refinancing bond" (synthetic sovereign bond), "buffer fund" (Climate Investment Trust), "buyer" (offtaker). One name, two things: "de-risked" (a guarantee; a loan rate), "the program" (whole path; increment).
4. Process residue: "no evidence on their size was found for this note"; "No use at project level was found"; "could not be checked"; "at the tool's default".
5. Repeated contrast: "a choice, not a forecast"; "cheap, not free" (three times); "used and not used up". Section 5 opens by restating each section and closes on the executive summary's last lines.
6. The executive summary mixes 2035 and 2030 without dates, never says "illustrative" and gives no price year.
7. Two values: electricity "a half" and "50–55"; storage "three-quarters" against 1.8 to 2.7 times in the Egypt output; batteries $250 (model) against $192 and $125 (cited). "$30 billion" is the whole path, not power.
8. US usage: "forever", "around the clock". Define IEA and IBRD in the body. I found no reflexive hedging.
