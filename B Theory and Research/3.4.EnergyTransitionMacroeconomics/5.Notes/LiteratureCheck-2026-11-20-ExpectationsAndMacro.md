Method. Citations are from the Crossref REST API unless another source is named. Abstracts are from Crossref, OpenAlex, IDEAS/RePEc, the Semantic Scholar API, the publisher page or an open working-paper version; the source read is named in each entry. ## A. Investment under uncertainty and policy credibility

**1. [A]** Pindyck, Robert S. (1991). "Irreversibility, uncertainty, and investment." *Journal of Economic Literature* 29(3): 1110-1148. No DOI in Crossref for the journal article (working-paper version: NBER Working Paper 3307, 1990, DOI 10.3386/w3307). Volume, issue and pages from IDEAS/RePEc.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract, borrowed from another version; NBER abstract via OpenAlex): the paper "reviews some basic models of irreversible investment to illustrate the option-like characteristics of investment opportunities". NBER abstract: investments "can be delayed, allowing the firm to wait for new information"; "Irreversible investment is especially sensitive to risk"; "if a policy goal is to stimulate investment, stability and credibility may be more important than tax incentives or interest rates."
- Claim: supported for the option value of waiting. The words "hurdle rate" are not in either abstract; the article text was not read.

**2. [A]** Bernanke, Ben S. (1983). "Irreversibility, uncertainty, and cyclical investment." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 98(1): 85-106. DOI 10.2307/1885568. (Last page from IDEAS.)
- Finding (IDEAS abstract; NBER WP 502 abstract via OpenAlex): agents "must make investment timing decisions that trade off the extra returns from early commitment against the benefits of increased information gained by waiting". WP abstract: "Uncertainty, because it increases the value of waiting for new information, retards the current rate of investment."
- Claim: supported.

**3. [A]** Bloom, Nicholas (2009). "The impact of uncertainty shocks." *Econometrica* 77(3): 623-685. DOI 10.3982/ECTA6248. (Crossref record has no author field; author from IDEAS and OpenAlex.)
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): a simulated macro uncertainty shock "produces a rapid drop and rebound in aggregate output and employment. This occurs because higher uncertainty causes firms to temporarily pause their investment and hiring." "Thus, uncertainty shocks generate short sharp recessions and recoveries."
- Claim: none attached.

**4. [A]** Baker, Scott R., Nicholas Bloom and Steven J. Davis (2016). "Measuring economic policy uncertainty." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 131(4): 1593-1636. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjw024.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "policy uncertainty is associated with greater stock price volatility and reduced investment and employment in policy-sensitive sectors like defense, health care, finance, and infrastructure construction. At the macro level, innovations in policy uncertainty foreshadow declines in investment, output, and employment in the United States and, in a panel vector autoregressive setting, for 12 major economies."
- Claim: supported. The abstract says "associated with" and "foreshadow".

**5. [A]** Rodrik, Dani (1991). "Policy uncertainty and private investment in developing countries." *Journal of Development Economics* 36(2): 229-242. DOI 10.1016/0304-3878(91)90034-S.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract, borrowed from NBER WP 2999): "rational behavior by the private sector calls for withholding investment until much of the residual uncertainty regarding the eventual success of the reform is eliminated. This paper shows that even moderate amounts of policy uncertainty can act as a hefty tax on investment, and that otherwise sensible reforms may prove damaging if they induce doubts as to their permanence."
- Claim: supported. The abstract's words are "even moderate amounts of policy uncertainty" and "a hefty tax"; the "small probability of reversal" wording was not checked in the text.

**6. [A]** Kydland, Finn E. and Edward C. Prescott (1977). "Rules rather than discretion: the inconsistency of optimal plans." *Journal of Political Economy* 85(3): 473-491. DOI 10.1086/260580.
- Finding (OpenAlex abstract): discretionary policy "does not result in the social objective function being maximized", because "economic planning is not a game against nature but, rather, a game against rational economic agents."
- Claim: citation confirmed.

**7. [A]** Noailly, Joëlle, Laura Nowzohour and Matthias van den Heuvel (2022). "Does environmental policy uncertainty hinder investments towards a low-carbon economy?" NBER Working Paper 30361, August 2022. DOI 10.3386/w30361. Crossref also returns Noailly, Nowzohour, van den Heuvel and Pla (2024), "Heard the news? Environmental policy and clean investments", *Journal of Public Economics* 238: 105190, DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2024.105190; whether it is the published version was not checked.
- Finding (OpenAlex abstract): a news-based index of US environmental and climate policy uncertainty (EnvPU), monthly, 1990-2019. "a rise in the EnvPU index is associated with a reduced probability for cleantech startups to receive venture capital (VC) funding"; "a rise in our EnvPU index is associated with higher stock volatility for firms with above-average green revenue shares"; at the macro level, shocks "lead to declines in the number of cleantech VC deals and higher volatility of the main benchmark clean energy exchange-traded fund."
- Claim: supported. The abstract gives no magnitudes.

**8. [A]** Berestycki, Clara, Stefano Carattini, Antoine Dechezleprêtre and Tobias Kruse (2022). "Measuring and assessing the effects of climate policy uncertainty." OECD Economics Department Working Papers No. 1724, 26 August 2022. OECD Publishing. DOI 10.1787/34483d83-en. (Authors and number from IDEAS; Crossref has no author field.)
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): the indicator covers "12 OECD Member Countries" over 1990-2018. "Climate Policy Uncertainty is associated with economically and statistically significant decreases in investment, particularly in pollution-intensive sectors that are most exposed to climate policies, and among capital-intensive companies."
- Claim: supported in direction. The number: NOT FOUND (the abstract gives none; oecd.org refused the full text).

**9. [A]** Gavriilidis, Konstantinos (2021). "Measuring climate policy uncertainty." SSRN working paper 3847388. DOI 10.2139/ssrn.3847388. Crossref also returns Gavriilidis, Känzig, Raghavan and Stock (2026), "The macroeconomic effects of climate policy uncertainty", NBER Working Paper 34762, DOI 10.3386/w34762.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): a news-based Climate Policy Uncertainty (CPU) index from major US newspapers; "climate policy uncertainty has a strong and negative effect on CO2 emissions."
- Claim: citation confirmed.

**10. [A]** Dorsey, Jackson (2019). "Waiting for the courts: effects of policy uncertainty on pollution and investment." *Environmental and Resource Economics* 74(4): 1453-1496. DOI 10.1007/s10640-019-00375-2.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): uses a legal challenge to the Clean Air Interstate Rule. "plants with a lower probability of being regulated invested in fewer capital-intensive pollution controls and reduced pollution by 13% less on average." "Policy uncertainty increased compliance costs by $124 million by delaying efficient investments."
- Claim: none attached.

**11. [A]** Fuss, Sabine, Jana Szolgayova, Michael Obersteiner and Mykola Gusti (2008). "Investment under market and climate policy uncertainty." *Applied Energy* 85(8): 708-721. DOI 10.1016/j.apenergy.2008.01.005.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): "the producer facing market uncertainty about CO2 prices invests into carbon-saving technology earlier than if the actual price path had been known on beforehand." "On the other hand, policy uncertainty induces the producer to wait and see whether the government will further commit to climate policy. This waiting is a real options effect."
- Claim: supported, and stronger than stated: market price volatility brings investment forward, while policy uncertainty postpones it.

**12. [A]** Lemoine, Derek (2017). "Green expectations: current effects of anticipated carbon pricing." *Review of Economics and Statistics* 99(3): 499-513. DOI 10.1162/REST_a_00627.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): "I report evidence that an anticipated strengthening of environmental policy increased emissions. I find that the breakdown of the U.S. Senate's 2010 climate effort generated positive excess returns in coal futures markets. This response appears to be driven by an increase in coal storage." The legislative process "may have increased emissions by over 12 million tons of carbon dioxide leading up to April 2010."
- Claim: the first half is supported (anticipated policy changes current energy markets). The direction is NOT supported as written: coal futures prices rose, not fell, when the cap-and-trade prospects collapsed. The anticipated policy had lowered current coal prices and raised current emissions.

## B. Long-run relative prices, capital and innovation

**13. [A]** Atkeson, Andrew and Patrick J. Kehoe (1999). "Models of energy use: putty-putty versus putty-clay." *American Economic Review* 89(4): 1028-1043. DOI 10.1257/aer.89.4.1028.
- Finding (OpenAlex abstract): "Energy use is inelastic in time-series data, but elastic in international cross-section data." "In the putty-clay model, capital and energy are complementary in the short run, but substitutable in the long run." A tax that doubles the energy price lowers long-run output by 33% in the putty-putty model and "only 5.3%" in the putty-clay model.
- Claim: supported.

**14. [A]** Hassler, John, Per Krusell and Conny Olovsson (2021). "Directed technical change as a response to natural resource scarcity." *Journal of Political Economy* 129(11): 3039-3072. DOI 10.1086/715849.
- Finding (OpenAlex abstract): "aggregate US data call for a very low short-run substitution elasticity between energy and the capital/labor inputs. Our estimates imply that energy-saving technical change took off when the oil shocks hit in the 1970s. This response implies significant substitutability with the other inputs in the long run".
- Claim: supported. The abstract says "very low", not "near zero".

**15. [A]** Popp, David (2002). "Induced innovation and energy prices." *American Economic Review* 92(1): 160-180. DOI 10.1257/000282802760015658.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): US patent data, 1970-1994. "both energy prices and the quality of existing knowledge have strongly significant positive effects on innovation."
- Claim: supported.

**16. [A]** Newell, Richard G., Adam B. Jaffe and Robert N. Stavins (1999). "The induced innovation hypothesis and energy-saving technological change." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 114(3): 941-975. DOI 10.1162/003355399556188.
- Finding (OpenAlex abstract): "(i) the rate of overall innovation was independent of energy prices and regulations; (ii) the direction of innovation was responsive to energy price changes for some products but not for others; (iii) energy price changes induced changes in the subset of technically feasible models that were offered for sale; (iv) this responsiveness increased substantially during the period after energy-efficiency product labeling was required; and (v) nonetheless, a sizable portion of efficiency improvements were autonomous."
- Claim: none attached.

**17. [A]** Aghion, Philippe, Antoine Dechezleprêtre, David Hémous, Ralf Martin and John Van Reenen (2016). "Carbon taxes, path dependency, and directed technical change: evidence from the auto industry." *Journal of Political Economy* 124(1): 1-51. DOI 10.1086/684581.
- Finding (published abstract via OpenAlex): "firms tend to innovate more in clean (and less in dirty) technologies when they face higher tax-inclusive fuel prices. Furthermore, there is path dependence in the type of innovation (clean/dirty) both from aggregate spillovers and from the firm's own innovation history."
- Numbers (WP version: CEP Discussion Paper 1178, updated September 2014): "The elasticity of 0.97 implies that a 10% higher fuel price is associated with about 10% more clean patents"; "a 10% increase in fuel prices is associated with about a 6% decrease in dirty innovation"; an increase "of 40% of fuel prices with respect to the 2005 fuel price will allow clean innovation stocks to overtake dirty stocks after fifteen years."
- Claim: supported.

**18. [A]** Anderson, Soren T., Ryan Kellogg and James M. Sallee (2013). "What do consumers believe about future gasoline prices?" *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 66(3): 383-403. DOI 10.1016/j.jeem.2013.07.002.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): two decades of the Michigan Survey of Consumers. "average consumer beliefs are typically indistinguishable from a no-change forecast, justifying an assumption commonly made in the literature on consumer valuation of energy efficiency."
- Claim: supported. The abstract does not say "real"; it also reports deviations in some circumstances and "significant cross-consumer forecast heterogeneity".

**19. [A]** Busse, Meghan R., Christopher R. Knittel and Florian Zettelmeyer (2013). "Are consumers myopic? Evidence from new and used car purchases." *American Economic Review* 103(1): 220-256. DOI 10.1257/aer.103.1.220.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "we calculate a range of implicit discount rates similar to the range of interest rates paid by car buyers who borrow. We interpret this as showing little evidence of consumer myopia."
- Claim: supported.

**20. [A]** Li, Shanjun, Joshua Linn and Erich Muehlegger (2014). "Gasoline taxes and consumer behavior." *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* 6(4): 302-342. DOI 10.1257/pol.6.4.302.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "We find robust evidence that consumers respond more strongly to gasoline tax changes under a variety of model specifications. We discuss two potential reasons for our main findings". WP version (SSRN abstract, 2012): "a 5-cent tax increase would reduce gasoline consumption by 0.86 percent, compared with 0.29 percent from an equivalent change in tax-inclusive gasoline prices."
- Claim: partly supported. The larger response to taxes is confirmed. Neither abstract names persistence as the reason or mentions vehicle choice (the WP abstract refers to "the intensive and extensive margins of gasoline consumption"); the article text was not read.

**21. [A]** Rivers, Nicholas and Brandon Schaufele (2015). "Salience of carbon taxes in the gasoline market." *Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 74: 23-36. DOI 10.1016/j.jeem.2015.07.002.
- Finding (published abstract via IDEAS): the British Columbia carbon tax "caused a decline in short-run gasoline demand that is significantly greater than would be expected from an equivalent increase in the market price of gasoline"; "during its first four years, the tax reduced carbon dioxide emissions from gasoline consumption by 2.4 million tonnes."
- Ratio: the published abstract gives none, and the published text was not read. The versions read give different ratios. Manuscript dated 22 October 2014 (Western University repository): "the BC carbon tax generated demand response that is 7.1 times larger than is attributable to an equivalent change in the carbon tax-exclusive price" (a five cent increase: 12.5% against 1.8%); its abstract says 3.6 million tonnes, not 2.4. University of Ottawa Working Paper 1211E, August 2012: "4.9 times greater" (10.6% against 2.2%).
- Claim: supported in direction ("several times"). The ratio for the published article is NOT FOUND.

**22. [A]** Andersson, Julius J. (2019). "Carbon taxes and CO2 emissions: Sweden as a case study." *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* 11(4): 1-30. DOI 10.1257/pol.20170144.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "carbon dioxide emissions from transport declined almost 11 percent, with the largest share due to the carbon tax alone, relative to a synthetic control unit constructed from a comparable group of OECD countries. Furthermore, the carbon tax elasticity of demand for gasoline is three times larger than the price elasticity."
- Claim: supported. The 11 percent is the effect of the carbon tax and VAT together.

**23. [A]** Labandeira, Xavier, José M. Labeaga and Xiral López-Otero (2017). "A meta-analysis on the price elasticity of energy demand." *Energy Policy* 102: 549-568. DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2017.01.002.
- Finding (published abstract via IDEAS): a meta-analysis of short and long-term elasticities for energy, electricity, natural gas, gasoline, diesel and heating oil. The published abstract gives no numbers.
- Numbers (WP version: EUI RSCAS 2016/25): sample of 951 short-term and 991 long-term estimates, with raw averages "-0.237" (short term) and "-0.598" (long term). Meta-analysis result: "the short-term price elasticity of energy demand is, on average, -0.22"; conclusions: "-0.22, and of -0.65 in the long term". Short-term elasticities of energy products "range between -0.26 and -0.20".
- Claim: supported for the short run (about -0.2). The long-run figure in the working paper is -0.60 to -0.65 for energy, slightly above the "-0.5 to -0.6" stated. Exact averages in the published article: NOT FOUND.

## C. Long-term contracts

**24. [A]** Fabra, Natalia (2021). "The energy transition: an industrial economics perspective." *International Journal of Industrial Organization* 79: 102734. DOI 10.1016/j.ijindorg.2021.102734.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract; postprint text, Universidad Carlos III repository): a survey focused on "the regulatory and market design challenges triggered by the expansion of intermittent renewables with almost zero marginal costs." Text: "renewable investors are now called to compete to access the market through auctions of long-term, legally enforceable, contracts that reduce electricity market price risk." "the possibility of entering into long-term contracts prior to carrying out the investment should reduce investors' risk-premia, facilitate access to credit, and promote entry, ultimately benefiting consumers through more vigorous competition and lower prices."
- Claim: supported. The paper says "should reduce investors' risk-premia"; it does not estimate the effect.

**25. [A]** Fabra, Natalia and Juan-Pablo Montero (2023). "Technology-neutral versus technology-specific procurement." *Economic Journal* 133(650): 669-705. DOI 10.1093/ej/ueac075.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "one size does not fit all: the preferred instrument depends on the costs of the available technologies, their degree of substitutability, the extent of information asymmetry and the costs of public funds."
- Claim: citation confirmed.

**26. [A]** Neuhoff, Karsten, Nils May and Jörn C. Richstein (2022). "Financing renewables in the age of falling technology costs." *Resource and Energy Economics* 70: 101330. DOI 10.1016/j.reseneeco.2022.101330.
- Finding (published abstract via IDEAS and OpenAlex): "Contracts for differences provide in all scenarios the most effective hedge for investors against power prices uncertainty, enabling low-cost financing and reducing costs for consumers, while also hedging electricity consumers against high power prices." Sliding premia "provide for less risks hedging, when technology costs approach wholesale power prices."
- Numbers (WP version: DIW Discussion Paper 1746, 2018, titled "Renewable Energy Policy in the Age of Falling Technology Costs"; Germany, 2030 scenario): "consumers can expect to save around 0.8 billion Euro per year by 2030 on new renewables, if they are backed by contracts for difference rather than a sliding premium. Fixed premia render the same investments 2.7 billion Euro more expensive than contracts for differences." Without support, "the annual costs lie around 3.4 billion Euro higher". The paper cites a financing cost premium for full power price exposure "of 0.8 – 1.7 percentage points" (NERA 2013) and "around 1.2 percentage points" (May and Neuhoff 2017).
- Claim: supported. Numbers in the published article: NOT FOUND (published text not read).

## D. Macroeconomic effects

**27. [A]** Pisani-Ferry, Jean (2021). "Climate policy is macroeconomic policy, and the implications will be significant." Policy Brief 21-20, August 2021. Peterson Institute for International Economics. https://www.piie.com/sites/default/files/documents/pb21-20.pdf
- Finding (PDF read): "Decarbonization can thus be regarded as an adverse supply shock—very much like the oil shocks of the 1970s." Investment: "the net increase should therefore amount to at least 2 percentage points of GDP in 2030" (from IEA 2021); the IMF (2021) "also concludes that annual additional investment should amount to 2 percent of GDP between 2030 and 2040." "Public finances will also be affected." Section 4 is headed "The net zero transition will likely push public finances further into debt".
- Claim: supported.

**28. [A]** Pisani-Ferry, Jean and Selma Mahfouz (2023). *The Economic Implications of Climate Action*. Report commissioned by the French Prime Minister. France Stratégie, published 22 May 2023. https://www.strategie-plan.gouv.fr/en/publications/economic-implications-climate-action
- Finding (publisher's page with the key messages read; full report not opened): "Decarbonisation will require significant additional investment in the next decade (more than 2 percentage points of GDP in 2030, or €70 billion, in comparison to a scenario without climate action)." "the transition away from fossil fuels will likely result in a temporary slowdown in productivity, estimated at one quarter of a percentage point per year." "the risk to public debt is approximately 10 percentage points of GDP in 2030, 15 percentage points in 2035 and 25 percentage points in 2040, assuming that the decline in energy-related revenue is offset".
- Claim: supported on all three points.

**29. [A]** International Monetary Fund (2022). "Near-term macroeconomic impact of decarbonization policies." Chapter 3 in *World Economic Outlook, October 2022: Countering the Cost-of-Living Crisis*. International Monetary Fund, published 11 October 2022. DOI 10.5089/9798400218439.081.ch003. Pages: not found.
- Finding (IMF-hosted text rendering of the chapter, read through the page-fetch tool; wording as returned by the tool, not checked against the PDF): "Global growth could be lower by 0.15 to 0.25 percentage point annually as a result of the transition." "Inflation could be 0.1 to 0.4 percentage point higher globally." Delay "Produces much larger economic costs in terms of output loss and inflation." Report page: "Decades of procrastination have transformed what could have been a smooth transition into a more challenging one."
- Claim: supported on the numbers and on delay. The horizon "to 2030" was not confirmed in the text read.

**30. [A]** International Monetary Fund (2023). *Fiscal Monitor, October 2023: Climate Crossroads: Fiscal Policies in a Warming World*. International Monetary Fund. DOI 10.5089/9798400250286.089 (Chapter 1: DOI 10.5089/9798400250286.089.ch001).
- Finding (Crossref abstract; executive summary through the page-fetch tool): relying mostly on spending-based measures "could raise public debt by 45−50 percent of GDP" for a representative large-emitting country by midcentury. Under the indicative policy mix for advanced economies, "Public debt would rise by 10−15 percent of GDP by 2050." Crossref abstract: "Carbon pricing is a necessary instrument and should be part of the policy mix. However, it is not sufficient and should be complemented by policies to address market failures and catalyze private financing and investment in low-carbon technologies."
- Claim: supported. The 45-50 percent is for "a representative large-emitting country".

**31. [A]** Metcalf, Gilbert E. and James H. Stock (2023). "The macroeconomic impact of Europe's carbon taxes." *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 15(3): 265-286. DOI 10.1257/mac.20210052.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "We find no evidence for a negative impact on employment or GDP growth but rather find a zero to modest positive impact. We also find a cumulative emissions reduction on the order of 4 to 6 percent for a $40/ton CO2 tax covering 30 percent of emissions."
- Claim: supported.

**32. [A]** Känzig, Diego R. (2023). "The unequal economic consequences of carbon pricing." NBER Working Paper 31221, May 2023. DOI 10.3386/w31221. Now published: *American Economic Review* 116(11): 4003-4040, November 2026. DOI 10.1257/aer.20230448.
- Finding (Crossref abstract, same in both versions): "A restrictive carbon policy shock raises energy prices, reduces emissions, spurs green innovation, but decreases economic activity—disproportionately burdening poorer households. Not only are the poor more affected because of their higher energy spending, but they also experience larger income losses." Indirect effects through income and employment account "for about two-thirds of the aggregate consumption response."
- Claim: supported, except that the abstract names energy prices, not inflation.

**33. [A]** Konradt, Maximilian and Beatrice Weder di Mauro (2023). "Carbon taxation and greenflation: evidence from Europe and Canada." *Journal of the European Economic Association* 21(6): 2518-2546. DOI 10.1093/jeea/jvad020.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): "carbon taxes did not significantly increase inflation, with dynamic effects estimated around zero in most specifications. Instead we find support for relative price changes, increasing the cost of energy but leaving the price of other goods and services unaffected."
- Claim: supported.

**34. [A]** Schnabel, Isabel (2022). "A new age of energy inflation: climateflation, fossilflation and greenflation." Speech at a panel on "Monetary Policy and Climate Change", The ECB and its Watchers XXII Conference, Frankfurt am Main, 17 March 2022. European Central Bank. https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/key/date/2022/html/ecb.sp220317_2~dbb3582f0a.en.html. And Schnabel, Isabel (2023). "Monetary policy tightening and the green transition." Speech at the International Symposium on Central Bank Independence, Sveriges Riksbank, Stockholm, 10 January 2023. European Central Bank. https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/key/date/2023/html/ecb.sp230110~21c89bef1b.en.html
- Finding (both speech pages read). 2023 speech: "Since fossil fuel-based power plants have comparably low upfront costs, a persistent rise in the cost of capital may discourage efforts to decarbonise our economies rapidly. Put simply, renewable energies are more competitive when interest rates are low. While simulations suggest that the LCOE of a gas-fired power plant would change only marginally if discount rates were to double, that of offshore wind could rise by nearly 45%". 2022 speech: "After all, interest rates directly affect the cost of capital and hence the incentive to invest in greener technologies."
- Claim: supported by the 2023 speech. The 2022 speech makes only the general point.

**35. [A]** Monnin, Pierre (2015). "The impact of interest rates on electricity production costs." CEP Discussion Note 2015/3, June 2015. Council on Economic Policies. https://www.cepweb.org/wp-content/uploads/CEP_DN_Interest_Rates_Energy_Prices.pdf
- Finding (PDF read): "low interest rates increase the competitiveness of green energy technologies versus brown energy technologies". "the average costs of producing electricity are higher for green energy technologies for interest rate levels above 2%"; "with interest rates at 2% the cost ratio of green energy technologies compared to brown energy technologies is 1.0 (i.e. reflecting cost parity) and drops further as interest rates go lower." "Wind on-shore is competitive with all other technologies up until interest rates of about 8%." The LCOEs of green technologies "have a higher interest rate elasticity than those for brown energy technologies."
- Claim: supported.

**36. [A]** Presbitero, Andrea F. (2016). "Too much and too fast? Public investment scaling-up and absorptive capacity." *Journal of Development Economics* 120: 17-31. DOI 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2015.12.005.
- Finding (OpenAlex abstract): World Bank investment projects approved 1970-2007 in 80 countries. "projects undertaken in periods of public investment scaling-up are less likely to be successful, although this effect is relatively small, especially in poor and capital scarce countries."
- Claim: supported, with the paper's qualifier that the effect "is relatively small".

**37. [A]** Office for Budget Responsibility (2021). *Fiscal risks report – July 2021*. OBR, 6 July 2021. https://obr.uk/frs/fiscal-risks-report-july-2021/
- Finding (PDF read): "The fiscal impact of achieving net zero in the early action scenario adds 21 per cent of GDP to public sector net debt in 2050-51 (£469 billion in today's terms)." "receipts worth 1.6 per cent of GDP are lost in 2050-51, with fuel duty accounting for 76 per cent and VED for 18 per cent." "If lost receipts from fuel duty and VED were replaced by an equivalent yielding levy on motoring (of whatever form), receipts in 2050-51 would be 1.5 per cent of GDP higher and PSND would be 24 per cent of GDP lower than in the early action scenario".
- Claim: supported. The figure is for the early action scenario; the net public spending cost averages "just 0.4 per cent of GDP a year".

## E. Finance, de-risking and its critics

**38. [A]** Gabor, Daniela (2021). "The Wall Street Consensus." *Development and Change* 52(3): 429-459. DOI 10.1111/dech.12645.
- Finding (Crossref abstract): the "de-risking state" creates "a safety net for investors in development assets, protecting their profits from demand risks", "political risks" and "liquidity and currency risks. These risks are transferred to the balance sheet of the state. The new 'development as de-risking' paradigm narrows the scope for a green developmental state that could design a just transition to low-carbon economies."
- Claim: supported. The term "contingent liabilities" is not in the abstract.

**39. [A]** Cordella, Tito and Andrew Powell (2021). "Preferred and non-preferred creditors." *Journal of International Economics* 132: 103491. DOI 10.1016/j.jinteco.2021.103491.
- Finding (IDEAS abstract): "a creditor, such as an IFI, that can commit to lend limited amounts at the risk-free rate and can refrain from lending into arrears is always repaid and adds value." "To maintain their status, preferred lenders should offer low cost financing in volumes that are consistent with countries' incentives to repay even in bad states. This suggests such lenders should not differentiate lending interest rates according to risk and should not participate in the restructuring of commercial debt."
- Claim: supported.

**40. [A]** Schmidt, Tobias S. (2014). "Low-carbon investment risks and de-risking." *Nature Climate Change* 4(4): 237-239. DOI 10.1038/nclimate2112.
- Finding (standfirst on the Nature page; a commentary with no abstract): "especially in developing countries, low-carbon investments often suffer from high risks. More research is needed to address these risks and allow sound policy decisions to be made."
- Claim: none attached.

**41. [A]** Egli, Florian, Bjarne Steffen and Tobias S. Schmidt (2018). "A dynamic analysis of financing conditions for renewable energy technologies." *Nature Energy* 3(12): 1084-1092. DOI 10.1038/s41560-018-0277-y.
- Finding (abstract on the Nature page): 133 utility-scale photovoltaic and onshore wind projects in Germany over 18 years. The general interest rate and experience effects in the finance industry "contribute 5% (photovoltaic) and 24% (wind) to the observed reductions in levelized costs of electricity (LCOEs)."
