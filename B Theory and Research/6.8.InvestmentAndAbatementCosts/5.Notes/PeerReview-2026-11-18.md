# Peer review of 6.8, methodology note v0.1 (18 Nov 2026)

A referee-style review of "Pricing the Measures: Investment Needs, Abatement Costs and Public Money in CPAT" (Theory and Research Note No. 6.8, v0.1, 18 Nov 2026, about 13,000 words with tables, drafted with an agent and not reviewed before), written from a full reading and before any change to the master. Every equation was rederived by hand. `2.Source/worked_examples.py` was rerun (output identical to `worked_examples_output.txt`), and its numbers were recomputed under alternative inputs in a scratch script. The SISEPUEDE unit cost factors were checked against the public cost-benefit documentation (the seven sector pages of costs-benefits-ssp.readthedocs.io, read 18 Nov 2026) and against the factor database in the public repository `milocortes/costs_benefits_ssp` (commit adc22ba, the commit the note cites: `tmp_cb_data.db`, tables `transformation_costs`, `cost_factors`, `LVST_enteric_fermentation_tx`, `strategy_interactions`; `cb_calculate.py`). CPAT's hooks were checked against paper 5.1 and the public CPAT-AI repository (`cpmodelai/cpat_ai`, commit cda413a: `excel_ai/mitigation/docs/Equations.md` E53, E69; `Equations_addendum.md` R5). Consistency was checked against papers 6.3 and 6.5 and the house norms (`NORMS.md`, `paper-standard.md`, `ai-tells.md`).

## Summary judgement

The note answers the right brief and gets the architecture right: one accounting identity (physical change times unit factor), capital separated from running cost and annualized once, a split by payer, abatement costs at stated rates and prices, measures entering CPAT where CPAT already has hooks, and power measures acting inside the allocation rather than on top of it. Most algebra is correct: equations (1)–(5), the CRF, the de-levelization, the subsidy cost per tonne, the grant-to-subsidy annuity, Proposition 3's algebra and the $8.76\,CF$ conversion from dollars per kilowatt to dollars per megawatt-hour all check. The worked examples reproduce exactly from the code.

It is not yet usable as a specification, for six reasons. (i) Two core formulas double count: the viability gap and the "price first" test subtract fuel taxes already inside the private abatement cost, and the non-energy subsidy ledger pays inframarginal adopters twice. (ii) Proposition 1 does not nest paper 6.3's rule as stated: 6.3's $W$ includes co-benefits, while $\mathrm{MAC}^\lambda$ is built on the resource cost. (iii) The headline electric-car example mixes a United States purchase premium with Latin American maintenance savings; on the note's own Latin American factor the resource cost is about −150, not +130, dollars per tonne, and the story changes. (iv) The cattle example misstates the share of emissions left to pay the fee (84–100 percent, not "64 to 100"). (v) The power plug-in contradicts paper 5.1, which keeps the scale-up caps, and inherits an active-set error from 5.1 that gives shares summing to less than one; the target and grid terms need restating. (vi) The CPAT efficiency hook is coarser than assumed (one $\alpha^+$ per scenario for three sector groups, also applied to electricity demand), and several SISEPUEDE factors are mis-signed or mislabelled, including one unflagged threefold documentation–database gap. There is also a confidentiality problem, mostly in the README: it names the internal tool name it says it is holding back, and it names the private repositories and their paths.

Of the six deliverables, (3) and (4) are met; (1), (2), (5) and (6) are met in structure but need the corrections below. "Simple" is not met: the note runs to about 13,000 words, carries four abatement costs plus a snapshot and a leave-one-out variant, and reuses at least twelve symbols for different things.

## Major points

**M1. The viability gap and the "price first" flag count fuel taxes twice.** @tbl-macs defines $\mathrm{MAC}^{priv}$ at "retail price incl. taxes and subsidies", so fuel taxes already lower it through larger private fuel savings. @eq-gap then subtracts $\tau^e_m$, "the explicit carbon price plus fuel taxes net of subsidies". The fuel tax is counted twice, and the gap is understated wherever fuel is taxed. The same error appears in the "price first" test ($\mathrm{MAC}^{priv}_m < \tau^e_m$, Section 6). It also appears in @eq-lcoe if $p^{fuel}$ is a retail price, because $\tau^e_t e_i$ then adds the fuel taxes again. The claim that "each dollar of effective carbon price cuts the gap by a dollar per discounted tonne" holds only if every abated tonne carries the same charge. For fuel switching to electricity, it holds only net of the charge on the added electricity. *Fix:* use one price basis. Either compute the private cost at retail prices that already include every tax and the carbon price, and set
$$G_m = \max\!\big(0,\ \mathrm{MAC}^{priv}_m\big)\sum_t \delta_t(r^{priv})\,\Delta E_{m,t} \;=\; \max(0,\,-NPV^{priv}_m),$$
which is paper 6.3's "first correction" exactly, with the flag "price first" if $\mathrm{MAC}^{priv}_m \le 0$. Or compute $\mathrm{MAC}^{priv,0}_m$ at retail prices *excluding* carbon-related charges and subtract $\tau^e_m$, stating that $\tau^e$ then excludes taxes already in those prices. In @eq-lcoe write $p^{fuel}$ at the price before carbon charges and $\tau^{c}_t e_i$ with the explicit carbon price only; rename $e_i$ (see M11). Change "each dollar … cuts the gap by a dollar" to "by the charge on the tonnes the measure abates, net of the charge on any fuel it adds".

**M2. Proposition 1 nests paper 6.3's rule only if co-benefits are left out, and it needs $\sum\delta\Delta E > 0$.** In paper 6.3, $W$ is "benefits to everyone other than the budget, net of their costs", and lens (c) includes co-benefits $CB$. Proposition 1 instead defines $W_m$ so that $W_m - F_m = -\sum_t\delta_t NC_{m,t}$, which has no co-benefits. As stated, then, $\mathrm{MAC}^\lambda < P^C$ is equivalent to a funding rule that ignores co-benefits, not to 6.3's. The proof also divides by discounted tonnes, which reverses the inequality for a measure that raises emissions. *Fix:* define the public-money cost on the social cost,
$$\mathrm{MAC}^{\lambda}_m = \mathrm{MAC}^{soc}_m + (\lambda-1)\frac{F_m}{\sum_t\delta_t\Delta E_{m,t}},$$
and restate the proposition as follows. *Let $W_m - F_m = -\sum_t\delta_t NC_{m,t} + \sum_t\delta_t CB_{m,t}$ at resource prices and the social rate, and $\sum_t\delta_t\Delta E_{m,t} > 0$. Then $NB_\lambda = W_m - \lambda F_m + P^C\sum_t\delta_t\Delta E_{m,t} > 0$ if and only if $\mathrm{MAC}^\lambda_m < P^C$.* Change the "Public money" row of @tbl-macs to "Social cost plus $(\lambda-1)$ times the budget's cost". Update @eq-subsidy and the electric-car example to match (CPAT supplies the air pollution, crash and congestion benefits under Rule 3). Keep a resource-cost variant only as a sensitivity.

**M3. The electric-car example mixes a United States premium with Latin American factors and ignores electricity taxes; its headline numbers do not survive.** The documentation says the 12,000-dollar premium and 1,000-dollar charger are US figures, and that the 0.039 dollars per vehicle-kilometer factor is the result *after* adjustment "to 2019 costs for LAC": 13,000/(15,000 × 12) = 0.072, so the LAC scaling is about 0.54, the same ratio as the transmission factor (US 5 to LAC 2.70 dollars per megawatt-hour). The card (@tbl-sec-transport) says this ("before LAC scaling"). Yet the example uses 13,000 dollars with the LAC maintenance saving of 0.012 dollars per kilometer, and Section 2.3 says the 0.039 factor comes "from a 12,000-dollar premium plus 1,000 for charging". On the LAC basis (de-levelized with @eq-delev at $r_0 = 0$: 7,020 dollars, 3,358 in 2030), with everything else unchanged, the results recomputed are:

| | Note (US premium) | LAC premium |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Resource cost, 6% (USD/t) | 130 | −153 |
| Private cost, 12% retail (USD/t) | −7 | −390 |
| Budget, subsidy only (USD/t) | 369 | 199 |
| Budget incl. lost gasoline excise (USD/t) | 836 | 666 |
| Same, net of electricity tax gained (USD/t) | 724 | 554 |
| Public-money cost, λ = 1.2 (USD/t) | 297 | −20 |

The excise term is also one-sided. It treats the whole gasoline retail–supply gap (0.50 dollars per liter) as tax, which is acceptable as an upper bound, but it ignores the electricity retail–supply gap (0.05 dollars per kilowatt-hour) on 2,700 kilowatt-hours a year. On the note's own logic that gap is tax gained, and counting it cuts 112 dollars per tonne. The private rate (12 percent) also differs from the parameter table's default for developing countries (10.5 percent). *Fix:* pick one basis and say which. Either use the LAC factor (the note's own convention), which makes the lesson "price first, and a subsidy is mostly a transfer", or keep a US-cost case labelled as a stress case with the US maintenance saving (949 dollars a year). Net the electricity tax in `lost_excise_pv`. Use the default private rate or explain the 12 percent. Then update the abstract ("about 840 … about 130"), the executive summary and Section 12. Section 2.3 should read: "a 12,000-dollar US premium plus 1,000 for charging, over 15,000 kilometers a year and 12 years, with no interest, scaled to Latin American prices".

**M4. The non-energy fiscal flows double count, and the cattle example misstates its own result.** (a) @eq-nonenergy-fiscal pays the subsidy on $(u_{m,t} + u^0_{m,t})$. But $u_{m,t}$ from @eq-uptake is *total* uptake at reward $\pi_m$, which already includes those who would have adopted anyway. The payment base is $u_{m,t}$, not $u + u^0$. And $u^0$ is not a free parameter: in this deterministic rule it is the uptake at $\pi = \tau_g$ alone. *Fix:* $SUB_{g,t} = \sum_m b_{m,t}\, r_m\,\omega_{m,g}\,E^{CPAT}_{g,t}\,u_{m,t}$, with inframarginal uptake $u^0_{m,t} = u_{m,t}(\pi = \tau_{g,t})$ reported as the share of payments that buys nothing. Use $E^{CPAT}$ consistently: @eq-potential and @eq-nonenergy-fiscal use $E^B$, but @eq-nonenergy uses $E^{CPAT}$. (b) The ramp and the cap disagree. "Fully adopted at 150" holds only if the maximum uptake $\bar u_m$ equals 1. With $\bar u = 0.8$, the cap binds at $c + 0.8\kappa$, and the code clips at 1.0, not 0.8. *Fix:* $u_{m,t} = \bar u_m \min\{1, \max[0, (\pi_{m,t} - c_m)/\kappa_m]\}$, so that $\kappa_m$ is the width of the whole ramp. Say also that $c_m$ is then the cost of the *cheapest* adopter. If $c_m$ is SISEPUEDE's average cost, the ramp should be centred on it, $\max[0, (\pi - c_m)/\kappa_m + 1/2]$. Proposition 2's objective changes accordingly. (c) "The fee raises revenue on the 64 to 100 percent of emissions that remain" confuses untreated heads with emissions. Each treated head cuts its methane by 25 percent, so remaining emissions are $1 - 0.25u$: 100 percent at a 75-dollar fee, 91 percent at 120 dollars and 83.5 percent at 75 + 60. With the $\bar u$ fix (u = 0.53 at 135 dollars), the last figure is 87 percent. (d) The example contradicts Section 7.3's own point that a per-head fee does not reward feed additives. The 75-dollar fee must be on *estimated emissions with a credited abatement technology*; say so or drop the fee leg. (e) "Paying per tonne verified … is what keeps $u^0$ small" is wrong: a payment per tonne still pays inframarginal adopters. What limits that leakage is a baseline or additionality test, or a reverse auction. Re-word.

**M5. The PPP switch builds in a bias toward partnerships in weak-PIM countries and books P2 assets wrongly.** (a) Proposition 3 is algebraically right, but it applies the public investment efficiency haircut to the public route only. At the note's own defaults (6 percent, a 3-point premium, 20 years) the break-even saving is $\eta^* = 1 - \mathrm{CRF}(0.06,20)/(s_c\,\mathrm{CRF}(0.09,20))$. That gives −33 percent for low-income countries ($s_c = 0.60$), −9 percent for emerging markets (0.73) and +8.5 percent for advanced economies (0.87), recomputed. So the interface will show a partnership winning in every low-income and emerging country without building any more cheaply. The IMF's efficiency gap measures weaknesses in appraisal, selection and management that partnerships share: PIMA assesses PPP management too. *Fix:* give the partnership route its own efficiency $s^{PPP}_c$, defaulting to $s_c$, so that
$$\eta^* = 1 - \frac{s^{PPP}_c\,\mathrm{CRF}(i_g, n_c)}{s_c\,\mathrm{CRF}(i_g+\rho, n_c)},$$
which reduces to the financing-premium test (20.4 percent at the defaults) unless the user argues a difference. Report $\eta^*$ for both settings. (b) "Any K item of type P1 or P2 can be switched". But a P2 asset is repaid from tariffs: switching it to a contract moves it to P3, with payments by the utility and a contingent liability for the budget, not an availability payment $AP$ in @eq-ledger. *Fix:* P1 → availability payment from the budget; P2 → P3 contract with $L^{PPP}$ recorded as contingent. (c) "A shorter contract raises the annual payment without changing its present value at $w$" is true but beside the point for the budget. At $i_g$ the premium rises with contract length: $\eta^*$ is 12.8, 17.0, 20.4 and 23.2 percent at 10, 15, 20 and 25 years, recomputed. Say so: contract length is a cost lever, not only a timing one. (d) "The capital outlay still drives CPAT's output through the investment multiplier … whoever finances it" contradicts Rule 2, under which CPAT's multipliers apply only to uses of recycled revenue. Privately financed capex (P3–P5, PPP) has no multiplier in CPAT today. *Fix:* apply the public investment multiplier to publicly financed capex only, and state that private capex has no output channel in CPAT (a limit).

**M6. The power plug-in departs from paper 5.1 without saying so, and four of its formulas need restating.** (a) *Caps.* Paper 5.1 says its proposal "would leave dispatch, retirement and the scale-up limits as they are". The note says the soft rule "replaces the logit and the caps", and calibrates $\lambda_a$ to reproduce the caps. Either keep the caps, as 5.1 does, or argue the replacement here and ask the coordinator to change 5.1. A cap limits capacity growth as a share of total effective capacity, while $\lambda_a$ penalizes the change in *investment* shares. They are different objects, so the calibration needs a conversion: share of new investment × need ÷ total effective capacity. (b) *Active set.* @eq-softsol (5.1's eq-combined) is the interior solution only when $\sum_{i\in A} s_{i,t-1} = 1$. If a technology that had a positive share last year drops out, recomputing "over the active set" with $\lambda_v/N$ gives shares that do not sum to one. For example, take 5.1's costs at 75 dollars per tonne (45, 55, 90, 136.2, 172.5), last year's velocity shares at zero carbon price, and $\lambda_v = \lambda_a = 100$. The note's procedure gives 43.9, 33.9 and 13.9 percent, summing to 91.8 percent; the exact minimizer is 46.7, 36.7 and 16.7 percent (recomputed). *Fix:* with $S_A = \sum_{j\in A}s_{j,t-1}$ and $N_A = |A|$,
$$s_{i,t} = \frac{\lambda_a s_{i,t-1} + \big[\lambda_v + \lambda_a(1 - S_A)\big]/N_A + \bar C^e_{A,t} - C^e_{i,t}}{\lambda_v+\lambda_a}, \quad i \in A.$$
**Coordinator:** paper 5.1's Appendix B ("Negative shares are handled by the same active-set procedure") has the same error for the acceleration and combined rules. (c) *Response to a cost change.* "The shares respond by $\Delta C_i/(\lambda_v+\lambda_a)$" omits the mean term: $\partial s_i/\partial C_i = -(1 - 1/N_A)/(\lambda_v+\lambda_a)$, and every other active share rises by $1/[N_A(\lambda_v+\lambda_a)]$. (d) *Targets.* $s_{i,t}$ is a share of *new investment*, but renewable targets and coal phase-outs are shares of generation or capacity. The quadratic penalty is also symmetric, so it punishes overshooting a renewable floor. *Fix:* translate the target into an investment-share path $\bar s_{i,t}$ (state the formula) and use a one-sided penalty, $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\max(0, \bar s_{i,t} - s_{i,t})^2$ for a floor and $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\max(0, s_{i,t} - \bar s_{i,t})^2$ for a ceiling. The implied subsidy is then $\mu\max(0, \bar s - s)$, and as $\mu \to \infty$ it converges to the multiplier of the hard constraint.

**M6 (continued). The grid, public-build and cost terms of the plug-in.** (e) *Grid.* @eq-grid uses $c^{int}(v_t)$ and $G^{req}_t = g I^{VRE}_t$, both of which depend on this year's allocation: a circular reference. Paper 5.1 evaluates system costs at last year's shares. *Fix:* use $v_{t-1}$ and $I^{VRE}_{t-1}$. More seriously, the term moves integration cost from investors to the budget but must not remove it from the resource cost. The power abatement cost ("change in annualized system cost") must include the public grid and storage capital $G_t$, or the measure looks free. And $g = 0.6$ is grid *and* storage dollars per dollar of renewables, while $c^{int}$ is CPAT's *storage* charge. If storage is in $C^{sys}$ and also in $g I^{VRE}$, storage capital is counted twice in the investment need. *Fix:* report $I^{pow} = \sum_i N_{i,t}k_{i,t} + G^{req}_t$ with $k$ excluding storage, and say that $C^{sys}$ serves the allocation only. (f) *Public build* (@eq-publicbuild): add $\max(0,\cdot)$ to $Need^{alloc}$. Say that next year's $s_{i,t-1}$ is the realized share including public builds, $s^{obs}_{i,t} = 8.76\,CF_i N_{i,t}/Need_t$; otherwise the adjustment penalty pulls the mix back toward what the public build displaced. State units (Need in gigawatt-hours, $B$ in megawatts). Source "This is how CPAT already treats exogenous hydro and nuclear plans" from CPAT-AI or mark it [VERIFY]. (g) @eq-lcoe is a static levelized cost at this year's fuel price. CPAT's investment cost $tic$ is forward-looking (discounted future variable cost with foresight of the carbon price) and includes transmission and storage (paper 5.1). State that the plug-in changes the capital, subsidy and rate terms *inside* $tic$ and does not replace it. Give fuel price units ($p^{fuel}$ in dollars per megawatt-hour thermal, or $3.6\,p$ for dollars per gigajoule). In @eq-route, average capital charges, not rates, $\sum_P\pi_{i,P}\mathrm{CRF}(r_P, n_i)$, because the CRF is nonlinear in $r$. Define $r_{P4}$, which is missing.

**M7. CPAT's efficiency hook is coarser than the note assumes, and the energy hooks need three precisions.** (a) In the public CPAT-AI equations, $\alpha^+$ is one value per scenario for each of three legacy groups: road vehicles (with rail, aviation and navigation), residential, and "industrial", which covers every other subsector (`Equations.md` E69). The same parameter enters power demand (E53). So $\alpha^+_{s,t}$ by sector and year, as @eq-alpha needs, does not exist. A residential gas retrofit entered through $\alpha^+$ would also cut residential electricity, LPG and kerosene. *Fix:* say that @eq-alpha needs a code change to $\alpha^+_{sf,t}$, and list it under "built first". Or implement efficiency measures as fuel-specific level scalars, like @eq-switch, with CPAT's rebound applied explicitly through the factor $(1-\psi)^{1+\epsilon_U}$. (b) @eq-alpha is exact only if $\alpha = 0$, because $\alpha$ and $\alpha^+$ add in one denominator. The exact form is $\alpha^+_t = (1+\alpha)\big[(1-\psi_{t-1})/(1-\psi_t) - 1\big]$. The error is small (a 0.05 percent difference at $\alpha = 1$ percent and $\psi = 5$ percent), but the text claims exactness. (c) @eq-switch must say that @eq-cpat runs on the unswitched path, with $\tilde F_{sf,t-1}$ and not $F^S_{sf,t-1}$ on the right-hand side; otherwise each year's switch compounds on the last. Rule 1 says combined effects multiply "as in @eq-switch", but @eq-switch is additive ($\sum_m\phi_m \le 1$). Re-word. (d) The derivation of @eq-shsub is right, but its caveat is wrong. Under any constant-returns technology the cost-minimizing input mix depends only on the price ratio $p/q$, so lowering $q$ by a share $\sigma$ is exactly equivalent to raising $p$ by the factor $1/(1-\sigma)$, whatever the elasticity of substitution. The real approximations are three: the subsidy reaches new purchases, not the stock; it reaches only eligible equipment; and CPAT's $\epsilon_F$ is a reduced-form elasticity, not a structural one. Re-word. And the worked example computes an efficiency-margin shadow price for an *electric-car* subsidy, which by the note's own taxonomy is a fuel switch (the vehicle-choice logit or @eq-switch). Drop that line or use an efficiency example.

**M8. The SISEPUEDE cards contain sign, label and unit errors, and one large documentation–database gap goes unflagged.** Checked against the documentation pages and the factor database at commit adc22ba:

It applies the factor to *total* building energy (`energy_consumption_scoe_total`), not to heat switched. The industrial "all" factor is 649,000 dollars per petajoule (2.34 dollars per megawatt-hour) against the documented 15 for high-temperature heat. Bracket both and correct the drivers in @tbl-sec-buildings.
- *Parity.* "Reaching parity in 10 years" does not follow from the database's annual change of 0.887: 0.887¹⁰ = 0.30. Say that @eq-cost applies a geometric decline with no floor. The electric-car premium then falls to 6 percent of its 2023 value by 2050.
- *Healthier diets.* The 385 dollars per person is a saving: the documentation says the better diet costs less, and the database row is `consumer_savings`. The card lists it as a "grocery cost".
- *Ending open dumping.* The 115 dollars per tonne is a non-technical *benefit* in the documentation (reduced environmental and health impacts). The card lists it as a K "transition" cost.
- *Biogas.* "[500/t]" is a unit conversion of 17 dollars per million Btu (17/1.05 × 30.58 ≈ 495), not a disagreement. The "10.30 capital, 4.30 operating" are the IEA's global source values before conversion; they sum to 14.6, not 17.
- *Count and notation.* By the note's own brackets there are eight documentation–database differences, not seven. Removing biogas and adding the two heat factors gives nine. Brackets also mark "[assumption]", which overloads them. Use a separate "Database" column.
- *Units and sources.* The rice factor is stored per acre ("$/acre", −31) although labelled per hectare. Flag it. The 10 percent annual decline of electric-vehicle capital is in the database only: the documentation says "declining over time". Say so in the card.
- *Incremental factors.* Most factors are premiums over the baseline technology, so they give incremental investment, not total investment. "Total investment $I^S_{m,t}$ compares with global studies" cannot be delivered from them without full unit costs. Say so, or report incremental investment only outside power.
- *Undocumented levelization.* Because building and industrial efficiency and heat factors are treated as running-cost equivalents until re-derived, deliverable (1), upfront capex by sector, is not met for buildings and industry. Say so in the executive summary, not only in the limits.

The checks that pass: enteric fermentation (40 dollars per head; 25 percent per head for cattle and buffalo, 35 percent for sheep and goats; maximum uptake 0.8), building interaction weights (0.9, 0.05, 0.05), decline rates (0.9, 0.887, 0.96), the power air-pollution factors (2.77, 0.99 and 1.43 dollars per gigajoule) and the transport externality factors. **M10. Consistency with paper 6.3 has five gaps that matter for double counting.** (a) @eq-ledger is introduced as "paper 6.3's ledger" but drops $-PIG_t$ (earmarked corrective revenue). **M11. Notation reuses at least twelve symbols, and the note is not yet simple.** The brief asks for a simple note that spells out every equation. These collisions make the equations hard to audit:

- $\kappa$: unit capital cost (eq. 5, 21), the uptake ramp width in dollars per tonne (eq. 18) and capital in dollars per kilowatt (eq. 30).
- $s$: PIM efficiency $s_c$, investment shares $s_{i,t}$, the running subsidy $s$, $s^{prod}$ and the shadow price $sh$.
- $\pi$: payer shares $\pi_{m,P}$, the reward per tonne $\pi_m$ and $\pi^{pub}$.
- $r$: rates, the reduction per unit treated $r_m$, $r^{rc}$ and $r_0$.
- $e$: tonnes per unit (eq. 14), and $e_i$ emission intensity (eq. 30); the code uses `e` for $\eta$.
- $\lambda$: the marginal cost of public funds and the penalty weights $\lambda_v$, $\lambda_a$.
- $g$: the decline factor $g_k$, the non-energy source index and the grid ratio.
- $u$: unit factor, uptake and $\bar u$.
- $P$: payer type, potential $P_{m,g,t}$, $P^C$ and $P$ in Proposition 2.
- $F$: fuel use and the budget flow, both in Section 3.
- $\eta$: end-use efficiency and construction saving.
- $G$: the viability gap and grid investment.
