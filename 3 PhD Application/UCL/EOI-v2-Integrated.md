## Proposed title

Sovereign Incentives for Decarbonisation: How a Climate Reserve and Guaranteed Contracts Can Lower the Cost of Capital and Reward Climate Policy

## Project summary (about 190 words)

Clean power in developing countries costs two to three times more to finance than in advanced economies, and the premium is set less by technology than by doubt that the payer will pay. Each part of a remedy has its own specialists: development banks manage risk, energy regulators write guaranteed contracts, macroeconomists track the savings of ageing countries and sovereign funds, trade analysts study EU carbon pricing and China's solar exports, and game theorists model climate clubs. Nobody joins them into one proposal that a finance ministry could act on. This project does, by making Michael Grubb's proposal that Norway's windfall underwrite developing-country investment concrete and testable. It asks whether a climate reserve funded by surplus sovereigns, lending its strength through guaranteed contracts whose capacity rises with credible policy and lapses if unused, can bring the cost of capital toward the sovereign's own at an expected cost funders will bear. The methods are a country-technology panel of financing costs, a project-finance and guarantee model, a coalition and adoption model calibrated with the Climate Policy Assessment Tool, and two or three country cases.

## Why this supervisor (about 120 words)

The project starts from Ameli et al. (2021, *Nature Communications*), which shows how higher financing costs trap developing economies in too little clean investment, and from Professor Ameli's ISR work on risk evaluation and underwriting instruments for renewable energy in developing countries. It asks what a coalition of funders and development banks can do about that trap, and how to price their support so that it rewards credible national policy rather than replacing it. Professor Grubb's work on energy policy, market design and induced innovation, and his proposal for Norway to underwrite investment in developing countries, would make him a natural subsidiary supervisor for the policy and coalition side.
